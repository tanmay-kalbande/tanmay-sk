1. Module 1: Introduction to Portuguese and the Alphabet

The Portuguese Alphabet: Your First Step to Speaking a New Language Imagine stepping off a plane in Lisbon or São Paulo, fresh off the plane, and hearing people around you speaking in rapid, melodic phrases. You recognize a few words—maybe "olá" or "obrigado"—but the rest is a blur. The alphabet is your first bridge to that world. It’s not just about memorizing letters; it’s about unlocking how sounds connect to meaning, how one culture shapes pronunciation differently from another, and how even a few spoken words can open doors. Portuguese uses the same 26 letters as English, but the sounds aren’t always the same. The letter R, for instance, isn’t rolled the same way in Lisbon as it is in Rio de Janeiro. The letter E can sound like the “e” in “bed” in one region and like the “e” in “they” in another. Mastering these differences early will save you from confusion—and embarrassment—later. Let’s start at the beginning. By the end of this chapter, you’ll not only know how to read and say each letter of the Portuguese alphabet, but you’ll also be able to greet someone in Portuguese, introduce yourself, and write your first simple sentences. You’ll be speaking—and reading—before you know it. --- The Portuguese Alphabet: Letters and Sounds Portuguese uses the Latin alphabet, just like English, but with 23 official letters (not 26). Wait—23? Yes. While English has A to Z, Portuguese traditionally uses only 23 letters in its alphabet. The letters K, W, and Y are considered foreign letters and are only used in words of foreign origin (like kilómetro, watt, or yoga). Here’s the full list of official Portuguese letters: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J, L, M, N, O, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V, X, Z Note that K, W, and Y are not part of the standard alphabet, though they appear in loanwords. --- Pronouncing Each Letter Let’s go through each letter one by one. For each, you’ll see: - The letter - Its name in Portuguese - How it sounds in English terms - An example word (in Portuguese transcription, not IPA, for simplicity) Vowel Sounds Vowels are the heart of pronunciation. You want to say, "I am good, thank you. Do you speak English?" But instead, you freeze. You know the words bom, obrigado, and falar, but how do you string them together so the sentence makes sense? This is where pronouns and verbs come in. They’re the glue that turns single words into real conversation. Without them, you’re pointing at objects and hoping for the best. With them, you start building sentences that actually communicate—even if they’re simple at first. This chapter will teach you the core pronouns you need to refer to yourself and others, and how to make verbs work with them. By the end, you’ll be able to say not just falar (to speak), but "Eu falo português" (I speak Portuguese). You’ll know how to ask "Você entende?" (Do you understand?) and answer "Sim, eu entendo" (Yes, I understand). Let’s begin with the building blocks: subject pronouns. --- Subject Pronouns: Who’s Doing the Action? A subject pronoun is a word that replaces a person’s name in a sentence. Instead of saying "Maria estuda" (Maria studies), you can say "Ela estuda" (She studies). Pronouns save time and make speech flow naturally. Here are the most common subject pronouns in Portuguese: | Pronoun | Meaning | Notes | |--------|--------|-------| | eu | I | Used for yourself. In Portugal, vós is used, but it’s rare in modern speech. | | eles | they (masculine or mixed group) | Can refer to people or things. | | elas | they (feminine) | Only for all-female groups. | 🔤 Jargon Alert: The words ele and ela are third-person singular pronouns—they refer to someone or something being talked about, not the speaker or listener. 5. Module 5: Asking and Answering Questions

Why Questions Are Your Secret Weapon in Portuguese Imagine you're in a small café in Lisbon. You order a bica (espresso) and the barista asks you a quick question: "Fala inglês?" If you recognize this as "Do you speak English?" and answer "Sim, um pouco" ("Yes, a little"), you’ve just turned a potential awkward moment into a friendly exchange. That single question opened a door. Questions aren’t just tools to get information—they’re social bridges. In Portuguese, how you ask and answer questions can mean the difference between a stilted conversation and a warm connection. And here’s the good news: you don’t need perfect grammar to start. You just need confidence, a few key patterns, and the willingness to stumble. This chapter will show you how to build that confidence. We’ll start with the simplest questions—yes/no types—and move toward open-ended ones using question words like quem (who), onde (where), and como (how). You’ll learn how rising intonation turns a statement into a question, and why qual is your best friend when asking about choices. By the end, you’ll be able to turn almost any sentence into a question, respond naturally, and keep the conversation flowing. Let’s begin with what you already know. --- Yes/No Questions: The Foundation of Any Conversation Yes/no questions are the most common type you’ll encounter. They’re formed by adding rising intonation to a statement—or, in writing, by placing the subject after the verb. But in spoken Portuguese, intonation is everything. How to Form Yes/No Questions Take a simple statement: - "Você fala inglês." → "You speak English." Add rising intonation (your voice goes up at the end), and it becomes a question: - "Você fala inglês?" → "Do you speak English?" That’s it. No extra words. No change in word order (unlike English, where we often invert subject and verb). Just a question mark in writing and a rising tone in speech. Try these: | Statement | Question (intonation) | |---------|---------------------| | Ele estuda português. | Ele estuda português? | | Nós comemos pão. | Nós comemos pão? | | Ela mora aqui. | Ela mora aqui? | Answering with Sim or Não The simplest answers require no verb conjugation: - "Você fala inglês?" - "Sim." - "Não." But to sound more natural, add a little extra: - "Sim, um pouco." → "Yes, a little." - "Não, não falo." → "No, I don’t speak." Notice the structure: - Sim/Não + (optional extra information) You can also add politeness: - "Sim, obrigado." → "Yes, thank you." - "Não, desculpe." → "No, sorry." 💡 Tip: In Portuguese, unlike English, you can’t just say "Yes" or "No" in a vacuum. Why Plans Matter in Portuguese Imagine this: You're in a bustling café in Lisbon, the scent of pastéis de nata filling the air. A friend texts you: "O que vais fazer hoje à noite?" This chapter will give you the tools to turn casual chats into concrete plans, using simple, natural phrases that work in real conversations. Plans aren’t just about schedules—they’re how you connect with others, share experiences, and say "Sim, claro!" ("Yes, of course!") when opportunity knocks. --- Phrases for Suggesting Activities Let’s start with the most direct way to invite someone to do something: using vamos ("let’s") combined with the preposition a (to) and a place or activity. Using 'Vamos' to Suggest Plans The word vamos comes from the verb ir (to go), but here it means "let’s go" or "let’s do." - Vamos + a + [place/activity] Examples: - Vamos ao cinema? — Let’s go to the cinema? - Vamos ao parque? — Let’s go to the park? - Vamos comer pizza? — Let’s eat pizza? Notice the a is used before places like cinema, parque, or pizza. This is similar to how we say "to the cinema" or "to the park" in English. 💡 Tip: When the place is feminine (like praia [beach], igreja [church]), use à: - Vamos à praia? — Let’s go to the beach? - Vamos à igreja? — Let’s go to church? But for masculine places (like cinema, restaurante), use ao: - Vamos ao restaurante? — Let’s go to the restaurant? --- Using 'Que tal...?' to Soften Invitations Sometimes you want to sound more casual or friendly. Que tal...? means "How about...?" or "What about...?" - Que tal [activity]? Examples: - Que tal assistir um filme? — How about watching a movie? - Que tal ir ao café? — How about going to the café? - Que tal jogar futebol? — How about playing soccer? 13. Module 13: Talking About the Past with Simple Past Tense

Why the Past Matters in Portuguese Imagine you're at a family dinner in São Paulo, and your cousin asks, "O que você fez no fim de semana passado?" (What did you do last weekend?). If you only know how to talk about the present—"Eu trabalho na segunda-feira" (I work on Mondays)—you’ll have to awkwardly point at your watch and say, "Ah, sim… agora…" (Ah, yes… now…). That’s a dead giveaway you’re not fluent yet. The simple past tense (passado simples) is your ticket to sharing stories, explaining what you did, and connecting with others on a deeper level. It’s the difference between saying "I eat bread" (Eu como pão) and "I ate bread this morning" (Eu comi pão esta manhã). Without it, conversations stay stuck in the present, like a photograph that never changes. In this module, you’ll learn to: - Turn everyday actions into past stories. - Talk about vacations, hobbies, and past routines. - Ask and answer questions about experiences. By the end, you’ll be able to say more than "Eu fui ao mercado" (I went to the market)—you’ll explain why, when, and how it felt. --- The Simple Past in Portuguese: The Basics What Is the Simple Past Tense? The simple past tense (passado simples) describes actions that started and finished in the past. Unlike the present (Eu como pão), which happens now, the simple past talks about what happened (Eu comi pão ontem). In English, simple past often adds -ed to verbs (walk → walked), but in Portuguese, verbs change endings based on who did the action. For example: - Present: Eu falo (I speak) - Past: Eu falei (I spoke) This change is called verb conjugation (conjugação verbal), which you’ve already practiced in Module 3 for the present tense. --- Regular -AR Verbs: The Foundation Most Portuguese verbs end in -ar, -er, or -ir.
