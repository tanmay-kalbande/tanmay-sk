1. Understanding Product Photography Fundamentals

The Three Faces of Product Photography: Commercial, E-Commerce, and Studio Product photography isn’t a monolith—it’s a spectrum of approaches tailored to different goals. A single product might appear in three distinct contexts: a glossy magazine ad, a clutter-free Amazon listing, or a controlled studio shoot for a brand campaign. Each has its own rules, aesthetics, and technical demands. Confusing these contexts is like showing up to a job interview in flip-flops—it’s not the right tool for the job. Consider a gold wristwatch. In a commercial shot, it might be styled on a mannequin’s wrist with dramatic lighting, shot at a 45-degree angle to emphasize the band’s sheen. On an e-commerce site, it’s likely a flat lay on a white backdrop, shot straight-on with soft, even illumination to show every detail without glare. In a studio session, it could be backlit to create a halo effect, or submerged in water to highlight its water resistance. The watch is the same, but the presentation changes entirely based on the medium and the buyer’s expectations. Understanding these distinctions early prevents wasted effort. A photographer who masters e-commerce lighting might struggle with commercial styling, and vice versa. This chapter breaks down the three primary types of product photography, their defining characteristics, and how to adapt your approach based on the final use case. --- Commercial Product Photography: Selling the Dream Commercial product photography is emotional storytelling. Its goal isn’t just to show the product—it’s to make the viewer want it. This style dominates print ads, billboards, and high-end brand campaigns. Think Apple’s minimalist hero shots, Nike’s action-packed lifestyle images, or Rolex’s aspirational wrist displays. Key Characteristics: - Contextual: Products are often shown in use, not isolation. A coffee mug might be steaming on a kitchen counter; a jacket draped over a chair in a misty forest. - Styling: Props, models, and environments add narrative. Even a "simple" product like a watch might be shot with a vintage map, leather-bound book, and soft candlelight to evoke adventure. - Lighting: Dramatic and directional. Hard shadows, rim lighting, and chiaroscuro (high-contrast) techniques create mood. Think of a single spotlight on a whiskey bottle to emphasize its amber glow. - Angles: Dynamic perspectives—low angles to make products feel larger-than-life, overhead shots to showcase craftsmanship, or Dutch tilts for edgy compositions. - Post-Processing: Heavy retouching to remove imperfections, enhance colors, and add effects like lens flares or film grain. The final image often feels curated, even surreal. 2. Essential Gear and Equipment Selection

Choosing Your Weapon: Cameras for Product Photography The first time you photograph a glossy ceramic cup, only to find the image washed out with harsh reflections, you’ll realize why gear selection isn’t just about megapixels—it’s about control. A camera with a weak dynamic range will struggle to retain detail in both the cup’s glaze and the dark shadows behind it. Conversely, a model with a wide dynamic range preserves subtle gradations in reflective surfaces, letting you clean up highlights in post without banding. Between DSLR and mirrorless systems, the choice isn’t ideological—it’s about specific performance needs and workflow. Let’s break down what actually matters when you’re evaluating cameras for product photography. DSLR vs. Mirrorless: Where Resolution and Dynamic Range Clash Resolution isn’t just about megapixels—it’s about usable detail. - DSLRs (Digital Single-Lens Reflex) have mature sensor technology with proven dynamic range, especially in mid-range models. The Canon EOS 90D, for example, delivers 32.5 MP with a broad dynamic range (around 12 stops in RAW), making it a strong contender for small to medium-sized products where fine texture matters. - Mirrorless cameras often lead in dynamic range and autofocus sophistication. The Sony A7 IV offers 33 MP with approximately 14 stops of dynamic range in RAW, giving you more latitude to recover blown highlights or crushed shadows when editing. - Crop sensors (APS-C) like the Fujifilm X-T5 (40.2 MP) can be surprisingly effective for product work, especially for jewelry or small accessories. Their smaller sensors mean deeper depth of field at equivalent apertures, which can simplify focus stacking for macro shots. Which wins for product photography? - If you need maximum dynamic range and low-light flexibility, mirrorless typically leads. - If you prioritize battery life and rugged build, DSLRs still hold an edge. - If you’re on a tight budget, a used full-frame DSLR like the Canon 6D Mark II (26.2 MP, ~12 stops) can deliver professional results without the mirrorless premium. Pro tip: Don’t get stuck on "full-frame vs. crop." Many product photographers swear by APS-C for its cost efficiency and sharpness—especially when paired with the right lens. --- Lenses: Focal Length and Aperture for Every Product Type A 35mm lens on a full-frame camera might seem ideal for lifestyle shots, but it distorts small products like rings or watches. 3. Lighting Techniques for Product Photography

Mastering the Language of Light: Shaping Shadows to Sell A matte black ceramic mug sits under a single bare bulb. Half of it vanishes into darkness; the rim catches a razor-thin highlight. The handle, in deep shadow, feels tactile without being distracting. Swap the bulb for a large softbox, and the mug becomes a flat, featureless silhouette. Rotate a silver ring on a white surface, and a single overhead light creates a harsh circle of reflection that obscures the gemstone. Move that same ring to a black velvet background with two softboxes at 45 degrees, and the metal sparkles with subtle gradients. These examples aren’t just lighting variations—they’re conversations between light and material. One setup tells a story of mystery; another screams “luxury.” The difference isn’t the product—it’s how you speak through light. This chapter teaches you to write that language fluently. --- The Inverse Square Law: Why Distance Dictates Drama Light doesn’t just illuminate—it decides. The Inverse Square Law states that the intensity of light is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source. In plain terms: move your light twice as far away, and it becomes four times weaker. Move it half as close, and it becomes four times stronger. This isn’t just theory—it’s the foundation of lighting ratios, the heartbeat of professional product photography. Why Ratios Matter Lighting ratios compare the key light (main light) to the fill light (secondary light) or shadow side of the subject. A 2:1 ratio means the key light is twice as bright as the fill. A 4:1 ratio creates deeper shadows—ideal for dramatic, high-end looks. Real-World Application Imagine photographing a glossy stainless-steel watch: - At 1m from the key light, the reflection is harsh and overexposed. - Move the key light to 2m—its intensity drops to 25%. - Now the highlight is softer, easier to control. - Use a reflector at 45 degrees to bounce fill back at 50%, balancing the ratio to 2:1. This isn’t guesswork—it’s calibration. The Inverse Square Law gives you predictable control over contrast and mood. 🔍 Troubleshooting Tip: If your shadows disappear when you move the light closer, you’ve likely flipped the ratio. Distance changes intensity faster than you think—always meter or test your exposure after moving lights. --- Three-Point Lighting: The Blueprint for Any Product Three-point lighting is a modular lighting system—adaptable, repeatable, and scalable. The simple sneaker isn’t just in the center of a frame—it’s angled toward an open window, where light spills in like a spotlight. What’s the difference between a product photo and a compelling visual story? It’s not just the product—it’s how you place, position, and style it. 5. Camera Settings for Professional Results

Mastering the Exposure Triangle in Manual Mode The first time you hand a client a product shot with blown-out highlights or deep shadows that hide details, you realize that auto mode isn’t just lazy—it’s unreliable. Manual mode isn’t about guessing; it’s about intentional control over the three pillars of exposure: ISO, shutter speed, and aperture. When these work in concert, you produce images that are consistently professional, not just technically correct. Think of the exposure triangle as a balancing act. Adjust one side, and the others shift in response. Here’s how to make that work for product photography. Balancing ISO, Shutter Speed, and Aperture ISO: Minimize Noise in the Signal ISO determines your camera’s sensitivity to light. Lower ISO (100–400) means cleaner images; higher ISO (above 800) introduces noise that muddies fine textures—especially on metallic, fabric, or matte surfaces. When to increase ISO: - In controlled studio environments with studio strobes or continuous lights, keep ISO at 100–200. - In mixed lighting or dim environments, a slight increase to 400 may be necessary—but only if you can’t brighten the scene with lighting. - Never rely on high ISO as a crutch. If your image is too dark at ISO 200, adjust lighting first. Scenario: You’re photographing a glossy ceramic mug in a home studio with a single softbox. The meter reads -1.5 stops underexposed at ISO 100, f/8, 1/125s. Instead of bumping ISO to 800 (which would introduce visible grain), add a second light or reflector to lift the shadows by one stop. Your exposure stays clean, and the ceramic’s subtle glaze remains sharp. Rule of thumb: Keep ISO as low as possible without compromising shutter speed or aperture. Shutter Speed: Freeze Motion, Avoid Blur Shutter speed controls how long the sensor is exposed to light. In product photography, you’re usually working with static subjects—so 1/125s is often sufficient. But when lighting is dim or you’re shooting handheld, even slight camera shake can blur fine details. Key considerations: - 1/125s or faster for handheld shots with a standard lens (e.g., 50mm). - 1/250s or faster for macro lenses or close-ups to prevent micro-blur. - Tripod use allows slower speeds (1/60s or below), especially in low light. Pitfall: Many photographers forget that mirrorless cameras have no mirror slap, so even 1/60s can work handheld—but test your camera’s limits. Use image stabilization only as a safety net, not a license to shoot carelessly. Pro tip: Enable “mirror-up” or “electronic front curtain shutter” on DSLRs to eliminate internal vibrations at slow speeds. Aperture: Control Depth of Field and Sharpness Aperture (f-stop) affects depth of field (DOF) and lens sharpness. 6. Advanced Post-Processing Workflow

The "Perfect" Shot That Isn't Quite There Imagine you’ve spent three hours meticulously implementing the Lighting Techniques and Composition and Styling covered in previous chapters. You’ve nailed the angle, the product is positioned perfectly, and your Camera Settings were spot on. You open the RAW file, and while the image is strong, it’s not "commercial" yet. There is a distracting speck of dust on the matte black surface, a slight yellow cast from a stray bounce of light, and the texture of the product—the very thing that sells the quality—feels flat. This is where the professional gap is bridged. High-end product photography is rarely about "fixing" a bad photo; it is about the surgical refinement of a great one. The goal is an image that looks effortless and natural, where the viewer never suspects that a single pixel was altered. Establishing a Non-Destructive Workflow The cardinal rule of professional post-processing is Non-Destructive Editing. If a client asks you to change a color or restore a detail three weeks after the shoot, you cannot afford to have "baked in" your changes. The Lightroom-to-Photoshop Pipeline For maximum efficiency, use a hybrid workflow. Lightroom (or Adobe Camera Raw) is for global adjustments, while Photoshop is for surgical, local refinements. 1. Global Grading (Lightroom): Handle exposure, white balance, and lens corrections. 2. Surgical Cleaning (Photoshop): Handle blemish removal, complex masking, and fine-detail enhancement. 3. Final Polish (Lightroom): Bring the PSD back into Lightroom for final sharpening and batch synchronization. The Power of Layers and Masks Once inside Photoshop, never edit the Background layer. Immediately duplicate your layer (Cmd/Ctrl + J) or, better yet, use Adjustment Layers. Adjustment Layers: These allow you to change levels, curves, and hue/saturation without altering the original pixels. Layer Masks: Instead of using the Eraser tool (which is destructive), use masks. Black hides, white reveals. This allows you to "paint" your edits back in if you realize you've over-processed an area. Smart Objects: Convert your product layer into a Smart Object. This allows you to apply filters (like Gaussian Blur or High Pass sharpening) that can be toggled off or adjusted in intensity at any time. Correcting the RAW Foundation Before you can enhance, you must neutralize. Using the RAW files you captured (as emphasized in Camera Settings for Professional Results), focus on these three pillars of accuracy. 7. Reflective and Transparent Product Photography

The Challenge of the "Invisible" Product Imagine you are commissioned to shoot a high-end skincare line featuring heavy glass jars and a sleek, polished chrome cap. You set up your studio lighting as you did for matte products, only to find that your preview screen is filled with distracting white hotspots, the reflection of your own camera lens in the cap, and a muddy, grey interior to the glass. When dealing with reflective and transparent materials, you are no longer just photographing an object; you are photographing the environment around the object. Glass, chrome, and liquids act as mirrors or windows. To master these surfaces, you must stop thinking about how to light the product and start thinking about what the product is reflecting. Taming the Glare: Polarizers and Diffusion In previous chapters, we discussed general Lighting Techniques, but reflective surfaces require a more surgical approach to light modification. The goal is rarely to eliminate reflections entirely—which often makes a product look flat or "fake"—but to control their shape, position, and intensity. The Power of the Circular Polarizer (CPL) A Circular Polarizer is an essential tool for any intermediate photographer dealing with non-metallic reflections. By rotating the filter, you can selectively block light waves that are vibrating in specific directions, effectively "cutting through" glare on glass or plastic. Glass and Plastic: A CPL can remove the surface reflection of a perfume bottle, allowing the viewer to see the color and clarity of the liquid inside. The Metallic Limitation: It is critical to remember that polarizers do not work on pure metals. Because metal reflects light specularly rather than refracting it, a CPL will not remove a reflection from a chrome surface. For metals, you must rely entirely on physical modifiers. Strategic Diffusion When a light source is small and powerful, it creates "specular highlights" (tiny, blindingly bright spots). To achieve a professional, commercial look, you need to increase the size of your light source relative to the product. Scrims and Silk: Placing a large translucent fabric (a scrim) between your light and the product turns a harsh point of light into a soft, broad glow. The "White Card" Technique: Instead of pointing your light directly at a reflective product, point it at a large piece of white foam core. The product will reflect the clean, white surface of the card rather than the chaotic geometry of the light fixture. Creating the Controlled Environment: Tents and Curves For products that are highly reflective from all angles—such as jewelry or luxury watches—random studio reflections are the enemy. 8. Large and Small Product Photography

The Scale Paradox: Why Size Changes Everything Imagine you are tasked with photographing a diamond earring and a mid-century modern sofa for the same client. In both cases, the goal is the same: a professional, commercial image that conveys quality. However, the technical approach for these two items is diametrically opposed. With the earring, your greatest enemy is the minimum focus distance of your lens and the microscopic presence of dust. With the sofa, your enemy is keystoning—the frustrating perspective distortion where parallel lines seem to converge, making a rectangular piece of furniture look like it’s leaning backward. Scaling your photography isn't just about moving the tripod closer or further away; it is about managing the physics of light, the geometry of perspective, and the behavior of depth of field. Mastering the Micro: Small Product Photography When photographing jewelry, electronics, or cosmetics, you are entering the world of macro photography. At this scale, the margin for error disappears. A single fingerprint or a speck of dust that is invisible to the naked eye becomes a glaring distraction in a high-resolution image. Macro Setup and Close-Up Techniques To achieve the necessary magnification, you have three primary options based on the gear discussed in Essential Gear and Equipment Selection: 1. Dedicated Macro Lenses: These offer a 1:1 reproduction ratio, meaning the image on the sensor is the same size as the object in real life. 2. Extension Tubes: Hollow spacers placed between the lens and the camera body to shorten the minimum focus distance. 3. Close-up Filters: Diopters that screw onto the front of a lens to act as a magnifying glass. The Challenge of the "Working Distance": The closer you get to a small product, the more you block your own light. To avoid casting a shadow of the camera over the product, use a longer focal length (e.g., 90mm or 105mm macro) to maintain a comfortable distance while still achieving high magnification. Managing Depth of Field at Scale As you move closer to a subject, the depth of field (DoF) shrinks drastically. Even at f/11, you may find that the front of a ring is in focus, but the gemstone is blurred. To solve this, implement Focus Stacking. Instead of stopping down to f/22—which introduces diffraction and softens the image—take a series of photos (a "stack") where you shift the focus point slightly from the front of the product to the back. These are then merged during Advanced Post-Processing Workflow to create an image that is sharp from foreground to background. 9. Flat Lay and White Background Techniques

The Psychology of the "Clean" Shot Imagine a customer browsing a high-end e-commerce site. They are toggling between three different brands selling a similar leather wallet. One brand uses moody, contextual shots; another uses inconsistent home-office backgrounds. The third brand uses a clinical, seamless white background with a perfectly centered product. Statistically, the third brand often wins the conversion. Why? Because a pure white background removes all cognitive load. It signals professionalism, transparency, and a focus on the product's physical attributes rather than a "vibe." While you have already mastered Lighting Techniques and Camera Settings, applying them to the specific constraints of flat lays and "high-key" white backgrounds requires a shift in strategy. You are no longer chasing mood; you are chasing precision. Mastering the Flat Lay Composition A flat lay—an image taken from a 90-degree angle looking straight down—is the gold standard for "kit" shots, apparel, and beauty products. However, the biggest challenge is avoiding the "stiff" look while maintaining the clarity and consistency required for commercial work. Designing for Visual Appeal To move beyond a basic overhead shot, apply these design principles to your layout: The Rule of Odds: Grouping items in threes or fives creates a more natural, less engineered feel than even pairings. The "S" Curve: Arrange items in a subtle meandering path across the frame. This guides the viewer's eye through the entire product story rather than pinning them to a single center point. Layering and Depth: Even in a 2D plane, you can create perceived depth. Place a primary product on top of a secondary element (e.g., a skincare bottle resting on a linen cloth) to add tactile dimension. Negative Space: For e-commerce, leave generous "breathing room" around the edges. This ensures that when images are cropped for different screen sizes, the product isn't clipped. Ensuring Technical Consistency When shooting a series of flat lays for a brand, inconsistency is the primary pitfall. If the angle shifts by even two degrees between shots, the catalog will feel disjointed. 1. Use a C-Stand or Tripod Overhead Arm: Never hand-hold flat lays. Use a boom arm to ensure the sensor is perfectly parallel to the surface. 2. The Level Tool: Use a bubble level on your camera's hot shoe to verify a true 90-degree angle. 3. Fixed Focal Length: Stick to a "normal" focal length (approx. 50mm to 85mm) to avoid the barrel distortion common with wide-angle lenses, which can warp the edges of your products. 11. Color Accuracy and Material-Specific Techniques

The High Cost of a "Near-Miss" Color Imagine a luxury skincare brand launching a "Midnight Plum" cream. The product is a deep, sophisticated violet. After a high-end shoot, the images look stunning—moody, sharp, and professional. However, once the campaign goes live, the company is flooded with returns. The reason? In the photos, the cream looks like a bright magenta. The customer felt misled; the brand felt the blow to its reputation. In professional product photography, "close enough" is a failure. When a customer buys a product online, the photograph is the only proxy for the physical object. If the color is off by even a few percentage points in saturation or hue, you aren't just delivering a bad photo—you are creating a customer service liability. Achieving absolute color accuracy requires moving beyond the "Auto White Balance" setting and treating color as a measurable science rather than an artistic intuition. Mastering Color Calibration and Reproduction To ensure that the color on your screen matches the product in your hand and the print in the customer's mail, you must establish a Color Pipeline. This is the process of ensuring consistency from the light source to the sensor, through the monitor, and finally to the end-user's device. Using Color Checkers and Calibration Tools Relying on your eyes is risky because of chromatic adaptation—your brain naturally adjusts to the lighting in the room, making a warm light source look "neutral" after a few minutes. To bypass this biological bias, use a physical reference. ColorChecker Charts: These are standardized targets containing a grid of scientifically formulated color patches. By placing a chart in the first frame of your sequence, you create a "truth map" for that specific lighting environment. Gray Cards (18% Gray): Essential for establishing a neutral midpoint. A gray card allows you to set a precise white balance in Post-Processing, ensuring that neutrals are neither too blue (cool) nor too orange (warm). Custom White Balance: Instead of using presets (like "Daylight" or "Tungsten"), use your camera’s custom WB setting to sample a gray card. This eliminates the "guesswork" before the image even hits the sensor. Matching Brand Guidelines and Digital Assets Many clients provide a Brand Style Guide that includes specific HEX, RGB, or Pantone codes. However, a HEX code is a digital value, while a product is a physical object. Bridging this gap requires a targeted workflow: 1. The Reference Sample: Always request a physical sample of the product and the brand's official color swatch. 2. Controlled Lighting: Use high-CRI (Color Rendering Index) lighting. 12. Creating Product Photography Sets for Brands

The Chaos of the "Quick Shoot" Imagine you’ve been hired to shoot a new skincare line consisting of 12 different products. You arrive at the studio, set up a beautiful lighting rig based on the techniques from Lighting Techniques for Product Photography, and nail the first shot. However, halfway through the day, you realize the client also needs a "lifestyle" version of every product, three different angles for the e-commerce site, and a specific hero shot for a billboard. Suddenly, you are swapping backgrounds, resetting lights, and hunting for props every ten minutes. By the time you reach the twelfth product, your lighting has shifted slightly, your angles are inconsistent, and you've run out of time. This is the "Quick Shoot" trap—where a lack of systemic planning turns a professional project into a frantic scramble. Creating a set for a brand is not about taking a single great photo; it is about building a repeatable system that ensures the 1st image and the 100th image look like they belong to the same family. Strategic Planning: Shot Lists and Storyboarding Before a single light is plugged in, you must translate the brand’s vision into a technical roadmap. For a professional brand set, "winging it" is a liability. Developing the Shot List A shot list is a granular inventory of every single image required for the project. It strips away the emotion of the creative brief and replaces it with a checklist. A professional shot list should include: Product SKU/Name: Exactly which item is being photographed. Shot Type: (e.g., Hero, Detail, Group Shot, Lifestyle, White Background). Angle: Referencing the Multiple Angles concepts from earlier chapters (e.g., 45-degree, Eye-level, Top-down). Required Props: Specific items needed for that specific shot. Aspect Ratio/Orientation: Whether the shot is for a vertical Instagram Story or a horizontal website banner. 13. Optimizing Images for Digital Platforms

The Cost of a Heavy Image Imagine a potential customer lands on your Shopify store after clicking a high-performing Instagram ad. They are excited to see the product you’ve meticulously styled using the techniques from Composition and Styling for Maximum Impact. However, because your homepage features five uncompressed, 10MB RAW-converted JPEGs, the page takes six seconds to load. Statistics consistently show that conversion rates drop significantly for every second of delay. By the time your stunning, high-resolution imagery finally renders, the customer has already bounced back to the search results. The paradox of digital product photography is that while you want the highest possible quality to convey luxury and detail, the internet demands the smallest possible file size to ensure speed. Optimization is the art of finding the "perceptual equilibrium"—the point where the image looks professional to the human eye, but the file size is lean enough for a mobile browser on a 4G connection. Mastering Color Spaces for the Screen Before you resize or compress a single pixel, you must ensure your image is speaking the right "language" for the device it will be viewed on. If you skip this step, the vibrant colors you achieved during Color Accuracy and Material-Specific Techniques will appear dull, shifted, or neon on your customer's screen. sRGB: The Universal Standard For almost every digital platform—Amazon, Shopify, Instagram, and personal portfolios—sRGB (Standard Red Green Blue) is the non-negotiable standard. It is the smallest color gamut, but it is the only one consistently supported by every web browser and mobile device. Adobe RGB and ProPhoto RGB You likely used Adobe RGB or ProPhoto RGB during your Advanced Post-Processing Workflow to preserve a wider range of colors for editing. While these are superior for printing, they are not designed for the web. If you upload an Adobe RGB file to a platform that expects sRGB, the browser will often fail to map the colors correctly, resulting in "muddy" greens and washed-out reds. The Conversion Workflow: 1. Complete all edits in your wide gamut (Adobe RGB). 2. Use the "Convert to Profile" command (not "Assign Profile") to switch the image to sRGB. 3. Export the final version for web use. Precision Resizing and Compression Many photographers make the mistake of uploading a 6000px wide image and letting the website's software "shrink" it. This is inefficient; the browser still has to download the massive file before resizing it on the fly, killing your page load speed. Strategic Resizing Resize your images to the exact maximum dimensions required by the platform. 14. Portfolio Development and Client Presentation

The "Invisible" Filter: Why Great Photos Aren't Enough Imagine two photographers applying for the same high-ticket commercial contract for a luxury skincare brand. Photographer A sends a link to an Instagram feed with 500 stunning images. The photos are technically perfect—impeccable Lighting, flawless Post-Processing, and sharp Color Accuracy. However, the images are scattered; a jewelry shot is followed by a burger, then a bottle of perfume, then a car. Photographer B sends a curated PDF and a link to a clean website. They present three distinct "Collections": Luxury Cosmetics, Minimalist Tech, and Artisanal Beverages. Each collection includes a brief narrative explaining the client's goal, the challenge (e.g., managing Reflective and Transparent surfaces), and the final result. Even if Photographer A is technically more skilled, Photographer B wins the contract. Why? Because clients don't just buy photos; they buy problem-solving capabilities. A portfolio is not a gallery of your favorite shots; it is a strategic tool designed to prove you can solve a specific client's problem. Curating for Impact and Diversity The most common mistake intermediate photographers make is including too much work. A portfolio is judged by its weakest image. If you have ten world-class shots and two "okay" shots, the client will remember the two "okay" shots. The Curation Framework To build a portfolio that attracts commercial work, categorize your images by "Style" and "Product Type" rather than chronologically. 1. The E-commerce Pillar: Showcase your ability to create clean, high-volume, high-consistency imagery. This is where you demonstrate mastery of Flat Lay and White Background Techniques. Focus on clarity, precise clipping paths, and absolute Color Accuracy. 2. The Lifestyle Pillar: Demonstrate Emotional Storytelling and Contextual Photography. Show the product in use. This proves you can handle art direction and styling, moving beyond the studio to create a mood. 3. The Technical Pillar: This is your "flex" section. Include your most difficult wins—complex Reflective and Transparent glass, macro shots of intricate jewelry, or large-scale products that required specialized Essential Gear. Diversity vs. Focus While diversity shows versatility, too much variety can make you look like a generalist. If you want to be hired for cosmetics, 60% of your portfolio should be cosmetics.
