1. Test Structure & Calculator Mastery

The Lay of the Land: GED Math Test Structure Imagine sitting down at the testing center, logging into your computer, and staring at the first math question. You know the math, but you’re spending precious seconds trying to figure out how to flag the question for review or where to find the calculator. The test interface itself can become an invisible barrier to your success. Passing the GED Math test requires more than just knowing how to solve equations; it requires mastering the environment in which you will solve them. The GED Mathematical Reasoning test is designed to assess your foundational mathematical competence. You will face exactly 46 questions and have a strict time limit of 115 minutes to complete the entire exam. That gives you an average of roughly two and a half minutes per question, though some will take far less time and others will take more. The Four Content Areas The test is not a random assortment of math problems. It is strictly divided into four main content areas. Understanding how the test weights these areas allows you to prioritize your study time effectively: 1. Algebraic Problem Solving (approximately 55%): This is the heavyweight of the GED math test. You will be solving linear equations, working with polynomials, evaluating functions, and graphing inequalities. Because this makes up more than half of your score, algebra mastery is non-negotiable. 2. Quantitative Problem Solving (approximately 45%): This broad category covers number sense, ratios, proportions, percentages, data analysis, statistics, probability, and geometry. 3. Geometry and Measurement (integrated into Quantitative): While not always listed as a standalone percentage, expect a heavy dose of two-dimensional and three-dimensional figure calculations. 4. Data Analysis and Probability (integrated into Quantitative): You will read charts, graphs, and tables, and calculate basic probabilities based on given data sets. (Note: The exact percentages can fluctuate slightly between test forms, but expect roughly a 55/45 split between Algebra and Quantitative problem solving.) Question Formats: More Than Multiple Choice Gone are the days of simple A, B, C, D bubble sheets. The GED is entirely computerized and utilizes several question formats to test your true understanding: Multiple Choice: The classic format. You select one correct answer from four options. Fill-in-the-Blank: You must calculate the exact answer and type it into a provided box. No guessing here. Drop-Down: You click a menu to select the correct answer or step in a process from a list of options. 2. Number Sense and Operations

The Number Line: Opposites and Absolute Value Imagine checking your bank account after a weekend of expenses and seeing a balance of -$45.50. You are in the hole, but by how much? You owe $45.50, regardless of the negative sign. This is the core idea behind absolute value. On the GED Math test, you will frequently encounter rational numbers, which include all integers, fractions, and terminating or repeating decimals. Every rational number has an opposite (a number the same distance from zero but on the other side of the number line) and an absolute value (the distance of that number from zero). The opposite of a number changes its sign. The opposite of $5$ is $-5$. The opposite of $-\frac{3}{4}$ is $\frac{3}{4}$. The absolute value is always positive (or zero), because distance is never negative. We write it with vertical bars: $|-5| = 5$ and $|5| = 5$. Applying Opposites and Absolute Value On the test, these concepts rarely appear in isolation; they are usually baked into word problems. Scenario: A submarine is cruising at an elevation of $-150$ feet. It descends another $65$ feet, then ascends $40$ feet. What is the submarine's final elevation, and what was the total vertical distance it traveled? 1. Final Elevation: Start at $-150$, add $-65$ (descending), then add $+40$ (ascending). $-150 + (-65) + 40 = -175$ feet. 2. Total Distance Traveled: This requires absolute value because distance is a positive quantity regardless of direction. $|-65| + |40| = 65 + 40 = 105$ feet. When a test question asks for "distance traveled" or "total change," use absolute value. When it asks for "final position" or "net change," keep the positive and negative signs. Converting Between Fractions, Decimals, and Percentages In Chapter 1, we covered how to use the TI-30XS MultiView calculator. While you can use it to convert forms, the GED test often requires you to understand the mechanics of these conversions to answer fill-in-the-blank or drag-and-drop questions correctly. Here is a brisk review of the core conversions: Fraction to Decimal: Divide the numerator by the denominator. $\frac{3}{8} = 3 \div 8 = 0.375$. Decimal to Percentage: Multiply by $100$ (or move the decimal point two places to the right). $0.375 \times 100 = 37.5\%$. Percentage to Decimal: Divide by $100$ (or move the decimal point two places to the left). $45\% = 0.45$. Decimal to Fraction: Place the decimal over its place value and simplify. $0.45 = \frac{45}{100} = \frac{9}{20}$. Fraction, Decimal, and Percent Equivalents to Memorize While you have the TI-30XS, relying on it for every simple conversion eats up precious seconds on a test with a strict 115-minute time limit. Memorize these common equivalents: $\frac{1}{2} = 0.5 = 50\%$ …

3. Ratios, Proportions, and Percentages

The Blueprint of Everyday Math: Ratios and Rates You are standing in the hardware store staring at two buckets of paint. A 1-gallon bucket costs $24.50, while a 5-gallon bucket costs $98.00. Which is the better deal? Without realizing it, your brain is already trying to calculate a ratio—a comparison of two quantities. On the GED Math test, ratios, proportions, and percentages aren't just abstract concepts; they are the primary tools used to test your ability to navigate real-world financial and spatial scenarios. Since Quantitative Problem Solving makes up approximately 45% of the 46 questions on the test, mastering these concepts is non-negotiable. Reducing and Using Ratios A ratio compares two quantities, often written as a fraction (e.g., $3/4$), with a colon ($3:4$), or in words ("3 to 4"). In GED word problems, ratios often represent things like ingredients in a recipe, gear rotations, or demographic splits. If a problem states that a trail mix has a peanut-to-raisin ratio of $12:16$, your first step should always be to simplify it just as you would a fraction. Both numbers are divisible by 4, giving a simplified ratio of $3:4$. Calculating Unit Rates A unit rate tells you how much of one thing corresponds to exactly one unit of another thing. Miles per hour (mph) and price per pound are classic examples. To find a unit rate, divide the numerator by the denominator. Scenario: You drive 315 miles in 4.5 hours. What is your unit rate in miles per hour? Set it up as a fraction: $$315 \text{ miles} / 4.5 \text{ hours}$$ Divide 315 by 4.5 on your TI-30XS MultiView to get 70. Your unit rate is 70 mph. When comparing two options (like the paint buckets), finding the unit rate for both options allows you to make an apples-to-apples comparison. 1-gallon bucket: $\$24.50 / 1 = \$24.50$ per gallon. 5-gallon bucket: $\$98.00 / 5 = \$19.60$ per gallon. The 5-gallon bucket is the better deal. Scale Factors A scale factor is a ratio that describes how much a figure has been enlarged or reduced. This is highly relevant for the Geometry and Measurement concepts integrated into the GED. If a blueprint has a scale of $1 \text{ inch} = 5 \text{ feet}$, the scale factor is $1:60$ (since 5 feet equals 60 inches). If a room on the blueprint is 4 inches long, the actual room is $4 \times 5 = 20$ feet long. Be careful when working with area and scale factors. 4. Data Analysis and Statistics

Measures of Center: Finding the "Average" Imagine you are looking at a new apartment, and the landlord tells you that the "average" utility bill for the five tenants in the building is $90 a month. That sounds perfectly reasonable. But what if the actual bills are $40, $45, $50, $55, and $260? That $260 outlier (perhaps a tenant who runs a server farm in their living room) heavily skewed the "average" up to $90. On the GED Math test, data sets are rarely straightforward. The test measures your ability to calculate four key statistical measures, but more importantly, it tests your ability to interpret what those numbers actually tell you about a data set. Mean, Median, Mode, and Range You likely remember the basic concepts, but let's move briskly through the calculations and focus on how the GED will test them. 1. Mean (The Arithmetic Average) To find the mean, add all the values and divide by the number of values. On the TI-30XS MultiView calculator, you can type the entire numerator, press the ÷ key, type the denominator, and press ENTER. The calculator follows the Order of Operations automatically, so you don't necessarily need parentheses unless you are dividing multiple separate fractions. 2. Median (The Middle Value) To find the median, arrange the data from least to greatest and find the exact middle. - If there is an odd number of data points, the median is the single middle number. - If there is an even number of data points, the median is the mean of the two middle numbers (add them together and divide by 2). 3. Mode (The Most Frequent) The mode is the number that appears most often. A data set can have one mode, more than one mode (bimodal), or no mode at all if every number appears only once. 4. Range (The Spread) The range measures how spread out the data is. Subtract the minimum value from the maximum value: Range = Maximum - Minimum. Applying the Measures The GED rarely asks you to just "find the mean." Instead, it asks you to compare these measures or determine which one best represents a data set. GED Scenario: A small company has five employees. Their annual salaries are $32,000, $35,000, $38,000, $42,000, and $150,000 (the owner). - Mean: ($32,000 + $35,000 + $38,000 + $42,000 + $150,000) ÷ 5 = $59,400 - Median: $38,000 - Mode: None - Range: $150,000 - $32,000 = $118,000 If a GED question asks, "Which measure of center best represents the typical employee's salary at this company?" the answer is the median. 5. Probability and Counting

The Logic of What Might Happen Imagine you are at a diner staring at a dessert menu. There are 5 flavors of ice cream and 3 types of toppings. If you can pick one flavor and one topping, how many different dessert combinations can you make? If you mentally pair them up—vanilla with sprinkles, vanilla with fudge, vanilla with whipped cream, chocolate with sprinkles—you’ll eventually find there are 15 possible combinations. You just used the Fundamental Counting Principle. Probability and counting are simply structured ways of figuring out how likely a specific outcome is out of all possible outcomes. On the GED math test, probability questions fall under Quantitative Problem Solving. While they make up a smaller portion of the 46 questions, they are often some the easiest points to secure—provided you know the rules. Because you already mastered fractions, decimals, and percentages in earlier chapters, you have the computational skills needed. Now, we just apply them to real-world scenarios. Simple Probability: The Foundation Probability is the measure of how likely an event is to occur. Every probability problem hinges on one fundamental formula: Probability of an event = (Number of favorable outcomes) / (Total number of possible outcomes) If you have a standard 6-sided die and want to roll a 4, there is exactly 1 favorable outcome (rolling a 4) out of 6 possible outcomes (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6). The probability is 1/6. Key Probability Rules to Memorize Probabilities always fall between 0 and 1. A probability of 0 means an event is impossible. A probability of 1 (or 100%) means an event is certain. The sum of all probabilities is 1. If there is a 30% chance of rain, there must be a 70% chance of no rain. "Or" vs. "And": If a question asks for the probability of Event A or Event B, you add their probabilities together. If it asks for Event A and Event B, you multiply them. GED Scenario: Simple Probability A bag contains 5 red marbles, 3 blue marbles, and 2 green marbles. What is the probability of randomly drawing a blue marble? 1. Find the favorable outcomes: 3 (the blue marbles) 2. Find the total possible outcomes: 5 + 3 + 2 = 10 (all the marbles) 3. Calculate: 3/10 The probability is 3/10. If the GED asks for this as a percentage, you can use your TI-30XS MultiView to convert the fraction to a decimal (0.3) and move the decimal two places to the right (30%). 6. Algebraic Expressions and Polynomials

The Language of Algebra: Variables and Expressions Imagine you are planning to tile a floor. You know each box of tile covers 12 square feet and costs $35. But you haven't measured the room yet. If you let the letter x represent the number of boxes you need to buy, the total cost of your project is 35x. You just wrote an algebraic expression. Algebra acts as a bridge between the concrete numbers we use every day and the unknown values we need to figure out. On the GED math test, Algebraic Problem Solving makes up approximately 55% of the exam. A large portion of that relies on your ability to manipulate, simplify, and evaluate these expressions before you ever set up a full equation. An algebraic expression is a mathematical phrase that contains numbers, variables (letters representing unknown quantities), and operation symbols (like $+$, $-$, $\times$, and $\div$). Because expressions do not contain an equals sign ($=$), they don't make a statement; they simply represent a value. Evaluating Expressions by Substitution The most fundamental skill in algebra is evaluating an expression. This means replacing the variable with a known number to find the total value. To do this correctly, you must rely heavily on the Order of Operations covered in Module 2: Number Sense and Operations. If you forget the order, even a perfectly substituted expression will yield the wrong answer. Example Scenario: You are calculating the height of a projectile thrown into the air. The expression $-16t^2 + vt + h$ represents its height after $t$ seconds, where $v$ is the starting velocity and $h$ is the starting height. Let's evaluate the expression $-5t^2 + 20t + 4$ when $t = 2$. 1. Substitute the value into the expression: $-5(2)^2 + 20(2) + 4$ 2. Evaluate exponents first: $2^2 = 4$, so the expression becomes $-5(4) + 20(2) + 4$ 3. Perform multiplication next: $-20 + 40 + 4$ 4. Add and subtract from left to right: $20 + 4 = 24$ The evaluated result is 24. Calculator Tip: When plugging variables into your TI-30XS MultiView, use the $[x]$ button or the $[(-)]$ button for negative signs. If you type $5 \times -2^2$, the calculator might interpret this as $-(5 \times 2^2)$ depending on how it's keyed. Always use parentheses around substituted values to be safe: $-5 \times (2)^2$. 7. Linear Equations and Inequalities

Solving Multi-Step Linear Equations You are comparing two cell phone plans. Plan A charges a flat monthly fee of $30 plus $0.10 per text message. Plan B charges a flat fee of $15 plus $0.25 per text. How many texts would you have to send for both plans to cost the exact same amount? To answer this, you need to set two expressions equal to each other and solve for the unknown. This is the essence of algebra, which makes up roughly 55% of your GED math test. Building directly on the simplification skills from Algebraic Expressions and Polynomials, we will now solve for the actual value of variables. The golden rule of solving equations is simple: whatever you do to one side of the equation, you must do to the exact same side. Your goal is always to isolate the variable on one side of the equals sign. The Standard Path to Isolation Follow this reliable sequence to solve multi-step linear equations: 1. Clear parentheses by using the distributive property. 2. Clear fractions by multiplying both sides by the least common denominator. 3. Combine like terms on each side. 4. Move variables to one side using addition or subtraction. 5. Move constants to the opposite side using addition or subtraction. 6. Divide by the coefficient to isolate the variable. Example: Solve $3(x - 4) + 2x = 2(x + 5) - 1$ Distribute: $3x - 12 + 2x = 2x + 10 - 1$ Combine like terms: $5x - 12 = 2x + 9$ Move variables (subtract $2x$): $3x - 12 = 9$ Move constants (add $12$): $3x = 21$ Divide: $x = 7$ Handling Special Cases Sometimes, the variable cancels out entirely. Do not panic; this is a special case. No Solution: If you simplify an equation and get a false statement (like $4 = 9$), the equation has no solution. It means the lines never intersect. Infinite Solutions: If you get a true statement with no variables (like $5 = 5$), it means any value for $x$ will work. Solving and Graphing Linear Inequalities Linear inequalities operate exactly like linear equations, with one critical difference: if you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, you must flip the inequality sign. $<$ (less than) and $$ (greater than) are represented by open circles on a number line (or dashed lines on a coordinate plane) because the endpoint is not included. $\le$ (less than or equal to) and $\ge$ (greater than or equal to) are represented by closed circles (or solid lines) because the endpoint is included. 8. Functions and Quadratic Equations

Relations, Functions, and the Vertical Line Test Imagine you are operating a ride at an amusement park. The safety rules are strict: every rider must be exactly 48 inches or taller to ride. However, there is no rule against someone being 60 inches tall and riding twice. In mathematics, this scenario represents a function—a relationship where every input (a person) is assigned to exactly one output (whether they can ride). Even if multiple inputs yield the same output, or an input repeats, the relationship remains a function. The only thing that breaks the rule is if a single input produces two different outputs simultaneously (e.g., a 50-inch person being told both "yes" and "no" at the exact same time). A relation is simply any set of ordered pairs. A function is a specific type of relation where each input (the $x$-value) maps to exactly one output (the $y$-value). On the GED math test, you will frequently be asked to look at a table or a graph and determine whether a relation is a function. Identifying Functions from Tables When evaluating a table of values, look only at the $x$-column (the inputs). If an $x$-value repeats with different $y$-values, the relation is not a function. If an $x$-value repeats with the same $y$-value, or if all $x$-values are entirely unique, the relation is a function. Example 1: | $x$ | $y$ | | :--- | :--- | | 1 | 5 | | 2 | 9 | | 3 | 14 | | 2 | 9 | | 4 | 20 | This is a function. The input $2$ appears twice, but it maps to the exact same output ($9$) both times. Example 2: | $x$ | $y$ | | :--- | :--- | | -1 | 4 | | 0 | 4 | | 2 | 6 | | -1 | 7 | | 5 | 12 | This is not a function. The input $-1$ maps to both $4$ and $7$. One input, two different outputs. Identifying Functions from Graphs: The Vertical Line Test When given a graph on the coordinate plane, you cannot easily check for repeating $x$-values by eye. Instead, you use the Vertical Line Test. Imagine dragging a straight vertical line across the entire graph. If the vertical line touches the graph at more than one point at any time, the graph does not represent a function. Straight lines (like those from Linear Equations and Inequalities) pass the test. They are functions. Parabolas (U-shaped graphs, which we will graph later in this chapter) pass the test. 9. Geometry: Two-Dimensional Figures

The Pythagorean Theorem: Finding Missing Sides Imagine you are building a wheelchair ramp for a front porch. The porch is 3 feet off the ground, and local building codes require the ramp to span 4 feet horizontally out from the porch. How long does the actual wooden ramp surface need to be? If you just add 3 and 4, you get 7 feet—but a ramp doesn’t go straight down and then straight across. It forms a diagonal slope. This diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, and to find its length, you need the Pythagorean Theorem. A right triangle is any triangle that contains exactly one 90-degree (right) angle. The two shorter sides that form the right angle are called legs, and the longest side (opposite the right angle) is the hypotenuse. The Pythagorean Theorem states: a² + b² = c² Where a and b are the legs, and c is the hypotenuse. Applying the Theorem Let's solve the ramp problem. Leg a (vertical height) = 3 feet Leg b (horizontal distance) = 4 feet Hypotenuse c (ramp length) = ? 1. Substitute the values into the formula: 3² + 4² = c² 2. Square the numbers (recall Order of Operations from Chapter 2): 9 + 16 = c² 3. Add the legs: 25 = c² 4. To find c, take the square root of both sides. On your TI-30XS MultiView, press 2nd then √ (which is above the x² button), type 25, and press ENTER. 5. c = 5 The ramp surface must be 5 feet long. Finding a Missing Leg The GED test will not always ask you to find the hypotenuse. Sometimes you will know the hypotenuse and one leg, and need to find the other. Example: A 15-foot ladder is leaning against a wall. The base of the ladder is 9 feet away from the wall. How high up the wall does the ladder reach? c = 15 (the ladder is the hypotenuse) a = 9 (the distance from the wall) b = ? (the height on the wall) 1. Set up the equation: 9² + b² = 15² 2. Square the knowns: 81 + b² = 225 3. Isolate the variable using inverse operations (as covered in Linear Equations and Inequalities). Subtract 81 from 225: b² = 144 4. Take the square root of 144: b = 12 The ladder reaches 12 feet up the wall. Circles: Circumference and Area Circles are unique among 2D figures because they don't have straight sides. 10. Three-Dimensional Figures and the Coordinate Plane

Volume and Surface Area of 3D Solids Imagine you are shipping a custom piece of cylindrical machinery, and you need to determine how much bubble wrap is required to line the inside of the crate versus how much cardboard you need to build the crate itself. This scenario perfectly illustrates the difference between volume and surface area. Volume measures the space inside an object—the amount of fluid or material it can hold. Surface area measures the total area of all the exterior faces of the object. In the Geometry: Two-Dimensional Figures module, we calculated the area of flat shapes like rectangles, triangles, and circles. Now, we take those 2D formulas into the third dimension. The GED test will require you to calculate both volume and surface area for four primary 3D solids: prisms, cylinders, cones, and spheres. Prisms and Cylinders Prisms and cylinders are structurally similar. Both have two identical, parallel bases connected by a lateral (side) surface. - A prism has polygonal bases (like rectangles or triangles) and flat sides. - A cylinder has circular bases and a curved side. Volume of Prisms and Cylinders The volume of any prism or cylinder is found by multiplying the area of its base ($B$) by its height ($h$). Formula: $V = B \times h$ For a rectangular prism, the base is a rectangle, so $B = \text{length} \times \text{width}$. For a cylinder, the base is a circle, so $B = \pi r^2$. Scenario: A cylindrical rain barrel has a radius of 1.5 feet and a height of 4 feet. How much water can it hold? 1. Find the area of the circular base: $B = \pi (1.5)^2 = 2.25\pi$ sq ft. 2. Multiply by the height: $V = 2.25\pi \times 4 = 9\pi$ cubic feet. 3. Using your TI-30XS MultiView, $9 \times \pi \approx 28.27$ cubic feet. Surface Area of Prisms and Cylinders Surface area is the sum of all the outside faces. For prisms and cylinders, this includes the two bases and the lateral surface. The lateral surface area of a cylinder or a prism can be visualized by "unrolling" the side into a flat rectangle. The width of this rectangle is the height of the solid, and the length of the rectangle is the perimeter (or circumference) of the base. - Total Surface Area of a Cylinder: $SA = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$ - ($2\pi r^2$ covers the top and bottom circles; $2\pi r h$ covers the unrolled side). - Total Surface Area of a Rectangular Prism: $SA = 2lw + 2lh + 2wh$ - (This adds the areas of all six rectangular faces).
