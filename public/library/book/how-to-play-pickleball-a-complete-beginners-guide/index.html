1. Introduction to Pickleball

What Exactly is Pickleball? Imagine a sport that takes the net and court geometry of tennis, the racket-style play of badminton, and the social, accessible pace of ping-pong, then blends them into a single game. That is pickleball. At its core, pickleball is a paddle sport played on a court significantly smaller than a tennis court. It can be played in singles (one-on-one) or doubles (two-on-two), though doubles is by far the most popular format. The game is played with a perforated plastic ball and solid paddles, and the objective is to hit the ball over the net and into the opponent's court in a way that they cannot return it. The Genetic Makeup of the Game To understand pickleball, it helps to look at its "ancestors." If you have played any of the following, you will find familiar elements: Tennis: The layout of the court, the concept of a "baseline," and the general goal of hitting a ball over a net come from tennis. However, the court is much smaller, which reduces the amount of running required. Badminton: A standard pickleball court is exactly the same size as a doubles badminton court (20' x 44'). The scale of the game—and the emphasis on quick reflexes at the net—is very similar to badminton. Table Tennis (Ping-Pong): The use of a solid paddle instead of a stringed racket makes the "feel" of the hit similar to a giant version of table tennis. A Brief History Pickleball wasn't designed by a sports committee; it was invented by accident in 1965 on Bainbridge Island, Washington. Three fathers—Joel Pritchard, Bill Bell, and Barney McCallum—returned from a day of golfing to find their children bored. They had a badminton court, but couldn't find the shuttlecocks. Improvising with what they had, they lowered the net, grabbed some improvised wooden paddles, and used a perforated plastic ball. The game evolved from a backyard pastime into a global phenomenon because of its low barrier to entry. Unlike tennis, which requires months of practice just to keep the ball in play, a beginner can learn the basic mechanics of pickleball and play a competitive game within an hour. --- The Essential Gear You don't need a massive investment to start playing, but using the correct equipment is vital for your safety and the quality of your game. 2. Fundamental Grip and Stance

The Connection Point: Why Your Grip Matters Imagine you are standing on the court, your opponent hits a fast shot toward your right side, and you swing with all your might. The paddle makes contact, but instead of the ball sailing over the net, it flies off at a wild angle, landing far out of bounds. You didn't miss because your timing was off or because you weren't strong enough; you missed because your paddle face was angled incorrectly. In pickleball, the angle of the paddle face is determined by how you hold the handle. This is your grip. Many beginners make the mistake of changing their grip for every different single shot—holding it like a frying pan for one hit and switching to a different hold for the next. This "grip switching" creates a split-second delay in your reaction time. To play efficiently, you need a reliable, versatile foundation that allows you to react instinctively. Mastering the Continental Grip While there are several ways to hold a paddle, the gold standard for beginners and pros alike is the Continental Grip. Often called the "Hammer Grip," this hold is the most versatile because it allows you to hit forehands, backhands, and volleys without having to shift your hand on the handle. How to Execute the Continental Grip To find the Continental Grip, follow these steps: 1. The Handshake: Hold the paddle handle in your non-dominant hand so it stands vertically (perpendicular to the ground). 2. The V-Shape: Extend your dominant hand and imagine you are shaking hands with the paddle. The "V" formed by your thumb and index finger should point toward the top-left slope of the handle (for right-handers) or the top-right slope (for left-handers). 3. The Wrap: Wrap your fingers firmly around the grip. Your index finger should be slightly separated from the others, acting as a guide rather than being squeezed tight against the middle finger. 4. The Pressure: Hold the paddle with a "firm but relaxed" pressure. If you grip it too tightly (the "death grip"), your wrist becomes stiff, and you lose the ability to flick the paddle for power or angle. If you hold it too loosely, the paddle will twist in your hand upon impact. Why the Continental Grip Works The beauty of the Continental Grip is that it puts the paddle face in a neutral position. Because you aren't locked into a specific angle, you can easily rotate your wrist to: Square the face for a direct drive. Angle the face to slice the ball. 3. The Basic Strokes

The Secret of the "Low to High" Path Imagine you are standing on the court. The ball is bouncing toward you, and your instinct is to swat it hard, like you're hitting a fly with a newspaper. You swing fast and flat, but the ball screams straight into the net. Why did that happen? Because in pickleball, the net is your first and most persistent opponent. To get the ball over the net and keep it inside the court, you cannot hit "through" the ball in a straight line. You must hit "up" into the ball. This is the concept of the Low to High swing path. Because the ball bounces on the ground, the paddle must start below the level of the ball and finish above it. This creates an upward arc. This arc does two things: it ensures the ball clears the net and it creates a slight natural curve (or "topspin") that helps the ball dive back down into the opponent's court rather than sailing long. If you start your paddle high and swing down, the ball goes into the net. If you swing perfectly flat, you have a very small margin for error. By mastering the "Low to High" motion, you create a safety window that allows for consistency and control. The Groundstroke Forehand A groundstroke is any shot hit after the ball has bounced once on your side of the court. The forehand is the most natural stroke for most players; it is hit from the dominant side of your body (the right side for right-handers, the left for left-handers). The Setup Before you swing, you must prepare. A common beginner mistake is waiting for the ball to arrive before moving the paddle. 1. The Unit Turn: As soon as you see the ball coming to your forehand side, turn your shoulders and hips as one unit. Do not just move your arm; rotate your entire upper body. 2. The Ready Stance: Refer back to your Fundamental Grip and Stance. Ensure your weight is on the balls of your feet, knees slightly bent, allowing you to step into the shot. 3. Paddle Position: Bring your paddle back and slightly below the expected height of the ball. This "pre-sets" your Low to High path. The Execution The goal is a smooth, fluid motion rather than a jerky "hit." 1. The Step: Step toward the ball with your non-dominant foot. This shifts your weight forward, giving the shot power from your legs rather than just your wrist. 2. The Contact Point: Aim to hit the ball in front of your body. 4. Serving and Returning

The Most Important Shot You’ll Never Win With Imagine you are standing on the baseline, paddle in hand, facing your opponent. You hit a powerful, screaming serve that lands exactly on the line. Your opponent is caught off guard, the ball bounces once, and they miss it entirely. You just won the point on the serve. In many sports, that is the ultimate goal. In pickleball, however, the serve is not a weapon used to end the point; it is simply the "key" that unlocks the door to the game. Because of the specific rules regarding how the ball must be returned, a serve that is too aggressive or too short can actually put you at a disadvantage. The goal of the serve is simple: put the ball in play safely and deeply, forcing your opponent to stay far away from the net. The goal of the return is the same: send the ball deep and high enough to give yourself time to move forward. The Legal Underhand Serve Unlike tennis, where the serve is an overhead smash, the pickleball serve must be an underhand stroke. This rule is designed to keep the game balanced and prevent the server from dominating the point before it even begins. The Three Golden Rules of the Serve To be considered a legal serve, you must satisfy three specific technical requirements at the moment of impact: 1. Underhand Motion: The arm must move in an upward arc. You cannot "spike" the ball or hit it with a downward motion. 2. Contact Point: The ball must be struck below the waist (specifically, below the navel). 3. Paddle Head Position: The highest point of the paddle head must be below the highest part of the wrist. If you violate any of these three rules, it is called a service fault, and you lose your turn to serve. Step-by-Step Execution Using the Fundamental Grip and Stance we established in Chapter 2, follow these steps to execute a consistent serve: 1. The Setup: Stand behind the baseline (the back boundary line). Both feet must be behind the line; if your toe touches the line or crosses it during the serve, it is a foot fault. 2. The Drop: Hold the ball in your non-dominant hand. You may either drop the ball and hit it on the bounce or hit it out of the air (a "volley serve"). Note: You cannot throw the ball up into the air like a tennis player. 3. The Swing: Using the fluid motion of the Basic Strokes from Chapter 3, swing your paddle in a low-to-high arc. 4. 5. The Non-Volley Zone (The Kitchen)

The Most Important Seven Feet on the Court Imagine you are in a heated rally. You’ve returned a serve, and your opponent has hit the ball back. You see an opening and decide to charge forward, sprinting toward the net to put the ball away with a powerful strike. Just as you leap into the air to smash the ball, your foot lands inside the narrow rectangular area bordering the net. Even if your shot was a perfect winner that your opponent couldn't touch, the referee blows the whistle. You just lost the point. Welcome to the most confusing, debated, and strategically vital part of the pickleball court: The Non-Volley Zone, affectionately known by every player in the world as "The Kitchen." Unlike tennis or badminton, where you can stand anywhere on your side of the net, pickleball has a strict "no-fly zone" for certain types of shots. Understanding the Kitchen is the difference between looking like a seasoned player and constantly giving away free points to your opponent. Defining the Kitchen The Non-Volley Zone (NVZ) is the 7-foot area on both sides of the net. It extends from the net back to the NVZ line (the line that marks the boundary of the Kitchen) and spans the entire width of the court. To understand the rules of the Kitchen, we first need to define a specific term: the Volley. A Volley is any shot hit out of the air before the ball bounces on the ground. If you hit the ball after it has bounced, it is no longer a volley; it is a groundstroke. The Golden Rule of the Kitchen: You cannot hit a volley while standing inside the Non-Volley Zone. If you are standing inside the Kitchen—or even touching the NVZ line—you must let the ball bounce before you can possibly hit it. If you strike the ball in the air while any part of your body is touching the Kitchen, it is a fault, and you lose the rally. What Counts as "Being in the Kitchen"? The rules regarding the Kitchen are strict and literal. A fault is called if: Your foot touches the line: The NVZ line is considered part of the Kitchen. If your toe is touching the white paint of the line when you volley, it is a fault. Your momentum carries you in: This is the most common mistake for beginners. If you hit a volley while standing outside the Kitchen, but your momentum causes you to step on the line or into the zone after the shot, it is still a fault. 6. The Dinking Game

Why "Soft" is Often Stronger than "Hard" Imagine you are standing at the edge of the Kitchen. Your opponent is across from you, just inches away. You have a sudden urge to smash the ball as hard as you can to end the point instantly. You swing with everything you’ve got—and the ball flies three feet past the net, landing well beyond the baseline. Your opponent laughs, and you’ve just handed them a point. In most sports, power is the primary weapon. In pickleball, however, the most dangerous player isn't always the one who hits the hardest; it's the one who can keep the ball soft. This is the essence of dinking. A dink is a soft, controlled shot hit from the Kitchen line that arcs over the net and lands within the opponent's Kitchen. Because the ball lands in the Non-Volley Zone, the opponent cannot volley it (hit it out of the air) without stepping into the Kitchen—which, as you learned in the previous chapter, is a foul. This forces them to let the ball bounce and hit it back upward, keeping the game in a tactical "soft-game" loop. The Purpose of the Dink: Patience as a Weapon To a beginner, dinking can feel like "just tapping the ball back and forth," and it might seem boring compared to a high-speed rally. But dinking is not about scoring the point immediately; it is about creating the opportunity to score. The primary goals of dinking are: Neutralizing Power: By keeping the ball low, you prevent your opponent from hitting a powerful smash. If the ball is below the net height when it reaches them, they cannot hit "down" on it; they must hit "up," which leaves them vulnerable. Waiting for the Mistake: Dinking is a game of patience. You are waiting for your opponent to get impatient, hit the ball too high, or hit it into the net. Moving the Opponent: By changing where you dink the ball, you can pull your opponent out of position, opening up a gap in the court for a winning shot. The Mechanics of a Great Dink Because a dink requires finesse rather than force, you must shift your mindset from the "full swing" used in the basic strokes to a "lift and push" motion. 7. Volleys and Overhead Smashes

The Moment of Truth at the Kitchen Line Imagine you are standing at the edge of the Non-Volley Zone (the Kitchen), locked in a patient dink rally. The ball has been bouncing softly, moving back and forth in a rhythmic, low-energy dance. Suddenly, your opponent miscalculates. Instead of a soft dink, they hit the ball slightly too hard, and it pops up—not as a bounce, but as a floating ball traveling directly toward your chest. In a split second, the game transforms. The slow, tactical dinking ends, and a high-speed encounter begins. You have two choices: let the ball bounce and continue the slow game, or strike it out of the air to end the point immediately. This is the essence of the volley. While the dinking game is about patience and precision, the volley is about reaction, aggression, and closing the door on your opponent. Volleys vs. Groundstrokes: Understanding the Difference Before you swing, you must understand the fundamental mechanical difference between a volley and a groundstroke. A groundstroke is any shot hit after the ball has bounced once on your side of the court. As you learned in The Basic Strokes, groundstrokes allow you more time to set your feet, take a full backswing, and dictate the direction of the ball. A volley is hitting the ball "out of the air" before it touches the ground. Because the ball never bounces, the timing is much faster. You do not have the luxury of a long wind-up. If you attempt a full groundstroke swing during a volley, the ball will likely fly past you or sail out of bounds before you can complete your motion. Volleys require "short" movements—compact, efficient strikes that use the opponent's own power against them. The Punch Volley The most essential tool for a beginner at the kitchen line is the punch volley. Think of this not as a "swing," but as a "push" or a "punch." The goal is to redirect the ball deep into the opponent's court or into an open space, using a short, controlled motion. The Mechanics of the Punch To execute a proper punch volley, follow these steps: 1. The Ready Position: Start in the stance you learned in Fundamental Grip and Stance. 8. The Third Shot Drop

The "No-Man's Land" Dilemma Imagine this: You’ve just served the ball, and your opponent has returned it deep into your court. You are standing near the baseline, and your opponent is already standing at the edge of the Kitchen, paddles ready, waiting to slam any ball you hit too hard. You have a choice. If you hit the ball hard and low, your opponent can volley it back at your feet with immense power. If you try to hit a winner from the back of the court, you’ll likely hit the net or sail it out of bounds. You are stuck in the "transition zone"—the area between the baseline and the Kitchen—and you feel exposed. How do you get to the net without getting smashed? The answer is the Third Shot Drop. It is widely considered the most important, and most difficult, shot in pickleball. Mastery of this single shot is what separates a casual backyard player from a competitive one. What is the Third Shot Drop? To understand the "Third Shot Drop," we first have to look at the sequence of a standard point: 1. The Serve: The first shot. 2. The Return: The second shot. 3. The Third Shot: This is where the drop happens. A Third Shot Drop is a soft, arching shot hit from the baseline that lands gently inside the opponent's Kitchen. Unlike a drive, which is meant to overpower the opponent, the drop is a "neutralizing" shot. Its goal is not to win the point immediately, but to force the opponent to hit the ball upward, which gives you the time and safety to move forward from the baseline to the Kitchen line. The Strategic Necessity: Why Not Just Drive It? You might wonder why you wouldn't just hit the ball as hard as possible (a "drive") to force an error. While driving is a valid strategy, doing it every time is risky. When you hit a hard drive from the baseline, the ball travels fast, but it also arrives at the opponent’s paddle fast. Because the opponents are already at the net, they can use that speed against you, hitting a powerful volley that lands at your feet before you have time to react. The drop removes that advantage. By landing the ball in the Kitchen, you force the opponent to let the ball bounce. Because the ball is low and soft, they cannot "smash" it; they must hit it upward to get it over the net. This creates a window of time—usually two to three seconds—for you to sprint forward and join them at the Kitchen line, where you can begin the Dinking Game. 9. Scoring and Game Flow

The "Wait, What's the Score?" Moment Imagine you’ve just stepped onto the court for your first real game. You’ve practiced your strokes, you can hit a decent Third Shot Drop, and you’re feeling confident. You step up to the baseline to serve, and your partner looks at you and says, "The score is 4, 2, 1." If you are coming from a background in Tennis or Table Tennis, your brain is likely searching for a second number. Why are there three? What does the "1" mean? Does it mean you're winning? Is it a tie-breaker? In pickleball, the score is more than just a tally of points; it is a roadmap of the game. It tells you who is serving, which side of the court they should be on, and whose turn it is to hit the ball. Once you decode the three-number system, the flow of the game becomes intuitive. Decoding the Three-Number System In singles play, scoring is straightforward (just like most sports). However, in doubles—the most popular way to play pickleball—you use a three-number score. The score is always called in this specific order: Server Score, Receiver Score, Server Number. 1. The First Number: Server Score The first number represents the score of the team currently serving. If the serving team has won four points, the first number is 4. 2. The Second Number: Receiver Score The second number represents the score of the team currently receiving the serve. If the receiving team has won two points, the second number is 2. 3. The Third Number: Server Number This is the part that confuses beginners most. In pickleball doubles, each team gets two opportunities to serve before the ball is turned over to the other team. The "Server Number" (either 1 or 2) indicates which partner is currently serving. Server 1: The first partner of the team to serve. Server 2: The second partner of the team to serve. Example: "4 - 2 - 1" 4: The serving team has 4 points. 2: The receiving team has 2 points. 1: The first server for the team is currently serving. How Points are Won Before we dive deeper into the sequence of play, there is one golden rule you must memorize: Points can only be scored by the serving team. If the receiving team wins a rally, they do not get a point. Instead, they earn the right to move closer to winning the serve. This is known as Side-Out logic. Serving Team wins the rally: They earn one point, and the same server switches sides (left/right) with their partner to serve again. Receiving Team wins the rally: No point is awarded. 10. Basic Strategy and Positioning

The Battle for the Net Imagine you are playing a doubles match. You’ve just served the ball, and your opponent has returned it. You are standing at the baseline (the back line of the court), and your partner is beside you. Your opponent, however, has already rushed forward and is standing right at the edge of the Non-Volley Zone (the Kitchen). Who has the advantage? In pickleball, the team closest to the net generally controls the game. When you are stuck at the baseline, you are forced to hit the ball upward to get it over the net, which gives your opponents an easy target to smash the ball down at your feet. When you are at the Kitchen line, you can hit the ball downward or keep it low, forcing your opponents to defend. Most beginners make the mistake of staying in "no man's land"—the area between the baseline and the Kitchen. This is the danger zone. To win more points, you must learn the art of positioning: how to get to the net safely and how to move with your partner as a single unit. Closing the Gap Closing the gap is the process of moving from the baseline toward the Non-Volley Zone. The goal is to reach the Kitchen line as quickly and safely as possible without leaving yourself vulnerable to a "attack" shot. The Returner’s Advantage The team receiving the serve has the easiest path to the net. Because the server must stay behind the baseline until the ball bounces, the returner can hit their shot and immediately move forward. If you are the returner: 1. Hit a deep return to the baseline. 2. Immediately move forward toward the Kitchen line. 3. Stop exactly at the edge of the Non-Volley Zone. By doing this, you have "closed the gap" and put the pressure on the serving team. The Server’s Challenge Closing the gap is harder for the serving team. Because of the "two-bounce rule" (the serve must bounce, and the return must bounce), the server cannot rush the net immediately. They must wait for the return to bounce. To close the gap as a server, you rely on the Third Shot Drop. As covered in previous chapters, a successful drop shot lands softly in the opponent's Kitchen. Because the ball is low and slow, it prevents the opponent from smashing it, giving the server enough time to run forward and join their partner at the Kitchen line. 11. Etiquette and Match Play

You step onto the court for your first round-robin match. Your partner is a friendly local who’s been playing for six months. The opposing team is a pair of retirees who’ve been playing together for years. The first serve is called, the score is set, and the game begins. Three minutes later, the ball lands just inside the sideline—was it in or out? Your partner says “Out,” the opponents say “In.” Now what? Pickleball isn’t just about skill—it’s about respect, fairness, and knowing how to navigate the game beyond the lines. The court is a small space where personalities, emotions, and expectations collide. Without clear rules of engagement, even friendly matches can turn sour. Etiquette isn’t about stiff formality; it’s the lubricant that keeps the game flowing smoothly and ensures everyone has fun—win or lose. This chapter isn’t about how hard you can hit the ball or which grip gives you more power. It’s about how to shake hands with dignity, call lines with confidence, manage your emotions under pressure, and keep the match enjoyable for everyone involved. Whether you're playing in a local rec center or at a national tournament, good etiquette is the foundation of good pickleball. --- The First Impression: Before the Match Even Starts Pickleball is a social sport by nature. Unlike tennis or table tennis, where opponents are often separated by a net and a few feet of space, pickleball players share a smaller court and often stand much closer to each other during play. That closeness means first impressions matter—not just in skill, but in attitude. Arriving on Time and Ready Show up at least 10 minutes early. Courts can be in high demand, and starting late disrupts the schedule for players behind you. Bring your paddle, water bottle, appropriate shoes (no black-soled shoes on indoor courts), and a positive attitude. If you’re playing doubles, coordinate with your partner ahead of time on positioning and strategy. Pro tip: If you’re new to a facility, ask where the scorecards are and how the booking system works. Nothing disrupts flow like a confused player trying to figure out how to reserve the next match. Greeting Your Opponents Before the match, offer a handshake, nod, or verbal greeting. A simple “Good to see you” or “Nice to meet you” sets a friendly tone. In competitive circles, this is also a moment to assess your opponents—are they relaxed and casual, or focused and intense?
