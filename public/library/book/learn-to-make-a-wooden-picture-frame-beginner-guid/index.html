2. Understanding Wood: Types, Grain, and Selection

A Real‑World Decision: Choosing Wood for a Family Photo Frame Emma has just printed a favorite family photo and wants to surround it with a custom wooden frame that will sit on her living‑room mantel. She heads to the local lumberyard, eyes a stack of pine strips and a handful of maple boards, and wonders which will give her the look she wants, last for years, and stay within her budget. The answer lies in understanding wood grain, the differences between hardwoods and softwoods, and how to inspect wood for hidden defects before buying. This chapter equips you with the knowledge to make that choice confidently, setting a solid foundation for the frame‑building steps that follow. --- 1. The Language of Wood Grain What Is Grain? Grain describes the orientation, pattern, and texture of the wood fibers that run from the inner core of a tree outward. When a tree grows, new cells are added in concentric rings; the resulting fibers give wood its directional strength and distinctive visual character. - Longitudinal grain – fibers run parallel to the length of the board. This is the strongest direction and the one you’ll cut and join most often in a frame. - Radial grain – fibers radiate outward from the tree’s center; you see this most clearly in cross‑sections (the “growth rings”). - Tangential grain – fibers follow the curve of the growth rings; it shows up in the face of a board cut across the grain. Why Grain Matters in Picture‑Frame Making 1. Strength & Stability – Wood resists bending and warping best when forces act along the grain. A frame built with consistent grain direction will stay square and resist sagging over time. 2. Aesthetic Appeal – Grain patterns (straight, wavy, curly, or figured) become the visual signature of your frame. A well‑chosen grain can complement the artwork or photograph inside. 3. Ease of Work – Cutting, sanding, and joining are smoother when tools move with the grain rather than across it. Working against the grain can cause splintering or tear‑out, especially in softer woods. Tip: When you hold a board, feel the grain with your fingertips. The smoother side typically follows the grain; the rougher side indicates a cross‑grain cut. --- 2. Hardwood vs. Softwood: Which Is Right for Your Frame? Wood classification into hardwood and softwood is based on botanical family, not on actual hardness. Hardwoods come from angiosperm (flowering) trees, while softwoods come from gymnosperm (cone‑bearing) trees. 5. Introduction to Miter Boxes and Power Tools

The 45‑Degree Challenge: Why It Matters for Picture Frames When you picture a finished wooden frame, the four sides meet at crisp, clean corners. Those corners are almost always 45‑degree miter cuts—the angle that lets two pieces join to form a perfect 90‑degree corner. If the cuts are even a degree off, the frame will look lopsided, the joints will be weak, and the glass or artwork may not sit flush. Imagine you’re building a 12‑inch‑by‑16‑inch frame for a favorite photo. You cut the first two pieces by eye, and they look “close enough.” After glue‑up, the top edge leans 3 mm higher than the bottom. The frame still looks usable, but the mis‑alignment is obvious, and you’ve wasted time sanding and adjusting. A miter box eliminates that guesswork. By guiding the saw blade along preset 45‑degree slots, it guarantees repeatable, accurate cuts—every time. And when you graduate to a power miter saw, you get the same precision at a fraction of the effort, provided you respect the safety rules. --- What Is a Miter Box and How Does It Work? A miter box is a simple, low‑tech jig that holds a hand saw at a fixed angle. Its essential parts are: | Part | Function | |------|----------| | Base | Flat platform that rests on your workbench; often has slots for clamping the workpiece. | | Angle Slots | One set cut at 45 degrees (for picture frames) and another at 90 degrees (for straight cuts). The slots are V‑shaped guides that the saw blade slides in. | | Saw Guard (optional) | A thin piece of wood or plastic that sits against the blade to keep it from wandering. | Because the slots are machined to the exact angle, the saw blade follows that path automatically. The result is a cut that is geometrically true—no need for a protractor or trial‑and‑error. Why a 45‑Degree Slot Is Ideal for Frames Symmetry – Two 45‑degree cuts combine to make a perfect right angle. Material Efficiency – A 45‑degree cut uses the full width of the board, minimizing waste. Ease of Assembly – The two pieces meet flush, making glue‑up and clamping straightforward. --- Using a Hand Saw in a Miter Box: Step‑by‑Step Even if you’ve never used a hand saw before, the miter box makes the process intuitive. 6. Creating Strong Frame Joints: Mitered Corners

Why a Perfect Miter Is the Secret to a Professional‑Looking Frame Imagine you are assembling a 12 × 12 in. picture frame for a family portrait. The wood strips are all the same beautiful walnut, the grain runs consistently, and the glass and backing are already prepared. You slide the four pieces together and—click—the corners meet. But as you step back, the frame looks slightly “off.” One corner is a fraction of a millimeter wider than the opposite side, and a faint gap is visible at the joint. The problem isn’t the wood quality or the glass; it’s the mitered corners. A tight, accurately cut miter does three things that matter to every beginner and seasoned framer alike: Aesthetic clean‑line – The joint disappears, letting the wood’s grain and finish speak for themselves. Structural integrity – Evenly distributed forces keep the frame square and resist warping over time. Ease of finishing – A good miter reduces sanding time and prevents glue squeeze‑out from ruining the final look. Getting the corner right is the foundation for everything that follows in later modules—gluing, clamping, sanding, and finishing. The steps below walk you through the anatomy of a miter joint, how to mark and cut it, how to dry‑fit and tweak it, and what to do when things don’t go as planned. --- Anatomy of a Mitered Corner Joint A miter joint is formed when the ends of two frame members are cut at equal angles—commonly 45° for a right‑angle corner—so that they meet flush. Visualize the joint as a tiny “X” when viewed from the front; each leg of the X is the face of a board, and the intersection is the seam where the two pieces touch. Why Sanding Is the Secret to a Professional‑Looking Frame Imagine you’ve just glued the mitered corners of a new picture frame, trimmed the excess wood, and are ready to showcase a cherished family photograph. You run your fingers along the edge—only to feel a series of tiny, unexpected bumps. Those “splinters” are not only uncomfortable; they catch light, reveal imperfections, and ruin the polished look you were aiming for. Sanding is the process that transforms a rough‑cut joint into a seamless surface that feels smooth to the touch and looks flawless under any lighting. It removes tool marks, evens out the grain, and prepares the wood to accept stain, paint, or a clear coat without blotches or uneven absorption. Let’s turn that beautiful piece of woodworking into a ready‑to‑hang masterpiece. Why a Backing Is Essential A backing board performs three vital jobs: 1. Support – It holds the artwork, photograph, or print flat against the frame, preventing sagging or warping over time. 2. Protection – It shields the artwork from dust, humidity, and accidental impacts that could reach the edges of the frame. 3. Stability – By locking the front and rear of the frame together, it creates a rigid “box” that resists twisting when the frame is hung. Without a backing, the frame is essentially an open picture holder that can flex, crack, or allow the artwork to slide out. The backing also provides a solid surface for attaching hanging hardware (sawtooth hanger, D‑rings, or wire), ensuring the weight of the frame is distributed evenly. 11. Mounting Artwork or Photos Inside the Frame

Why the Inside of the Frame Is Just as Important as the Outside Imagine you have finally built a beautiful walnut picture frame using the techniques from Creating Strong Frame Joints and Sanding and Smoothing the Frame for a Professional Finish. The wood grain is flawless, the stain is even, and the hardware is ready for hanging. The only thing left is to place the cherished family portrait inside. If you slide the photo directly onto the raw back of the frame, you risk scratches, moisture damage, and a rushed appearance that can undermine all the care you put into the woodworking. The interior of a frame is a tiny museum case; it needs the same thought‑out protection and presentation as the exterior. This chapter shows you, step by step, how to give your artwork a safe home, a clean look, and a lasting lifespan. --- The Role of Mat Boards and Spacers What Is a Mat Board? A mat board (often simply called a “mat”) is a thin, stiff piece of paper‑based material that sits between the artwork and the frame’s backing. It serves three primary purposes: 1. Protection – It creates a physical barrier that keeps the artwork from rubbing against the glass or the frame’s wood. 2. Breathing Space – By separating the image from the glass, it reduces the risk of condensation forming on the surface of the artwork. 3. Presentation – A well‑chosen mat adds visual balance, highlights the image, and can complement the colors of the frame. Why Use a Spacer? A spacer (sometimes called a “frame spacer” or “inner frame”) is a thin strip of wood, foam, or hardboard that raises the artwork a few millimetres away from the glass. Spacers are especially useful when: The artwork is a delicate watercolor or pastel that could be damaged by the slight pressure of glass. The frame is deep (greater than 1 ½ in) and you want the image to sit at eye level rather than being pressed flat. Both mats and spacers are acid‑free or archival‑grade when you purchase them for fine‑art purposes. Using non‑acidic materials prevents yellowing over time—a concern you learned about in the Protection section of the introduction. --- Preparing the Mat Board Choosing the Right Thickness and Color | Thickness (typical) | Recommended Use | |---------------------|-----------------| | 0.25 mm (paper‑thin) | Small photos, inexpensive frames | | 0.38 mm (standard) | Most prints, photographs, art prints | | 0.50 mm (heavy) | Fine art, watercolor, archival displays | Color is a matter of taste, but a neutral tone (white, cream, or light gray) usually lets the artwork shine. Wood Burning (Pyrography) What it is – Using a heated tip to scorch the wood surface, creating lines, shading, or patterns. Tools you’ll need - Pyrography pen (basic models have interchangeable tips) – already in your toolbox from Essential Hand Tools for Woodworking. - Fine‑grit sandpaper (to prep the surface). - Safety gloves and a well‑ventilated workspace (burning releases faint smoke). Step‑by‑step 1. Prep the surface – Lightly sand the area you plan to burn until it feels smooth. Wipe away dust. 2. Plan your design – Sketch lightly with a pencil or use a stencil. Simple motifs like leaves, initials, or geometric lines work well for beginners. 3. Set the temperature – Most pens have a dial; start low and increase if the wood isn’t darkening. 4. Practice on a scrap piece – This helps you gauge pressure and speed. 5. Burn the design – Move the tip steadily; let the heat do the work—don’t press hard. 6. Seal the burnt area – After the finish from Chapter 9 has dried, apply a thin coat of clear polyurethane to protect the charred lines. Tips - Grain direction matters – Burn along the grain for smoother lines; across grain can cause splintering. - Contrast – Darker wood (e.g., walnut) yields subtle marks; lighter woods (e.g., pine) give striking contrast. 2.
