1. Exam Strategy and Study Planning

The True Cost of Walking in Unprepared Imagine spending 250 hours sacrificing your evenings, weekends, and social life, only to open your CFA Level 1 exam results to a "Did Not Pass." For a significant portion of candidates, this isn't a hypothetical scenario—it's a harsh reality. The CFA Institute historically reports pass rates for Level 1 that frequently hover between 35% and 45%. The most common culprit isn't a lack of intelligence or a background in liberal arts; it's a failure to treat the CFA exam as a strategic project. Walking into the testing center without a meticulously structured study plan, a deep understanding of the exam's mechanics, and the right materials is akin to entering a marathon having only jogged on a treadmill. To pass Level 1, you must transition from a student of finance to a manager of your own learning. This means understanding the computer-based testing (CBT) environment, reverse-engineering the curriculum based on topic weightings, and building a personalized 300-hour timeline that adapts to your professional background. Decoding the Computer-Based Testing Format The CFA Level 1 exam is no longer a grueling, single-day, paper-and-pencil marathon. The CFA Institute transitioned to a Computer-Based Testing (CBT) format, fundamentally changing how you must approach test day. The exam is divided into two distinct sessions, each lasting 2 hours and 15 minutes. You will face 90 multiple-choice questions in each session, totaling 180 questions. Between the two sessions, you are granted an optional break of up to 30 minutes. Crucially, the exam is closed book—you will not have access to the curriculum, notes, or a physical formula sheet. You will be provided with a digital notepad/scratchpad on your screen, and typically a physical laminated booklet and marker at the testing center for manual calculations. The Mechanics of Level 1 Questions Every question on the Level 1 exam is independent. Unlike case-study formats, each item stands alone, though some questions may share a common vignette (a short scenario). Each question presents three distinct choices (A, B, and C). There is no penalty for guessing, meaning you must answer every single question, even if it is a blind guess in the final seconds of the exam. Because the exam is entirely multiple-choice, your study strategy must emphasize pattern recognition and rapid calculation over writing long-form derivations. You need to get comfortable doing mental math and using the on-screen calculator quickly. The Approved Calculator Constraint You are only permitted to use one of two calculator models during the exam: the Texas Instruments BA II Plus (including the Professional version) or the Hewlett Packard 12C (including the Platinum edition). 2. Ethical and Professional Standards

The Code vs. The Standards: Knowing the Difference On the CFA Level 1 exam, candidates frequently blur the lines between the Code of Ethics and the Standards of Professional Conduct. Understanding the distinction is not just a matter of memorization; it forms the basis of how the CFA Institute expects you to frame your ethical decision-making. The Code of Ethics is the overarching philosophical framework. It is a brief, high-level set of six principles that establishes the moral foundation for the investment profession. Think of the Code as the "why" and the "what"—it dictates that you must act with integrity, place client interests above your own, and maintain your professional competence. The Standards of Professional Conduct are the "how." They are the specific, operational rules that enforce the Code. The Standards are divided into seven broad categories: 1. Professionalism 2. Integrity of Capital Markets 3. Duties to Clients 4. Duties to Employers 5. Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions 6. Conflicts of Interest 7. Responsibilities as a CFA Institute Member or CFA Candidate If the Code says "act with integrity," the Standards say "don't trade on material nonpublic information" and "don't misrepresent your performance history." The exam will test your ability to identify a violation and correctly map it to the specific Standard it falls under. Applying the Standards to Real-World Dilemmas Ethics on the CFA exam is rarely about obvious villainy. A candidate won't be asked if it's okay to embezzle client funds. Instead, the exam presents nuanced, everyday situations where well-intentioned professionals make critical errors in judgment. The key to mastering these scenarios is recognizing the subtle triggers that indicate a violation has occurred. Here are the most heavily tested areas of the Standards, applied to realistic scenarios. Professionalism: Knowledge of the Law and Independence Scenario: You are a portfolio manager based in Singapore. You are analyzing a foreign corporate issuer. In the issuer's home country, it is perfectly legal to compensate client referral agents without disclosing the arrangement to the referred client. However, the CFA Standards require disclosure of such compensation. Your firm's compliance manual states that you should follow local law above all else. What should you do? Application: Under Standard I(A) – Knowledge of the Law, you must adhere to the most strict applicable law, rule, or regulation. When local law, firm policy, and the CFA Standards conflict, you must follow the strictest rule. In this case, the CFA Standards are stricter than local law regarding referral disclosure. You must disclose the compensation. Exam Trap: Candidates often assume "follow local law" is a sufficient defense. It is not. 3. Quantitative Methods

The Time Value of Money: Beyond Standard Annuities You are evaluating a junior mining company. The firm requires an initial capital expenditure of $50 million today. Based on geological surveys, management projects the mine will generate $10 million in Year 1, $15 million in Year 2, $25 million in Year 3, and a terminal environmental reclamation cost of $5 million in Year 4. How do you determine if this investment meets your required rate of return? Standard annuity functions on your Texas Instruments BA II Plus or Hewlett Packard 12C won't help you here. The cash flows are unequal, and the final cash flow is an outflow. To solve this, you must rely on your calculator’s Cash Flow (CF) worksheet. Mastering Uneven Cash Flows The time value of money (TVM) framework assumes that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. While standard TVM problems handle level annuities, real-world investment analysis almost always involves uneven cash flows. To value these streams, you must calculate the Net Present Value (NPV) and the Internal Rate of Return (IRR). NPV calculates the value of the cash flow stream in today’s dollars given a specific discount rate. IRR calculates the discount rate that makes the NPV exactly zero. BA II Plus Workflow for Uneven Cash Flows: 1. Press [CF] to enter the Cash Flow worksheet. 2. Clear previous data by pressing [2nd] [CE/C] (CLR WORK). 3. CF0 is the initial cash flow at Time 0. Enter the $50 million outflow as -50, then press [ENTER] and [↓]. 4. C01 is the cash flow for Year 1. Enter 10, press [ENTER] and [↓]. 5. F01 is the frequency of C01. Since the Year 1 cash flow only happens once, enter 1, press [ENTER] and [↓]. 6. Repeat this for C02 (15), C03 (25), and C04 (-5), ensuring all frequencies (F01-F04) are set to 1. 7. Press [NPV]. The calculator will prompt you for the discount rate (I). Enter your required rate of return (e.g., 10 for 10%), press [ENTER], then [↓]. 8. Press [CPT] (Compute) to find the NPV. If the NPV is positive, the investment's return exceeds your required rate of return. Alternatively, you can compute the IRR by pressing [IRR] from the CF worksheet and pressing [CPT]. Compare the IRR to your required rate of return to make your accept/reject decision. 4. Economics

Imagine you are analyzing a specialty semiconductor manufacturer. The company reports record revenues, but its stock plummets 15% in a single day. Why? Because while revenues were high, the pricing power driving those revenues was shifting. A glut of inventory in the supply chain signaled that next quarter's average selling prices would collapse. To anticipate moves like this, investment professionals use microeconomics to understand how markets allocate resources and how prices are determined. At the CFA Level 1 level, microeconomics is less about drawing graphs and more about identifying the slopes, the underlying drivers, and the implications of shifts. Assume the fundamental shapes of supply and demand curves are known; the exam focuses on what happens when they move. Market Equilibrium and Interference Market equilibrium occurs where the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded. The CFA exam frequently tests your understanding of what happens when external forces—specifically governments—interfere with this natural equilibrium. Price Ceilings: Set below the equilibrium price. Result: Shortages. Producers lack the incentive to supply the good at the artificially low price. Price Floors: Set above the equilibrium price. Result: Surpluses. Consumers demand less at the artificially high price. Quotas: A legal limit on the quantity of a good that can be produced or imported. This restricts supply, artificially raising the price and transferring consumer surplus to producers. Consumer and Producer Surplus: The exam expects you to calculate or identify these on a graph. Consumer surplus is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and the market price. Producer surplus is the difference between the market price and the marginal cost of production. When a quota or tax is introduced, the total surplus decreases, creating what economists call deadweight loss. Elasticity and Total Revenue Understanding how sensitive quantity demanded is to a price change is critical for forecasting corporate revenues. Elastic Demand: Absolute value of price elasticity 1. A price increase leads to a decrease in total revenue. Inelastic Demand: Absolute value of price elasticity < 1. A price increase leads to an increase in total revenue. Exam Application: If a company announces it is raising prices by 10% and you know the industry's price elasticity of demand is -1.5, you can forecast the impact on unit sales. Quantity demanded will drop by 15% (10% price increase × 1.5 elasticity). Because the percentage drop in volume exceeds the percentage increase in price, total revenue will actually decline. This is the kind of rapid, Quantitative Methods-style calculation you must perform efficiently using your Texas Instruments BA II Plus. 5. Financial Statement Analysis: Foundations

The Trillion-Dollar Paper Trail In early 2009, investors in Satyam Computer Services—then India's fourth-largest IT outsourcing firm—were reviewing financial statements that showed over $1 billion in cash and equivalents. The problem? The cash did not exist. The company’s chairman had fabricated 13,000 fake employees to siphon funds and inflated revenue by recognizing fictitious transactions. The "Enron of India" collapsed overnight, destroying billions in shareholder value. Financial statements are the ultimate scorecard of corporate health, but they are built on management estimates, accounting choices, and sometimes outright fraud. For the CFA Level 1 exam, Financial Statement Analysis (FSA) is a High-Weight Core topic. You cannot pass the CFA exam without mastering the mechanics of how these statements interconnect, how to calculate ratios on your Texas Instruments BA II Plus, and how accounting choices distort reality. Building on the foundational mechanics of Quantitative Methods, we will now decode the language of business: the three primary financial statements, the ratios used to dissect them, and the critical divergences between IFRS and US GAAP. The Three Primary Financial Statements To analyze a company, you must understand how the Income Statement, the Balance Sheet, and the Cash Flow Statement interact. They are not isolated documents; they are deeply interconnected. The Income Statement The income statement summarizes a company's financial performance over a specific period. It follows a basic structure: Revenue minus Expenses equals Net Income. Key concepts for the exam include: - Revenue Recognition: Under both IFRS (IFRS 15) and US GAAP (ASC 606), revenue is recognized when control of a good or service transfers to the customer. This requires the "five-step model" (identifying the contract, identifying performance obligations, determining the price, allocating the price, and recognizing revenue upon satisfaction of the obligation). - Operating vs. Non-Operating Items: The income statement is typically split into operating and non-operating sections. Operating items (like cost of goods sold and selling expenses) relate to the core business. Non-operating items (like interest expense or gains/losses from asset sales) are peripheral. - Discontinued Operations: A component of an entity that has been disposed of or is classified as held for sale. It must be reported separately, net of tax, below the line of continuing operations. - Comprehensive Income: Net income alone does not capture all changes in equity. Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) captures items that bypass the income statement, such as unrealized gains/losses on available-for-sale debt securities, foreign currency translation adjustments, and adjustments for minimum pension liabilities. The Balance Sheet Also known as the statement of financial position, the balance sheet provides a snapshot of a company's assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time. 6. Financial Statement Analysis: Advanced Topics

Inventory Costing Methods and Financial Impact Imagine two identical retail companies operating in the same market, selling the same products, and facing the same inflationary environment. Company A reports rapidly rising gross margins and strong net income. Company B reports flat margins and pays lower taxes. Both are telling the truth. The difference lies entirely in an accounting election: their inventory costing method. For the CFA Level 1 exam, inventory accounting is a frequent testing ground for your ability to trace an accounting choice through the financial statements. Under both IFRS and US GAAP, inventory is initially recorded at cost. When inventory is sold, the cost must be allocated to Cost of Goods Sold (COGS). The two primary cost flow assumptions you must master are FIFO (First-In, First-Out) and Weighted Average Cost. (Note: While LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) is permitted under US GAAP, it is prohibited under IFRS. The CFA Institute has increasingly focused on IFRS-aligned treatments, but LIFO remains highly testable because of its distinct financial statement effects and the specific disclosures required under US GAAP.) FIFO vs. Weighted Average vs. LIFO In an environment of rising costs (the default assumption for most exam questions unless stated otherwise): - FIFO: The oldest, cheapest inventory costs are assigned to COGS. The newest, most expensive costs remain in ending inventory on the balance sheet. - LIFO: The newest, most expensive costs are assigned to COGS. The oldest, cheapest costs remain in ending inventory. - Weighted Average: Costs are blended. COGS and ending inventory fall between the FIFO and LIFO extremes. Tracing the Financial Statement Impacts When costs are rising, the choice of inventory method creates a cascade of effects. Let’s compare FIFO and LIFO: During Rising Costs: - COGS: FIFO is lower; LIFO is higher. - Net Income: FIFO is higher (due to lower COGS); LIFO is lower. - Ending Inventory: FIFO is higher (reflects current, higher costs); LIFO is lower. - Cash Flow: FIFO results in higher pre-tax income, thus higher taxes, and therefore lower operating cash flow. LIFO results in lower taxes and higher operating cash flow. Because LIFO generally results in lower net income but higher cash flow (due to tax savings), analysts often prefer LIFO for valuation purposes during inflation. However, LIFO distorts the balance sheet by leaving outdated, artificially low inventory values. The LIFO Reserve and Conversions To make LIFO-based balance sheets comparable to FIFO-based ones, US GAAP requires companies using LIFO to disclose a LIFO Reserve. 7. Corporate Issuers and Equity Investments

Capital Budgeting: Evaluating Investment Decisions A corporate board approves a $50 million expansion project. The CEO projects it will generate $10 million in annual after-tax cash flows for seven years. On paper, the company is expanding. In reality, if the firm's cost of capital is 9%, the board has just destroyed $4.7 million in shareholder value. Capital budgeting is the process of evaluating and selecting long-term investments that align with the company's objective of maximizing shareholder wealth. For the CFA Level 1 exam, you must master the application of the primary capital budgeting tools: Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR). Net Present Value (NPV) NPV is the undisputed king of capital budgeting criteria. It calculates the present value of a project's expected future cash flows, discounted at the firm's cost of capital, minus the initial investment. Because the Texas Instruments BA II Plus and Hewlett Packard 12C are the only approved calculators for the CBT environment, you should rely on their Cash Flow (CF) worksheets rather than computing individual present values manually. The decision rule is absolute: - If NPV 0: Accept the project. It earns more than the required rate of return. - If NPV < 0: Reject the project. It destroys value. Internal Rate of Return (IRR) The IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV equal to zero. It represents the project's expected annualized rate of return. The decision rule depends on the hurdle rate (the firm's cost of capital): - If IRR Cost of Capital: Accept. - If IRR < Cost of Capital: Reject. NPV vs. IRR: Conflicts and Pitfalls While NPV and IRR usually yield the same accept/reject decision for independent projects, they can conflict when evaluating mutually exclusive projects. A conflict occurs when one project has a higher NPV but a lower IRR than another. This conflict is driven by two factors: 1. Differences in project scale: One project requires a significantly larger initial investment. 2. Differences in cash flow timing: One project generates cash flows earlier than the other. When a conflict arises, always default to the NPV rule. NPV directly measures the absolute dollar amount of value created for shareholders. IRR, by contrast, measures a percentage return and assumes that interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself—an often unrealistic assumption. NPV safely assumes reinvestment at the firm's cost of capital. Additionally, be on the lookout for non-conventional cash flows (e.g., an initial outflow followed by inflows, then a major outflow for environmental cleanup at the end of the project). These shifting cash flow streams can produce multiple IRRs, rendering the metric useless. NPV remains reliable in these scenarios. Corporate Governance and Shareholder Value Evaluating a …

8. Fixed Income

The Price-Yield Inverse Relationship Imagine a corporate treasurer issuing $500 million in 10-year bonds at a time when prevailing interest rates are 4%. Two years later, macroeconomic shifts—concepts covered in Economics:—push prevailing market yields to 6%. If the treasurer’s company suddenly needs to buy back those bonds in the open market, they will not have to pay the face value. Because investors can now buy newly issued bonds yielding 6%, the company's older 4% bonds must trade at a discount to compete. This inverse relationship between bond prices and yields is the foundational pillar of fixed income valuation. As we transition from the issuer’s perspective in Corporate Issuers: to the investor’s perspective, our focus shifts to exactly how those prices are calculated, how yields are derived, and how to measure the risk that interest rate movements pose to a bond's value. Bond Valuation and Yield Measures At its core, a bond is simply a stream of predictable future cash flows: periodic coupon payments and a final principal repayment at maturity. Because these cash flows are contractually fixed, valuing a bond is a direct application of the discounted cash flow (DCF) techniques mastered in Quantitative Methods:. The price of a bond is the present value of its expected future cash flows, discounted at a rate that reflects the time value of money and the risk of those cash flows. $$P = \sum{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+r)^n}$$ Where: - P = Current market price - C = Periodic coupon payment - F = Face value (par value) - r = Required yield (discount rate per period) - n = Number of periods until maturity Yield to Maturity (YTM) vs. Spot Rates For a standard option-free bond, the YTM is the single discount rate that equates the present value of the bond's cash flows to its current market price. It is an internal rate of return (IRR). However, YTM operates on a critical assumption: that all coupon payments are reinvested at the YTM itself, and all cash flows are discounted at that same uniform rate. In reality, interest rates vary across different time horizons. The spot rate is the yield to maturity on a zero-coupon bond maturing at a specific date. Spot rates represent the actual market interest rate for a single, discrete time horizon. To accurately value a bond, you should discount each individual cash flow using the spot rate corresponding to the time period in which that cash flow is received. This is known as arbitrage-free valuation. If a bond's price calculated using spot rates differs from its market price, an arbitrageur could theoretically buy the bond and strip it into zero-coupon bonds (or vice versa) to make a risk-free profit. 9. Derivatives and Alternative Investments

Imagine you are the CFO of an airline. Fuel is your largest operating expense, and a sudden spike in oil prices could wipe out your profit margin for the year. You cannot control the price of crude oil, but you can sign a contract today to buy fuel six months from now at a fixed price. By doing so, you have just used a derivative. Derivatives and alternative investments often seem like the exclusive domain of Wall Street quants and ultra-high-net-worth individuals. However, at their core, they are simply tools for transferring risk and diversifying portfolios. For the CFA Level 1 exam, you need to understand how these instruments are priced, how their payoffs behave, and the unique risk-return profiles they offer. Derivative Fundamentals and Pricing A derivative is a financial instrument whose value is derived from an underlying asset, index, or interest rate. As we established in Fixed Income and Equity Investments, the value of traditional securities is generally driven by a firm's cash flows and discount rates. Derivatives, however, are driven strictly by the price of the underlying asset and the cost of carrying that asset over time. Forwards vs. Futures Both forward contracts and futures contracts are agreements to buy or sell an underlying asset at a predetermined price at a specified future date. The key difference lies in their trading venues and counterparty risk: Forwards are customized, over-the-counter (OTC) contracts. They are subject to counterparty default risk and are settled only at maturity. Futures are standardized, exchange-traded contracts. The exchange acts as the counterparty to both sides, virtually eliminating default risk. They are marked-to-market daily, meaning gains and losses are settled at the end of every trading day. The Cost of Carry Model To price a forward or futures contract, the CFA exam relies on the cost of carry model. The fundamental principle is that the forward price must equal the spot price compounded at the risk-free rate, plus any costs of storing the asset, minus any income yielded by the asset. The formula is: F = S × e^(r + c - i)T Where: F = Forward price S = Spot price e = Exponential function (continuous compounding) r = Risk-free rate c = Storage costs (as a percentage) i = Convenience yield or income (as a percentage) T = Time to maturity (in years) Scenario: You are pricing a 6-month forward contract on an ounce of gold. 10. Portfolio Management

Imagine a 35-year-old tech executive with a high salary, significant concentrated stock options, and a 30-year horizon to retirement. Now imagine a 68-year-old retiree who needs to draw down $50,000 a year from her investments to cover living expenses. Handing both of them the exact same investment portfolio would be malpractice. Portfolio management is the art and science of matching an investor's unique financial realities with the optimal mix of assets. On the CFA Level 1 exam, questions often test your ability to determine why a specific portfolio is appropriate for a specific client. To do this, you must master the structured portfolio management process. The process consists of three distinct steps: Planning, Execution, and Feedback. Step 1: Planning The planning step is the foundation of the entire relationship. It involves understanding the client’s circumstances and documenting them in an Investment Policy Statement (IPS). The IPS is a legally binding document that governs the manager's actions. If a portfolio manager violates the IPS, they are in breach of their fiduciary duty—a concept closely tied to the material in Ethical and Professional Standards. Step 2: Execution Once the IPS is established, the manager determines the target asset allocation. This is where the bulk of a portfolio's return and risk is determined. Execution also involves the actual selection of specific securities and the rebalancing of the portfolio to its target weights. Step 3: Feedback Markets are dynamic, and client lives change. The feedback step involves monitoring the portfolio relative to its benchmark, rebalancing when asset class weights drift outside of acceptable bands, and periodically reviewing and updating the IPS to reflect changes in the client's life or objectives. The Investment Policy Statement (IPS) Components For the CFA Level 1 exam, you must be able to identify and differentiate the components of an IPS. The exam frequently presents a mock IPS and asks you to identify what is missing or incorrectly stated. Objectives: Risk and Return An investor’s objectives are defined by two interrelated metrics: risk tolerance and return objectives. Risk Tolerance is evaluated on two fronts: - Willingness to take risk: The client’s psychological comfort with market volatility. A client who checks their portfolio daily and panics during market corrections has a low willingness to take risk, regardless of their wealth. - Ability to take risk: The client’s financial capacity to withstand losses. A young professional with a long time horizon and a stable, high income has a high ability to take risk. A retiree depending on portfolio withdrawals has a low ability to take risk. 11. Final Review and Mock Exam Execution

The Final Four Weeks: Structuring Your Review Phase You have spent months navigating the intricacies of discounting cash flows, parsing the nuances of lease accounting, and memorizing the rote mechanics of GIPS. The foundation is built. But the transition from studying to passing happens in the final four weeks. This phase is less about learning new material and entirely about consolidating, optimizing, and rehearsing. Consider a candidate who scores brilliantly on individual topic quizzes but fails the actual exam. They didn’t lack intelligence or work ethic; they lacked stamina, triage skills, and a calibrated sense of timing. The computer-based exam is a psychological and physical endurance event as much as an academic one. To cross the Minimum Passing Score (MPS) threshold, you must treat the final month as a dress rehearsal. Triage Your Knowledge Base With four weeks remaining, you do not have time to reread every reading from the previous ten modules. Instead, categorize your knowledge into three buckets: 1. High-Weight Core Mastered: Topics like Ethics, Financial Statement Analysis (FSA), Equity Investments, and Fixed Income where you are consistently scoring above 75%. 2. High-Weight Core Weak: High-weight topics where your scores are languishing below 65%. 3. Lower-Weight Supplemental: Topics like Economics or Portfolio Management where you have moderate proficiency. Your time allocation should heavily favor Bucket 2. If you are weak in Revenue Recognition under FSA: Advanced Topics, target that specific reading. Do not simply redo a broad FSA quiz; do a set of 30 questions strictly on revenue recognition. Targeted Remediation of Weak Areas Identifying weak areas is only half the battle; remediating them requires a surgical approach. The goal is to move topics from your "weak" column to your "competent" column. You do not need to achieve perfection in your weakest areas—you simply need to secure enough points to clear the MPS. The Error Log If you haven't maintained an error log throughout your studies, start one immediately. Every time you miss a practice question, log the following: Topic and Reading: (e.g., Quantitative Methods: Hypothesis Testing) Nature of the Error: Was it a conceptual gap, a calculation error, a formula mix-up, or a misread question?
