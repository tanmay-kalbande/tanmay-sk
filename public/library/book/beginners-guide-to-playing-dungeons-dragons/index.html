1. 1. What is Dungeons & Dragons?

A First‑Night Adventure: Why a Simple Dice Roll Can Turn a Living Room Into a Whole World Imagine a Saturday evening. Four friends are gathered around a coffee table, snacks within reach, a stack of dice clinking together, and a single sheet of paper covered with scribbled notes and a hand‑drawn map. One friend—Emily—leans forward, eyes bright, and says, “You hear the distant howl of a wolf pack, and the wind carries a faint, metallic scent. What do you do?” In that moment the ordinary room fades. The table becomes a tavern’s stone floor, the map a sprawling wilderness, and each player’s character—perhaps a brave dwarf fighter, a curious elf wizard, a quick‑witted halfling rogue, or a steadfast human cleric—steps into a story that none of them have ever lived before. The dice they roll will decide whether the wolf pack is a threat, a clue, or something else entirely. Welcome to Dungeons & Dragons (D&D), the tabletop role‑playing game that turns imagination into shared adventure. This chapter will take you from the game’s surprising beginnings to the essential pieces that make every session possible, and it will show why D&D is, at its heart, a collaborative storytelling experience. --- 1. The Origins of Dungeons & Dragons 1.1 From Wargames to Imagination - 1971–1973: The roots of D&D lie in miniature wargames. Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson, two hobbyists from the United States, were experimenting with “miniature battles” that focused on individual soldiers rather than whole armies. - 1974: Gygax co‑founded TSR (Tactical Studies Rules) to publish the first edition of Dungeons & Dragons. The game combined the tactical combat of wargames with fantasy storytelling drawn from mythology, classic literature, and early fantasy novels (e.g., J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Lord of the Rings). - Key Innovation: Instead of commanding an entire army, each player controls a single character—a heroic figure with distinct abilities—exploring dungeons, battling monsters, and solving puzzles. The game’s rules provided a framework, but the story itself was created at the table. 1.2 Evolution Through the Decades | Edition | Year | Notable Changes | |---------|------|-----------------| | Original D&D | 1974 | Simple rules, heavily influenced by wargaming. | | Advanced D&D (AD&D) | 1977–1989 | Expanded character options, more detailed combat and magic systems. | | Second Edition | 1989 | Streamlined rules, introduced “rules‑light” approach for some settings. | | Third Edition | 2000 | Introduced the d20 System, a unified mechanic using a 20‑sided die for most actions. | | 3.5 Edition | 2003 | Refinements and clarifications to the d20 system. | | Fourth Edition | 2008 | Emphasized tactical combat, more akin to board‑game miniatures. | | Fifth Edition (5e) | 2014 | Focused …

2. 2. Core Rulebooks and How They Work

A Game Night in the Making Imagine you’ve just gathered a group of friends around a table. The snacks are ready, the dice are clinking, and everyone is buzzing with excitement. Before the first monster roars or the first spell sparks, someone reaches into a stack of books and pulls out a slim, colorful volume. “This is where we’ll find out what our characters can do,” they announce. In that moment the three core rulebooks of Dungeons & Dragons (5e) step into the spotlight, each with a distinct job that keeps the adventure running smoothly. If you’ve never opened one of these books, you might wonder why a single game needs three different tomes. The answer lies in how D&D separates the responsibilities of the players and the Dungeon Master (DM), and how it bundles the world’s monsters and magic items. This chapter walks you through each book, shows you exactly where to find the rules you’ll need for character creation and everyday play, explains the line between official rules and optional “house rules,” and points you to the quick‑reference charts that will become your tabletop shortcuts. --- The Three Pillars of the 5e Library 1. Player’s Handbook (PHB) – The Player’s Playbook The Player’s Handbook is the go‑to guide for anyone who will play a character. It contains: Character creation rules – ability scores, races, classes, backgrounds, and the step‑by‑step process for building a hero. Core mechanics – how to roll ability checks, attack rolls, saving throws, and how the d20 (20‑sided die) interacts with modifiers. Equipment and spells – lists of weapons, armor, gear, and the full catalog of spells for spell‑casting classes. Class features – the special abilities that define each class (e.g., a Rogue’s Sneak Attack or a Cleric’s Divine Domain). Think of the PHB as the player’s rulebook. Anything you need to know about what your character can do lives here. 2. Dungeon Master’s Guide (DMG) – The DM’s Toolbox The Dungeon Master’s Guide is aimed at the person who tells the story, adjudicates the rules, and creates the world. Its primary sections include: World‑building advice – guidelines for creating towns, dungeons, and entire continents. Magic items – a treasure trove of artifacts, weapons, and wondrous items, each with detailed mechanics. Optional rules – variants for combat, downtime, and character advancement that the DM may choose to use. Creative inspiration – random tables for encounters, plot hooks, and NPC personalities. The DMG is the DM’s rulebook. It supplies the tools for shaping the adventure and expanding the game beyond the basics found in the PHB. 3. 3. 3. Building Your First Character

A First‑Time Hero in Action Maya has never rolled dice for a tabletop game before, but she’s eager to join her friends for a night of adventure. She opens the Player’s Handbook and sees a page titled “Creating a Character.” The first thing she needs to decide is who she wants to be in the world of D&D: a nimble elf scout, a sturdy dwarf fighter, a clever human wizard… The choices feel overwhelming, but the process can be broken down into four easy steps: 1. Pick a race – the species that shapes your looks, lifespan, and innate abilities. 2. Choose a class – the profession that defines what you can do in combat and beyond. 3. Generate ability scores – the six numbers that measure your raw physical and mental power. 4. Add the finishing touches – background, personality quirks, and a simple character sheet. Below is a step‑by‑step guide that walks Maya (and you) through each of these decisions, with clear explanations of the jargon that appears for the first time. --- 1. Understanding the Five Ability Scores Every character in D&D is built on five ability scores (sometimes called abilities). They are the core statistics that the game uses to resolve most actions, from swinging a sword to deciphering an ancient rune. The scores are: | Ability | What It Measures | Typical Uses | |---------|------------------|--------------| | Strength (STR) | Raw physical power | Melee attacks, lifting, breaking objects | | Dexterity (DEX) | Agility, reflexes, coordination | Ranged attacks, initiative order, armor class, stealth | | Constitution (CON) | Endurance and health | Hit points, resisting disease, staying awake | | Intelligence (INT) | Analytical ability, memory | Knowledge checks, spellcasting for Wizards | | Wisdom (WIS) | Perception and insight | Spotting danger, resisting mental effects, spellcasting for Clerics | | Charisma (CHA) | Force of personality | Persuasion, deception, leadership, spellcasting for Bards | Each ability score is a number ranging from 1 (virtually no ability) to 20 (extraordinary). For beginners, scores will usually fall between 8 and 15 before racial bonuses are applied. How Scores Affect Gameplay When you attempt an action that calls for an ability check, the Dungeon Master (DM) asks you to roll a twenty‑sided die (d20) and add the appropriate ability modifier. The modifier is derived from the ability score: \[ \text{Modifier} = \left\lfloor\frac{\text{Score} - 10}{2}\right\rfloor \] For example, a Strength of 14 yields a +2 modifier (because (14‑10)/2 = 2). A low score of 9 gives a –1 modifier. These modifiers are added to attack rolls, skill checks, saving throws, and many other rolls throughout the game. 4. 4. Equipment, Spells, and Character Advancement

Choosing Your Starting Gear Imagine you’ve just rolled your 1d20, scribbled down your character’s ability scores, and picked a class—perhaps a Fighter who wants to charge into battle or a Wizard who prefers to stay behind a desk littered with dusty tomes. The next step is picking the equipment that will let you survive the first few encounters. The Starting Equipment tables in the Player’s Handbook give you two options for each class: 1. Take the listed gear – a ready‑made bundle that matches the class theme. 2. Roll for gold – you receive a set amount of gold (e.g., 5 gp for a Fighter) and buy whatever you think fits best. Both routes end with the same three categories of gear: weapons, armor, and mundane gear. Understanding the differences between them will help you make choices that suit your play style and budget. 5. 5. Core Gameplay Mechanics

A Goblin Ambush: The First Turn of Combat You and your party have just entered the dim corridor of an ancient ruin when a guttural snarl echoes from the shadows. Three goblins leap out, daggers flashing. In the next few seconds everything that has been learned about your character—ability scores, weapons, spells—will be put to the test. How do you decide who acts first? What can each adventurer do on their turn? How do the iconic d20 rolls determine success or failure? This chapter walks you through every step, from the moment the goblins appear to the resolution of a single combat round. --- Initiative – Determining Turn Order Initiative is the mechanic that decides the order in which participants act during combat. It represents how quickly each creature reacts to the sudden danger. 1. Roll a d20 – Each player (and the Dungeon Master for the monsters) rolls a twenty‑sided die. 2. Add your Dexterity modifier – The same modifier that influences your character’s agility, found on the character sheet created in Chapter 3. 3. Compare the totals – The highest total goes first, the next highest second, and so on. If two or more totals are identical, the tie‑breaker is usually the higher Dexterity score; if those are also equal, the DM decides the order. Example Initiative Rolls | Creature | d20 Roll | Dexterity Mod. | Initiative Total | |----------|----------|----------------|------------------| | Fighter (you) | 14 | +2 | 16 | | Wizard (friend) | 8 | +1 | 9 | | Goblin 1 | 12 | +2 | 14 | | Goblin 2 | 6 | +2 | 8 | | Goblin 3 | 18 | +2 | 20 | Resulting order: Goblin 3 → Fighter → Goblin 1 → Wizard → Goblin 2. --- Types of Actions in a Turn During a creature’s turn, the rules distinguish several kinds of actions. Understanding what you can do each round prevents “I don’t know what I’m allowed to do!” moments at the table. Action The action is the primary activity you can perform on your turn. Typical actions include: - Making an attack with a weapon or spell - Casting a spell (if the spell’s casting time is 1 action) - Dashing to double your movement for the turn - Disengaging to avoid provoking reactions when moving away from a foe - Help to give an ally advantage on their next ability check Bonus Action A bonus action is a secondary activity that certain class features, spells, or abilities grant. 6. 6. Role‑Playing and Storytelling

A Moment on the Midnight Road The lanterns of Briarhollow flicker as the party’s wagon creaks to a halt. A lone figure steps from the shadows—a grizzled dwarf merchant named Thorn Ironfist, clutching a sack of precious herbs. He eyes the party, then the night‑filled road ahead, and says, “Evenin’ folk. I’m in a hurry, and I could use a strong arm to guard my cargo. What’s your price?” Your character, Lira the half‑elf bard, feels the weight of a secret promise to a distant patron. How does she respond? Does she flash a charming smile, recite a quick rhyme, or perhaps keep her voice low, hinting at a hidden motive? This single exchange can set the tone for the entire session, illustrating how role‑playing and storytelling are the heartbeat of D&D. --- 1. Expressing Personality, Voice, and Motivations 1.1. What Makes a Character Distinct? Even though Chapter 3 gave you the mechanical skeleton—ability scores, class features, and background—your personality and voice are what make the character feel alive. Think of them as layers added on top of the stats sheet: 1. Core Traits – a handful of adjectives (e.g., brash, meticulous, compassionate) that guide reactions. 2. Speech Patterns – word choice, accent, or catchphrases. 3. Motivations – the “why” behind actions (e.g., prove worth to a disapproving mentor). Tip: Write a short “character blurb” of 3–5 sentences that captures these layers. Keep it handy during play; it’s a quick reference for staying in character. 1.2. Finding Your Voice - Accent or Dialect: You don’t need a perfect British accent, but a consistent flavor (e.g., “I’ll be takin’ that treasure, mate!”) helps others picture your character. - Tempo & Volume: A soft‑spoken wizard may speak slowly, while a boisterous barbarian might shout. - Catchphrases: A simple line like “By the gods!” can become a memorable hallmark. Exercise: Pick one line from a favorite movie or book that fits your character’s tone and practice saying it aloud. Use it when your character is surprised or excited. 1.3. Aligning Actions with Motivations Motivations act as an internal compass. When a dilemma appears—say, a goblin begging for mercy—ask: - What does my character care about most right now? - How would achieving or protecting that goal influence my choice? If Lira’s secret mission is to retrieve a stolen relic for her patron, she may negotiate with the goblin rather than kill it outright, hoping to locate the relic through information. Quick Checklist for Decision‑Making - Goal: What does the character want? - Conflict: What stands in the way? - Personality Filter: How would a brave vs. cautious character act? - Outcome: Does the choice move the story forward? --- 2. The DM’s Triple Hat: Referee, Storyteller, World‑Builder When the first player rolls a natural 20 and the party’s wizard declares, “I’m opening the ancient door,” the person guiding the experience is wearing three distinct caps at once. | Role | What it means in practice | Core duties | |------|--------------------------|-------------| | Referee | Enforces the game’s mechanics and decides what happens when the rules are silent. | • Interprets the 5e Core Rulebooks (PHB, DMG, MM). <br• Calls for rolls, adjudicates results, and keeps the game moving. | | Storyteller | Shapes the narrative, gives NPCs personality, and creates dramatic tension. | • Uses the storytelling foundations from Chapter 6. <br• Provides vivid descriptions, sets scenes, and reacts to player choices. | | World‑Builder | Crafts the geography, cultures, and history that make the adventure feel lived‑in. | • Designs towns, dungeons, and the larger campaign setting. <br• Populates the world with monsters, treasure, and plot hooks. | A new DM can feel overwhelmed by the need to juggle all three, but the good news is that each role leans on tools you already have: the rulebooks for the referee, your imagination (and the storytelling concepts you just learned) for the storyteller, and a few simple techniques for world‑building. --- Getting to Know a Pre‑Written Adventure Module Most beginners start with a ready‑made adventure because it supplies the “big picture” while still letting you practice each of the three hats. 1. The Anatomy of a Module A typical 5e adventure (e.g., the introductory “Lost Mine of Phandelver”) is organized into clear sections: 1. Adventure Overview – A short paragraph that tells you the theme, recommended character level, and tone. 2. Background – Lore the DM should know: who the villains are, why the location matters, and any political or historical hooks. 3. Adventure Hooks – How the PCs are drawn into the story (e.g., a missing merchant, a mysterious map). 4. Locations & Maps – Detailed descriptions of each place the party can explore, often with a hand‑drawn or printed map. 5. Encounters – Stat blocks for monsters, NPC dialogue, and suggested tactics. 6. Treasure & Rewards – Loot tables and experience points. 7. Appendices – Optional rules, new monsters, or extra lore. Knowing where each piece lives lets you flip quickly during play, keeping the game’s flow smooth. 2. Five friends have gathered in a living‑room that’s currently a coffee‑table‑filled jumble of snack wrappers, a stack of board games, and a lone laptop. One of them, Alex, has promised to be the Dungeon Master (DM) for the first time. The air buzzes with excitement, but also a little uncertainty: “Where do we start?” The first game session is the bridge between the rulebooks you’ve just read and the living story you’ll create together. A well‑prepared space, clear expectations, and a smooth opening scene turn that chaotic room into a memorable adventure table. The steps below walk you through that transformation, step by step. --- 1. Knowing where to look saves time, reduces confusion, and keeps the game fun.
