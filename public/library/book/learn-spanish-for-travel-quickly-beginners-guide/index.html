1. Spanish Pronunciation and Greetings

Imagine stepping off a plane in Madrid, Mexico City, or Lima. You navigate to a café, and the barista smiles and says, "¡Buenos días!" You know what they mean, but when you try to reply, the words feel tangled in your mouth. You freeze, smile, and offer a weak "Hola." The barrier isn't your vocabulary. It's your pronunciation. Spanish is a beautifully phonetic language. Unlike English, which is full of silent letters and inconsistent rules, Spanish words are pronounced exactly as they are written. Once you lock in a few foundational sounds, you can confidently read almost any Spanish word aloud—even if you don't know what it means yet. The Foundation: Mastering the Five Spanish Vowels If you only learn one thing about Spanish pronunciation, make it the vowels. In English, vowels are notoriously rebellious. Consider the letter "a" in the words cat, cake, father, and ball. It makes four completely different sounds. In Spanish, a vowel has exactly one sound. It does not change based on the surrounding letters. There are five Spanish vowels: A, E, I, O, U. To pronounce them correctly, keep your mouth and tongue relatively tense and stationary. English speakers tend to glide their vowels, turning "o" into an "o-oo" sound. In Spanish, you hit the vowel and stop. Here is how to pronounce each one: A (ah): Like the "a" in father. Open your mouth wide. Example: Adiós (Goodbye) E (eh): Like the "e" in pet or bet. Keep it short and crisp. Example: España (Spain) I (ee): Like the "ee" in see or machine. Example: Sí (Yes) O (oh): Like the "o" in boat, but without the "oo" glide at the end. Round your lips tightly. Example: Ocho (Eight) U (oo): Like the "oo" in food or moon. Push your lips forward. Example: Usted (You, formal) The Golden Rule of Vowels Because vowels never change their sound, you can read any Spanish word by sounding it out syllable by syllable. Let’s look at the Spanish word for "Mexico": México. In English, we say "MEX-i-co." In Spanish, you pronounce every vowel clearly: MEH-hee-koh. Practice these common travel words aloud, focusing strictly on keeping your vowels pure: Agua (Water): AH-gwah Cerveza (Beer): ser-VEH-sah Vino (Wine): BEE-noh (Note: the 'v' in Spanish often sounds like a soft 'b', which we will cover shortly). 2. Polite Phrases and Quick Vocabulary Wins

The Golden Keys: "Por Favor" and "Gracias" You are standing at a busy market stall in Mexico City. The vendor is slicing fresh mango, and the midday sun is beating down on the crowd. You want a bag of mango slices with chili powder. You could just point and say "Mango." The vendor will probably understand you. But if you say, "Un mango, por favor," something shifts. You are no longer just a tourist pointing at things; you are a guest making a polite request. In Spanish-speaking cultures, courtesy is not just a nice bonus—it is the social glue that holds everyday interactions together. Using basic manners will completely change the way locals interact with you, making them far more patient and helpful. The two most important words you need to master immediately are por favor (please) and gracias (thank you). Por favor literally translates to "for favor." You use it exactly as you would use "please" in English. Agua, por favor. (Water, please.) La cuenta, por favor. (The bill, please.) Gracias means "thank you." If you want to say "thank you very much," you simply add muchas (many) to the front: muchas gracias. When someone says gracias to you, the automatic response is de nada. This phrase roughly translates to "you're welcome," though its literal meaning is "of nothing." You are essentially saying, "It was nothing, don't mention it." Scenario: You buy a bottle of water (agua) from a shop. You hand the cashier the money and say, "Gracias." The cashier hands you your change and replies, "De nada." Navigating Crowded Spaces: Excusing Yourself and Apologizing Travel often means dealing with crowds. Whether you are squeezing onto a packed subway in Madrid or trying to edge past a cluster of tourists blocking the entrance to a museum in Barcelona, you need to know how to politely announce your presence and ask people to move. To say "excuse me" in Spanish, you use the phrase con permiso. Literally, this translates to "with permission." You use it when you need to pass by someone, reach across a table, or enter an elevator. Think of it as the polite way to say "coming through." If you are trying to navigate a dense crowd, a soft, repeated "con permiso, con permiso" will part the sea of people much faster than simply shoving your way through. If you need to get someone's attention—say, a waiter who has just walked past your table or a shop clerk who is stocking shelves—you use disculpe. This translates to "excuse me" or "pardon me" in the sense of "sorry to interrupt." It is highly useful for getting service without seeming rude. Disculpe also doubles as a light apology. …

3. Numbers, Time, and Currency

The Market Stall in Oaxaca You are standing in a bustling market in Oaxaca, México. The sun is high, the air smells of roasted jamón and fresh comida, and you’ve just found the perfect hand-woven bracelet. The vendor looks at you, smiles, and says: "¿A cómo está?" (How much is it?). You want to buy it, but to complete the transaction, you need three skills: the ability to count, the ability to express prices, and the ability to understand what time the market closes. In Spanish, numbers, time, and money are deeply intertwined. By mastering the building blocks of counting, you unlock the ability to navigate schedules, negotiate prices, and handle currency seamlessly. Counting from Zero to One Hundred In Chapter 1, we established the foundational rules for Spanish vowels: A (ah), E (eh), I (ee), O (oh), U (oo). Spanish vowels are crisp and never change their sound. This makes counting in Spanish highly phonetic and straightforward. Once you know the vowels and the consonant sounds—like the trilled r in perro or carro—you can pronounce any number perfectly. Let's start with the fundamental building blocks: zero through fifteen. These numbers are unique words and must be memorized. 0: Cero (seh-roh) 1: Uno (oo-noh) 2: Dos (dohs) 3: Tres (trehs) 4: Cuatro (kwah-troh) 5: Cinco (seen-koh) 6: Seis (says) 7: Siete (syeh-teh) 8: Ocho (oh-choh) 9: Nueve (nweh-beh) 10: Diez (dyehs) 11: Once (ohn-seh) 12: Doce (doh-seh) 13: Trece (treh-seh) 14: Catorce (kah-tohr-seh) 15: Quince (keen-seh) The Pattern from 16 to 100 Starting at 16, Spanish numbers follow a highly logical "ten plus unit" pattern. You take the word for ten (diez) and combine it with the unit number using the letter y (pronounced "ee", meaning "and"). 16: Dieciséis (Diez y seis) 17: Diecisiete (Diez y siete) 18: Dieciocho (Diez y ocho) 19: Diecinueve (Diez y nueve) Notice that diez y contracts slightly into dieci- for a smoother flow. Next come the tens. These are your new base numbers: 20: Veinte (bayn-teh) 30: Treinta (trayn-tah) 40: Cuarenta (kwah-rehn-tah) 50: Cincuenta (seen-kwen-tah) 60: Sesenta (seh-sehn-tah) 70: Setenta (seh-tehn-tah) 80: Ochenta (oh-chehn-tah) 90: Noventa (noh-vehn-tah) 100: Cien (syehn) To form numbers between the tens, you use the word y ("and") between the ten and the unit. 21: Veintiuno (Note: For 21, veinte y uno contracts into a single word: veintiuno) 32: Treinta y dos (trayn-tah ee dohs) 45: Cuarenta y cinco (kwah-rehn-tah ee seen-koh) 58: Cincuenta y ocho (seen-kwen-tah ee oh-choh) 99: Noventa y nueve (noh-vehn-tah ee nweh-beh) Tip: In some Spanish-speaking regions, you might hear speakers drop the y and simply say treinta dos or cuarenta cinco. 4. Core Travel Verbs and Sentence Building

The Engine of Spanish: Verbs You have your pronunciation toolkit, a pocketful of polite phrases, and the ability to handle numbers, time, and currency. But to survive and thrive in a Spanish-speaking environment, you need to start building your own sentences from scratch. To do that, you need an engine. In Spanish, verbs are the engine of a sentence. A verb expresses an action (to eat, to run) or a state of being (to be, to need). When you look up a verb in a Spanish dictionary, you will find it in its infinitive form. An infinitive is the raw, unconjugated version of a verb. In English, infinitives usually include the word "to" (to want, to need, to be). In Spanish, infinitives always end in one of three letters: -ar, -er, or -ir. For example: Necesitar (to need) ends in -ar Ser (to be) ends in -er Venir (to come) ends in -ir To use a verb to talk about yourself or someone else, you have to conjugate it. Conjugation is the process of changing the ending of the verb to match the person doing the action (I, you, he, she, we, they) and the time it happens (present, past, future). In this chapter, we will focus entirely on the present tense. You already know the subject pronouns from previous chapters (yo = I, tú = you, él/ella = he/she, nosotros = we, ellos/ellas = they). Now, let's attach verbs to them. The Two Ways "To Be": Ser and Estar English has one verb for "to be." You can say "I am John," "I am tall," and "I am at the hotel." Spanish has two verbs for "to be," and they are not interchangeable. Understanding which one to use is one of the most important early steps in speaking Spanish correctly. The two verbs are ser and estar. Ser: Identity and Characteristics Use ser to describe the essence of a person or thing. Think of it as the "permanent" or "identity" verb. You use it for: Names and occupations Nationality Time, dates, and origin Inherent characteristics (like physical traits or material) Here is how you conjugate ser in the present tense: yo soy (I am) tú eres (you are) él/ella es (he/she is) nosotros somos (we are) ellos/ellas son (they are) Concrete Scenario: You arrive at a restaurant in Mexico City and the host asks who the reservation is under. You reply: "Soy Sarah." (I am Sarah.) If you are traveling with a friend and want to explain who he is, you say: "Él es mi amigo." (He is my friend.) To say where you are from, you use ser: "Yo soy de Canadá." (I am from Canada.) Estar: Location …

5. Navigation and Transportation

Finding Your Way: Asking for Locations You step out of your hotel lobby into the bright sunshine of a bustling Spanish-speaking city. The smell of fresh jamón and coffee drifts from a nearby café, but you have a more pressing need: you have no idea where the nearest bathroom is, and your train to the next city leaves in an hour. Getting lost is a normal part of traveling, but being unable to ask for help turns a minor detour into a stressful situation. By mastering a few core phrases and directional words, you can confidently navigate airports, train stations, and city streets. In Chapter 2, you learned the magic word puede (can you / could you). In Chapter 4, we built sentences using dónde está (where is). Let’s combine these to hunt down the specific locations you need most. Essential Locations Whether you are looking for a restroom or a boarding gate, you need the right vocabulary. Because Spanish nouns have gender (a concept introduced with words like agua and cerveza in Chapter 2), you will notice these location words end in either -o (masculine) or -a (feminine). El baño (el BAH-nyo): The bathroom El aeropuerto (el ah-eh-roh-PWEHR-toh): The airport La estación de tren (lah es-tah-SYOHN deh trehn): The train station La estación de autobús (lah es-tah-SYOHN deh ow-toh-BOOS): The bus station La parada de autobús (lah pah-RAH-dah deh ow-toh-BOOS): The bus stop La farmacia (lah far-MAH-syah): The pharmacy Pronunciation Note: Remember the vowels from Chapter 1. A (ah), E (eh), I (ee), O (oh), U (oo). The word estación uses an "a" (ah) and an "o" (oh). Also, remember that the letter 'ñ' (as in baño) makes a "ny" sound, like the "ni" in "onion." Asking the Question You can point at your phone, but a spoken question is much more effective. Use these two structures: 1. ¿Dónde está...? (Where is...?) 2. ¿Puede decirme dónde está...? (Can you tell me where... is?) Put them together with your locations: ¿Dónde está el baño? (Where is the bathroom?) ¿Puede decirme dónde está la estación de tren? (Can you tell me where the train station is?) If you are looking for something plural, like the bathrooms or the taxis, change está to están (eh-STAHN). ¿Dónde están los baños? (Where are the bathrooms?) ¿Dónde están los taxis? (Where are the taxis?) 6. Accommodation and Checking In

Your Home Base for the Night You’ve navigated the airport, figured out the metro, and arrived at your accommodation. Now, standing at the reception desk, you just want a key and a bed. But the front desk agent speaks rapidly, and you’re suddenly unsure how to confirm your reservation or ask for the Wi-Fi password. Accommodation interactions follow a highly predictable script. By learning the specific vocabulary for checking in, requesting amenities, and reporting issues, you can bypass the confusion and get to your room quickly. This chapter builds directly on the sentence structures you learned in Core Travel Verbs and Sentence Building and the time concepts from Numbers, Time, and Currency to help you master hotel and hostel interactions. Essential Accommodation Vocabulary Before we dive into full sentences, let’s establish the core nouns you will need at a reception desk. El hotel (ehl oh-TEHL): Hotel El hostal (ehl oh-STAHL): Hostel (Note: In Spanish-speaking countries, a hostal is often a small, budget-friendly guesthouse with private rooms, rather than just dormitory bunks). La toalla (lah toh-AH-yah): Towel El baño (ehl BAH-nyoh): Bathroom Checking In: The First Interaction Checking in requires you to state who you are, confirm your booking details, and provide your passport. You will rely heavily on two of the most critical travel verbs introduced in Chapter 4: Tener (to have) and Ser (to be). Stating Your Reservation When you approach the desk, the receptionist will typically greet you. You can reply using the greetings from Chapter 1, and then state your business. 7. Dining and Ordering Food

Getting Seated and Getting the Menu You’ve just spent the afternoon navigating cobblestone streets and marveling at colonial architecture. Your stomach rumbles, reminding you that it’s time to experience one of the greatest joys of travel: the local cuisine. You spot a charming sidewalk café, the air rich with the scent of roasting garlic and simmering tomatoes. But as you approach the doorway, a wave of hesitation hits you. How do you ask for a table? Will they understand you? Dining out in a Spanish-speaking country doesn’t require fluency. It just requires a handful of specific phrases and a willingness to try. By the end of this section, you will be able to confidently walk into a restaurant, secure a table, and get your hands on the menu. The Universal Icebreaker From your work in Spanish Pronunciation and Greetings, you already know the most important word you need when walking into any establishment: Hola (Hello). When you step into a restaurant, a simple, pleasant Hola is the perfect icebreaker. If you want to sound a bit more formal and polite—especially in nicer establishments—you can use a phrase we introduced in Polite Phrases and Quick Vocabulary Wins: Buenas tardes (Good afternoon) or Buenas noches (Good evening). Requesting a Table In the Spanish-speaking world, a "table" is a mesa (MEH-sah). To ask for a table, you will use one of the most useful verbs in the Spanish language: querer (to want). In Core Travel Verbs and Sentence Building, you learned how to build basic sentences using "I want" (quiero). Let’s put it to work. Quiero una mesa. (I want a table.) Una mesa, por favor. (A table, please.) If you are dining with others, you will need to specify the number of people. You can combine this with your numbers from Numbers, Time, and Currency: Una mesa para dos. (A table for two.) Una mesa para tres. (A table for three.) Una mesa para cuatro. (A table for four.) In many Latin American countries, it’s common to add the word personas (people) to be slightly more polite, though it’s entirely optional: Una mesa para dos personas. (A table for two people.) 8. Shopping and Souvenirs

The Market Experience: Setting the Stage Picture yourself wandering through a bustling open-air market in Oaxaca, México. The air smells of roasted chilies and freshly cut fruit. Stalls overflow with hand-woven textiles, leather goods, and painted ceramics. You spot a beautifully crafted leather wallet you want to take home. In previous chapters, you learned how to navigate towns, order cerveza and comida, and handle the basics of Spanish pronunciation—remembering that the vowels are always crisp: A (ah), E (eh), I (ee), O (oh), U (oo). Now, it is time to arm yourself with the vocabulary needed to buy that wallet, ask for it in a different color, figure out if you can use your credit card, and negotiate a fair price. Shopping in a Spanish-speaking country is a two-part experience. Modern boutiques and chain stores operate much like they do anywhere else: fixed prices, racks of clothing, and card terminals. Open-air markets, however, are a vibrant social dance. Prices can be flexible, cash is king, and a little polite Spanish goes a long way. Asking the Price and Understanding the Response The very first phrase you need when you point to an item is, "How much does this cost?" In Spanish, the word for "how much" changes depending on whether you are asking about one thing or multiple things. ¿Cuánto cuesta? (KWAHN-toh KWES-tah) - How much does it cost? (Use this for a single item). ¿Cuánto cuestan? (KWAHN-toh KWES-tahn) - How much do they cost? (Use this for plural items, like a pair of earrings or a stack of bracelets). You might also hear a vendor use the verb valer (to be worth) instead of costar (to cost). ¿Cuánto vale? (KWAHN-toh BAH-leh) - How much is it worth? / How much is it? ¿Cuánto valen? (KWAHN-toh BAH-lehn) - How much are they? Handling the Numbers When the vendor replies, they will give you a number. In Chapter 3, we covered Numbers, Time, and Currency, so you already have the tools to understand prices. However, market vendors speak quickly. If the price sounds like a blur of syllables, you can always ask them to write it down or show you on a calculator. ¿Puede escribirlo, por favor? (PWEH-deh es-kree-BEER-loh, por fah-VOR) - Can you write it down, please? ¿Me lo muestra en la calculadora? (meh loh MWES-trah en la cahl-kyoo-lah-DOH-rah) - Can you show it to me on the calculator? 9. Health, Emergencies, and Safety

When You Need Help Right Now Your flight landed in Mexico City three days ago. You have successfully navigated customs, found your hotel, ordered comida without accidentally ordering something strange, and bargained for souvenirs using the numbers you learned earlier. You are feeling confident. Then, at 2:00 AM, you wake up with a sharp pain in your stomach and a rising fever. When you are sick or in danger in a foreign country, your brain panics in your native language. You do not need to know how to debate medical theory in Spanish, but you do need to know how to get help, explain where it hurts, and stay safe. This chapter gives you the absolute minimum vocabulary and sentence structures to navigate a health crisis or an emergency. The Universal Emergency Words Before we look at specific symptoms, you need to know the words that stop traffic. If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember these three words. ¡Ayuda! (Ah-YOO-dah) - Help! ¡Emergencia! (eh-mehr-HEN-see-ah) - Emergency! ¡Por favor! (por fah-VOR) - Please! Recall from our chapter on polite phrases that adding "por favor" to the end of any request makes it polite and urgent without sounding demanding. If you are in distress, yelling "¡Ayuda, por favor!" is the fastest way to get a bystander's attention. To state that there is an emergency, use the phrase: Es una emergencia. (Ehs oo-nah eh-mehr-HEN-see-ah) - It is an emergency. Stating Basic Medical Symptoms When you finally find someone to help you, they will likely ask: ¿Qué pasó? (keh pah-SOH) - What happened? or ¿Qué tiene? (keh TYEH-neh) - What do you have? You can respond by pointing to the problem and using the word Dolor (doh-LOR), which means "pain." Expressing Pain In Spanish, to say "I have pain," you use the verb tener (to have), which we covered in our core travel verbs chapter. The structure is: Tengo dolor de + [body part]. Tengo dolor de cabeza. (TEHN-goh doh-LOR deh cah-BEH-sah) - I have a headache. Tengo dolor de estómago. (TEHN-goh doh-LOR deh ehs-TOH-mah-goh) - I have a stomachache. Tengo dolor de garganta. (TEHN-goh doh-LOR deh gahr-GAHN-tah) - I have a sore throat. Tengo dolor de oído. (TEHN-goh doh-LOR deh oh-EE-doh) - I have an earache. 10. Socializing and Small Talk

Breaking the Ice: Names and Origins You have navigated airports, checked into your hotel, ordered cerveza and comida without pointing at the menu, and successfully asked for directions to the nearest pharmacy. You have survived the logistical hurdles of travel. Now, you are sitting at a sidewalk cafe, or waiting at a bus stop, and someone turns to you with a friendly smile. It is time to make a human connection. Socializing in a foreign language does not require fluency. Locals are incredibly forgiving of mistakes when they see you making an effort to speak their language. In fact, attempting small talk is often the key that unlocks a more authentic travel experience, leading to local recommendations, cultural insights, and sometimes even lifelong friendships. To get a basic conversation rolling, you only need two things: to know who you are talking to, and to know where they are from. What’s Your Name? In Chapter 1, you learned the basic daytime and evening greetings (Buenos días, Buenas tardes, Buenas noches). Once the greeting is out of the way, the next logical step is exchanging names. To ask someone their name, you use the phrase: ¿Cómo te llamas? (What is your name?) Let’s break down the mechanics of this sentence. The word cómo means "how". The word te is a reflexive pronoun meaning "yourself", and llamas comes from the verb llamarse, which literally means "to call oneself". So, literally, you are asking "How do you call yourself?" To reply, you simply change the pronoun from te (yourself) to me (myself) and use the same verb: Me llamo... (My name is... / I call myself...) If you want to keep it even simpler, you can use another phrase introduced in Chapter 2 alongside your polite phrases: Soy... (I am...) followed directly by your name. Where Are You From? Once names are exchanged, the most common follow-up question in travel small talk is about origins. People are naturally curious about where visitors come from. To ask someone where they are from, you say: ¿De dónde eres? (Where are you from?) Here is how that breaks down: De means "from". Dónde means "where". Eres is the "you" form of the verb ser (to be), which you started exploring in Chapter 4. To answer, you flip the question around: Soy de... (I am from...) followed by your country or city. Here are a few examples of how this looks in practice: Soy de Estados Unidos. (I am from the United States.) Soy de Canadá. (I am from Canada.) Soy de Inglaterra. (I am from England.)
