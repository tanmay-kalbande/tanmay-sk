1. Introduction to Raising Baby Chicks

Why Raising Baby Chicks at Home Might Surprise You Imagine waking up to the soft peeping of tiny chicks in a box beside your desk. That’s exactly what happened to Maya, a first-time chicken keeper in Portland, Oregon. She’d ordered 10 chicks online after researching backyard chickens, expecting a few weeks of care before moving them outside. Instead, she found herself enchanted by the way they snuggled against each other for warmth, their tiny voices rising in unison when she approached. Within days, she was checking their bedding every few hours and learning to recognize each chick by personality—one boldly pecking at her finger, another hiding shyly in the corner. Maya’s experience isn’t unusual. Raising baby chicks at home is more than a practical step toward fresh eggs or meat; it’s an introduction to the rhythms of life in a way few modern hobbies are. For beginners, it can feel overwhelming at first—suddenly, you’re responsible for fragile creatures that depend entirely on you. Yet, the rewards go beyond the practical. Many people report a deep sense of connection to their food, a newfound appreciation for animal care, and even stress relief from the routine of feeding and observing their chicks. But it’s not all cute peeping and gentle clucks. Chicks are delicate. They need precise temperatures, clean environments, and constant monitoring. A single day without heat can be fatal. And while they grow quickly, the first few weeks demand your time and attention. If you’re used to pets that nap all day or plants that thrive on neglect, chicks will challenge your expectations. So why do people do it? Why choose to raise chicks at home when eggs are readily available at the store? For many, it’s about control—knowing exactly what their food contains and how the animals were treated. For others, it’s the joy of watching life unfold from day one. And for some, it’s simply the curiosity of trying something new. But raising chicks isn’t for everyone. It requires space, time, and a willingness to learn. If you live in a tiny apartment or travel frequently, chicks may not be the right choice. If you dislike mess or the idea of handling live animals makes you nervous, reconsider. Still, if you’re curious, open-minded, and ready to commit to a few weeks of intense care, raising baby chicks can be one of the most rewarding experiences in homesteading or backyard farming. This chapter will help you decide whether it’s right for you by exploring the benefits, challenges, and realities of raising chicks at home. --- The Benefits of Raising Baby Chicks Raising chicks yourself offers several advantages over buying adult chickens or purchasing eggs from a store. 2. Choosing the Right Chicks

Why Your First Flock Starts with the Right Chicks Imagine bringing home a box of fluffy chicks, only to watch half of them struggle to survive the first week. Or worse, realizing too late that they’ll outgrow your coop in six months because you didn’t research their adult size. These aren’t just hypotheticals—they’re real pitfalls for beginner chicken keepers. The difference between a thriving backyard flock and a frustrating experience often comes down to one decision: choosing the right chicks. This chapter will guide you through sourcing healthy chicks, picking breeds that suit your lifestyle, and avoiding common mistakes that can derail your flock before it even begins. By the end, you’ll know exactly where to get chicks, what to look for when you inspect them, and how to ensure you’re starting with the best possible birds for your first year of homegrown eggs. --- Where to Source Healthy Chicks Your chicks’ health starts with where—and how—you get them. There are two main options: local hatcheries or breeders, and mail-order suppliers. Each has trade-offs in cost, convenience, and reliability. Buying Chicks from Local Sources Local options include small-scale breeders (often hobbyists), agricultural suppliers, or family farms selling chicks seasonally. These can be found through: - Online directories like Murray McMurray Hatchery’s breeder locator or The Livestock Conservancy’s breeder directory - Facebook groups or local farming forums (search for “chick sales in [your county/region]”) - Farmers markets or 4-H club events - Feed stores that partner with reputable hatcheries (ask which hatchery they source from) Pros of local purchases: - Immediate pickup – No shipping stress or delays. You can inspect the chicks in person before committing. - Local knowledge – Breeders often share insights about their flock’s temperament, hardiness, and common health issues. - Lower risk of transit stress – Mail-order chicks endure 24–48 hours in transit, which can weaken their immune systems. Cons of local purchases: - Limited breed selection – You’re limited to what the breeder has on hand, which may not include rare or specialty breeds. - Potential for inbreeding – Some small breeders don’t maintain genetic diversity, which can lead to weaker chicks. - Seasonal availability – Not all breeders hatch year-round, so you may have to wait. Red flags to watch for: - Chicks crammed into dirty or overcrowded containers with wet bedding. - A strong ammonia smell (indicates poor ventilation or unclean conditions). - Breeders who can’t answer basic questions about feed, vaccination history, or parent flock health. 3. Setting Up a Brooder for Your Chicks

The First Home for Your Chicks: Building a Brooder That Keeps Them Safe and Warm Imagine this: You’ve just brought home a box of fluffy yellow chicks, their tiny peeps filling your living room like a chorus of tiny alarm clocks. They’re adorable, yes—but they’re also fragile, vulnerable, and entirely dependent on you. Within hours, they’ll need warmth, clean water, and a safe place to rest. That’s where the brooder comes in. A brooder isn’t just a box with bedding—it’s a controlled environment designed to mimic the warmth and safety a mother hen would provide. Without it, chicks can’t regulate their body temperature, leading to stress, illness, or worse. Whether you’ve chosen Easter Eggers, Buff Orpingtons, or Australorps, every chick deserves a proper start. And setting up that first home correctly is your first step toward raising healthy, thriving birds. Let’s build a brooder that works. --- What Is a Brooder—and Why Does It Matter? A brooder is a temporary, controlled environment where baby chicks live during their first few weeks of life. It replaces the warmth and protection a mother hen would naturally provide. While chicks hatch fully feathered in the wild, domesticated chicks often hatch in incubators and need an artificial environment that keeps them safe, warm, and growing. Why is a brooder so essential? Newly hatched chicks: - Cannot regulate their own body temperature effectively - Are highly susceptible to drafts, predators, and temperature fluctuations - Produce a lot of waste quickly, leading to ammonia buildup and respiratory issues - Need a clean, dry space to avoid coccidiosis, a common and dangerous parasitic disease A poorly set-up brooder can lead to chilled chicks, overheating, or even death within hours. On the flip side, a well-designed brooder gives your chicks the best possible start—setting them up for strong growth and long-term health. --- Essential Materials: What You Need Before Day One Before your chicks arrive, gather these must-have supplies. You don’t need everything at once—just what’s needed to keep them alive and safe for the first few days. Core Brooder Components - Brooder Box: This can be almost anything large enough for your chicks to move around but small enough to warm efficiently. Common options: - Large plastic storage bins (30–50 gallon) - Cardboard boxes (lined with plastic for moisture resistance) - Wooden crates or DIY plywood boxes - Old livestock water troughs - Tip: Avoid deep containers—they trap ammonia and make cleaning harder. - Heat Source: Chicks need consistent warmth. 4. Providing Proper Heat and Light for Chicks

Why Chicks Need Heat—and How to Give It to Them Safely A single, fluffy chick tucked under your palm feels like a tiny furnace. Their bodies generate very little heat on their own when they hatch, so they rely on their mother—or, in your case, a carefully managed heat source—to stay warm. Without proper heat, chicks can become sluggish, stop eating, and even die. But too much heat can be just as dangerous: chicks pant, spread their wings, and move away from the source, signaling they are overheated. This chapter will guide you through setting up a safe and responsive heat system for your chicks. You’ll learn why heat is non-negotiable, how to choose and adjust the right equipment, what temperatures to aim for at each stage of growth, and how light—both natural and artificial—supports their health. By the end, you’ll be able to spot distress signals quickly and keep your chicks thriving from day one. --- The Science of Chick Warmth: Why Heat Is Non-Negotiable When chicks hatch, they emerge from a 100°F (38°C) egg into a world that can be 50–70°F (10–21°C) cooler. Their downy feathers offer little insulation, and their internal heat regulation systems aren’t fully developed. That means they cannot self-regulate their body temperature for the first two to three weeks of life. This is why they huddle together for warmth and why a missing heat source can be fatal. Heat does more than prevent chilling—it supports digestion and growth. When chicks are cold, their bodies focus on staying warm instead of absorbing nutrients from food. This can lead to poor weight gain and vulnerability to illness like coccidiosis, a common parasitic disease that thrives in stressed, chilled chicks. Chick Reality Check: A single chick out of a brood of 10 that's constantly hiding in a corner may be too cold. Always observe their behavior, not just the thermometer. --- Heat Sources for Chicks: Choosing What Works Best You have two main options for providing heat in your brooder: 1. Heat lamp with a bulb 2. Heating plate (also called a radiant heater or hover) Each has pros and cons. Let’s break them down. Heat Lamps: The Traditional Choice A heat lamp uses an incandescent bulb (usually 250 watts) mounted in a clamp lamp fixture. It’s inexpensive, easy to install, and widely available. 5. Feeding Your Baby Chicks for Optimal Growth

Why Your Chick’s First Meal Sets the Stage for a Lifetime Imagine this: you’ve just brought home a box of peeping, downy chicks, their tiny legs wobbling on the ride home. You’ve set up the brooder with the right heat lamp, a soft bedding layer, and a waterer waiting at the perfect height. As you watch them explore their new world, one question burns in your mind: What should I feed these little flakes of life to help them grow strong and healthy? The answer isn’t as simple as tossing them some kitchen scraps or the same feed you use for your adult hens. Baby chicks have special nutritional needs that differ dramatically from adult chickens. Feed them the wrong thing—even something that seems harmless—and you could stunt their growth, weaken their immune systems, or even introduce deadly diseases like coccidiosis. In this chapter, you’ll learn exactly what to feed your chicks, when to feed it, and how to avoid the most common mistakes that new chicken keepers make. By the end, you’ll be confident in providing the right nutrition from day one—so your chicks don’t just survive, but thrive. --- The Unique Nutritional Needs of Baby Chicks Chicks grow at an astonishing rate—from a 2-ounce hatchling to a nearly full-sized pullet in just six weeks. To fuel this rapid development, they need a diet that’s far richer in protein, vitamins, and minerals than what adult chickens require. Protein: The Building Block of Growth Baby chicks need high-protein feed—typically 20–24% protein—to support muscle and feather development. Adult chickens, by contrast, thrive on 16–18% protein. Without enough protein in their diet, chicks may develop slowly, have weak feathers, or even suffer from metabolic bone disease, where their bodies can’t support their rapid growth. - Animal-based proteins (like fish meal or poultry by-products) are often used in commercial feed because they provide the complete amino acid profile chicks need. - Plant-based proteins (soybean meal, corn gluten) can be used, but may not offer all the essential amino acids without supplementation. Key Point: Never feed adult layer feed to chicks. It’s too low in protein and can lead to permanent developmental issues. Essential Vitamins and Minerals Chicks require higher levels of certain nutrients than adult chickens, including: - Vitamin D: Crucial for calcium absorption, which prevents rickets (soft, weak bones). Chicks can’t synthesize vitamin D from sunlight like adult chickens can until they’re fully feathered. - Vitamin E: Supports immune function and muscle development. Deficiency can lead to encephalomalacia, a neurological disorder. - Omega-3 fatty acids: Important for brain development and overall health. Spot the Problem Before It’s Too Late The first time Maya noticed something was wrong, she thought her chicks were just napping. Three of the five Buff Orpingtons were lying flat, eyes half-closed, feathers puffed up like tiny feather dusters. Their waterer was still full, but the chicks weren’t interested. Maya reached out to gently pick one up, and it felt cold and limp in her palm. She had read about Coccidiosis in Choosing the Right Chicks, but she hadn’t connected the list of symptoms to what she was seeing now. That evening, she rushed to her local feed store and bought an anticoccidial medication. Within 48 hours, three chicks were gone. Maya learned the hard way that baby chicks can’t always tell you they’re sick—and by the time they look obviously ill, it’s often too late. But she also learned that with a little knowledge and daily attention, most problems are preventable. This chapter is about seeing what’s coming before it arrives. It’s about keeping your chicks healthy, not just treating them when they’re sick. You’ll learn to spot early warning signs, prevent common diseases, and create a brooder that keeps pests and pathogens out—so your chicks don’t end up like Maya’s. --- Common Health Problems in Baby Chicks: Know the Signs Baby chicks are fragile. Their immune systems are still developing, and they’re exposed to more germs than adult chickens. Even in a clean brooder, common health issues can appear quickly. Knowing what to look for can help you act fast—or better yet, prevent the problem in the first place. Below are the most frequent health problems in chicks, their symptoms, and what they might mean. 1. Coccidiosis What it is: A parasitic disease caused by tiny oocysts (egg-like parasite forms) that damage the chick’s gut lining. It spreads through contaminated droppings and wet litter. Symptoms: - Watery or bloody diarrhea - Lethargy and weakness - Ruffled feathers - Pale combs - Sudden weight loss despite normal eating - Crowding near the heat source Why it happens: Coccidiosis thrives in damp, dirty brooders. Chicks ingest oocysts from feces-contaminated bedding or feeders. 7. Handling and Socializing Your Chicks

The Bond Begins: Why Socialization Matters Imagine a few months from now: you walk into your coop, and instead of a flock of panicked birds scattering in every direction, your hens trot toward you, chirping with excitement, eager for a treat. This isn't luck; it's the result of the work you do during the first few weeks of their lives. Socialization is the process of teaching your chicks that humans are a source of safety, food, and comfort rather than predators. Because chicks are prey animals, their natural instinct is "flight"—to run away from anything larger than they are. If you leave them alone in the brooder without interaction, they will grow up fearful of you. This makes essential tasks, such as health checks for Coccidiosis or treating a wing injury (as discussed in Keeping Your Chicks Healthy and Safe), a stressful struggle for both you and the bird. By investing time in handling your chicks now, you are building a foundation of trust. A tamed chicken is easier to manage, less likely to stress-peck its flockmates, and far more rewarding as a pet. How to Safely Pick Up and Hold a Chick Before you reach into the brooder, remember that chicks are fragile. Their bones are soft, and their internal organs are not yet fully protected by a sturdy ribcage. A sudden squeeze or a clumsy drop can cause permanent injury. The Approach Never dive into the brooder from above with fast movements. In the wild, hawks and owls attack from above. If you swoop down quickly, you are mimicking a predator, which will trigger a panic response. 1. Get on their level: Squat or sit next to the brooder. 2. Move slowly: Slide your hand in gently. 3. Use a soft voice: Talk or hum quietly so they associate your voice with a calm environment. The "Scoop and Secure" Technique Avoid grabbing a chick by the wings or picking them up by the top of the head. Instead, use the scoop method: 1. The Scoop: Gently slide your hand under the chick's breast and belly. 2. The Lift: Lift them upward and immediately tuck them against your body or cup them in both hands. 3. The Hold: Hold the chick securely but loosely. Your palm should support their weight, and your fingers should gently wrap around their sides—never squeezing the chest. Pro Tip: If a chick is particularly flighty, you can gently place your hand over its back to "still" it for a second before scooping it up. 8. Transitioning Chicks to Outdoor Coop Life

Is Your Chick Ready for the Great Outdoors? Imagine your chicks are huddled under their heat source in the brooder. They’ve grown from fluffy yellow balls into awkward, "teenage" birds with a mix of downy fluff and emerging adult feathers. They are more active, more curious, and perhaps a bit more chaotic. You might be wondering: Can they go outside today? Will they freeze the moment they hit the air? Moving chicks from the controlled environment of a brooder to a permanent outdoor coop is one of the most critical phases of poultry raising. If you move them too early, they risk hypothermia or predation; move them too late, and they become stunted or overly dependent on artificial heat. The transition isn't a single event—it's a process of "hardening off" that allows their bodies to adapt to the fluctuating temperatures and elements of the natural world. The "Fully Feathered" Rule The most reliable indicator that a chick is ready to leave the brooder is their plumage. You are looking for full feathering. When chicks first hatch, they are covered in down (the soft, fuzzy fluff). Down provides some warmth, but it is not waterproof and does not trap heat efficiently. As they grow, they develop true feathers. Your chicks are ready for the coop when: The "Fuzz" is Gone: They no longer have patches of down on their backs, wings, or tails. The Comb and Wattles are Visible: While not a temperature indicator, the development of the comb (the fleshy crest on top of the head) and wattles (the flaps of skin under the beak) usually coincides with the growth of adult feathers. They Are Ignoring the Heat Source: If you notice your chicks spending most of their time away from the heat lamp or radiant heater, it means their internal thermoregulation (the ability to maintain their own body temperature) is functioning. The Temperature Test Feathers are the primary requirement, but the ambient temperature of your region plays a massive role. Warm Climates: In summer or tropical regions, fully feathered chicks (usually around 4–6 weeks) can often transition directly to the coop. Cold Climates: In autumn or winter, "fully feathered" isn't always enough. If the nighttime temperature drops below 40°F (4°C), young chicks may still need a supplemental heat source in the coop for a few weeks to prevent chilling. --- Preparing the Permanent Home You wouldn't move into a house without checking the locks and turning on the water; your chicks deserve the same preparation. 9. Raising Chicks with Other Animals

The "Cute" Factor vs. The Predator Instinct Imagine this: You’ve spent weeks carefully managing your brooder, and your chicks are now fluffy, curious, and full of personality. You decide it’s time for your friendly Golden Retriever or your calm house cat to meet the new additions. You place a chick on the floor, expecting a gentle sniff and a little curiosity. Suddenly, the dog’s head tilts, the eyes widen, and a "playful" paw swat sends the chick flying across the room. In an instant, a moment meant for bonding becomes a crisis. The fundamental challenge of raising chicks with other animals is the conflict between domestication and instinct. Even the most loving pet has a biological blueprint that identifies small, fluttering, chirping animals as "prey." To a cat or dog, a chick isn't a new friend; it is a living toy or a snack. Understanding this distinction is the first step in keeping your flock safe. Assessing the Risk: Who is Safe and Who Isn't? Before introducing any animal to your chicks, you must categorize the potential interaction. Not all animals pose the same type of threat. High-Risk Animals (Predators) These animals have a natural hunting instinct. While individual personalities vary, the biological drive to hunt is often stronger than their training. Cats: Cats are ambush predators. Even if they don't intend to kill, their claws can cause deep lacerations or fatal infections in a chick's thin skin. Dogs: Some dogs are gentle, but many have a high prey drive (the instinctive urge to pursue and capture prey). A "playful" nip that wouldn't hurt a human can be fatal to a chick. Rats and Snakes: As discussed in Keeping Your Chicks Healthy and Safe, these are opportunistic predators that will actively seek out and kill chicks. They should never have access to your chicks. Moderate-Risk Animals (The Pecking Order) These are animals that may not want to "eat" the chicks, but can cause injury through territoriality or sheer size. Adult Poultry: Your existing hens are the most likely source of social stress. They follow a strict pecking order—a social hierarchy where dominant birds establish their rank by pecking subordinate birds. Baby chicks are at the bottom of this order. Ducks and Geese: Generally more tolerant than chickens, but their size and strength can lead to accidental injuries. Low-Risk Animals Rabbits and Guinea Pigs: Generally peaceful, though they may compete for food or be startled by the chicks' erratic movements. Horses and Goats: Due to their size, the risk is usually accidental (stepping on a chick). However, they rarely view chicks as prey. 10. Common Problems and Troubleshooting

When Things Go Wrong: The First Sign of Trouble Imagine you walk into your brooder on a Tuesday morning. Most of your chicks are chirping loudly and racing toward your hand for treats, but one chick is tucked away in a far corner. Its eyes are half-closed, its feathers are puffed out like a tennis ball, and it isn't interested in the feeder. For a first-time raiser, this is a heart-sinking moment. You might wonder: Is it just tired? Is it being bullied? Or is it seriously ill? Because chicks are prey animals, they are masters of disguise. In the wild, showing weakness makes a bird a target for predators. Consequently, by the time a chick looks sick to a human observer, the problem has often been progressing for a few days. Learning to spot the subtle "red flags" and knowing how to respond can be the difference between a quick recovery and the loss of a pet. Diagnosing Common Health Issues To troubleshoot your chicks' health, you must first establish a "baseline" of what normal behavior looks like. A healthy chick is curious, active, has bright eyes, and spends a significant portion of its day eating, drinking, and dust-bathing. When a chick deviates from this baseline, look for these specific symptoms to help diagnose the cause. Lethargy and Depression Lethargy refers to a state of tiredness, sluggishness, or a lack of energy. While chicks do nap, a lethargic chick will remain stationary even when food is offered or when other chicks are playing. What to look for: "Huddled" posture (sitting in a ball), drooping wings, and a lack of response to external stimuli. Common Causes: This is a general symptom rather than a specific disease. It could be caused by chilling (not enough heat from the heat source), dehydration, or an underlying infection. Digestive Issues and Diarrhea Because chicks eat a high-protein diet and grow rapidly, their digestive tracts are sensitive. Standard Droppings: Healthy chick droppings are generally brown or green and firm. Diarrhea: This appears as watery, loose stools. While occasional loose droppings can happen during a diet change, persistent diarrhea is a warning sign. Bloody Stool: If you see blood in the droppings, this is a classic sign of Coccidiosis, which we touched upon in Keeping Your Chicks Healthy and Safe. This requires immediate intervention. Pasty Butt: This occurs when droppings dry and harden over the chick's vent (the opening where waste leaves the body). This creates a seal that prevents the chick from pooping, which can be fatal if the waste backs up into the intestines. 11. Legalities and Ethical Considerations

The "Neighborly" Surprise: A Reality Check Imagine this: You’ve spent weeks meticulously setting up your brooder, your Buff Orpingtons are thriving, and you’ve successfully transitioned them to their outdoor coop. You are enjoying the peace of your backyard when a knock comes at the door. It’s your neighbor, and they aren't there to admire your flock—they are there to complain about the noise, the smell, or the fact that your chickens have discovered their prize-winning petunias. For many beginner keepers, the "excitement phase" of getting chicks overrides the "research phase" of checking the rules. While raising chickens feels like a return to a simpler way of life, in the eyes of the law, chickens are often categorized as livestock. This means that whether you live in a rural area or a suburban cul-de-sac, there are rules governing how you keep them. Ignoring these rules doesn't just risk a fine; it can lead to forced removal of your birds or strained relationships with the people living next door. Navigating Local Laws and Ordinances Before the first chick arrives, you must understand the legal framework of your specific location. Laws vary wildly—what is legal in one zip code may be a misdemeanor in the next. Understanding Zoning Laws Zoning laws are the rules established by local governments to dictate how land in specific areas can be used (e.g., residential, commercial, or agricultural). In many residential zones, chickens are viewed differently depending on their "utility." Some cities allow "backyard hens" for eggs but strictly prohibit roosters due to noise ordinances. If you purchased an "Easter Egger" or "Ameraucana" without knowing the sex, you might find yourself with a rooster in a zone where only hens are permitted. Common Poultry Ordinances While every city is different, most poultry ordinances focus on these four areas: 1. Flock Size Limits: Many municipalities cap the number of birds you can own (e.g., a maximum of 4 or 6 hens). 2. Setback Requirements: This refers to the minimum distance the coop must be from property lines, neighboring houses, or main roads. For example, a law might state the coop must be 20 feet from any neighbor's dwelling. 3. Containment Rules: Laws often require that birds be securely confined to a coop or run to prevent them from roaming freely in the neighborhood. 4. Waste Management: Some areas have specific rules about how manure is stored or disposed of to prevent runoff and odor. Where to Find Your Local Regulations Don't rely on "what the neighbor says," as they may be breaking the rules themselves. 12. Preparing for Your Chicks' First Laying Season

The Magic Moment: When Your Pullet Becomes a Hen Imagine waking up on a crisp Tuesday morning. You head out to the coop, expecting the usual greeting of excited clucking and scratching. But as you glance into the nesting box, you see it: a small, perfect, pale-colored egg. For a first-time chicken keeper, this is the "payoff" moment. After weeks of managing the brooder, monitoring for Coccidiosis, and carefully managing the transition to outdoor coop life, your chick has officially transitioned into a productive member of the flock. However, the jump from a growing teenager to an egg-laying adult isn't an overnight switch. It is a biological transition that requires specific nutritional shifts and environmental adjustments to ensure your hens stay healthy and productive. The Laying Timeline: When to Expect Eggs Before you start checking the nesting boxes every hour, it helps to understand the biological clock of a chicken. A young female chicken is called a pullet. Once she begins laying eggs, she is referred to as a hen. While most breeds begin laying around 18 to 24 weeks of age, this timeline varies significantly based on the breed you chose (refer back to Choosing the Right Chicks). Breed Variations Early Layers: Some production-focused breeds, like Australorps, may surprise you with eggs as early as 16–18 weeks. Average Layers: Many common breeds, including Buff Orpingtons, typically land in the 20–22 week range. Late Bloomers: Some heritage breeds or larger birds may take up to 28 weeks or longer to reach maturity. Factors That Influence Timing It isn't just genetics that dictate when the first egg appears. Two primary external factors play a role: 1. Daylight Hours: Chickens are triggered to lay by the length of the day. When the pineal gland in their brain senses longer daylight hours (usually in spring), it triggers the hormonal shift required for egg production. If your pullets reach 20 weeks in the dead of winter, they may not start laying until the days lengthen. 2. Overall Health: A bird that has struggled with illness or poor nutrition during its growth phase may experience a delay in maturity. Preparing Your Flock for the First Lay You cannot simply wait for the first egg to appear; you must prepare the hen's body and her environment in advance. If you wait until she is already laying to provide the right nutrients, you risk health complications.
