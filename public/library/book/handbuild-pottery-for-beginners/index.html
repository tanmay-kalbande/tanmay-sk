1. 1. Introduction to Hand-Build Pottery

A Hands‑On Beginning: Imagine Your First Cup You’ve just unwrapped a lump of soft, cool clay from the store. It feels a little like dough, a little like wet sand, and you can already picture a tiny cup forming in your hands. No wheel, no complicated setup—just your fingertips, a few simple tools, and a bit of imagination. Within a few hours you’ll have a hollow vessel that, after a quick fire, can hold a steaming mug of tea. This is the power of hand‑building: the most direct, tactile route into pottery, perfect for anyone who wants to start creating functional art right away. What Is Hand‑Building? Hand‑building is the process of shaping clay into three‑dimensional objects using only the hands and basic tools, without the aid of a potter’s wheel. The three classic techniques—pinch, coil, and slab—are the building blocks of virtually every hand‑built piece: | Technique | How It Works | Typical Uses | |-----------|--------------|--------------| | Pinch | A ball of clay is pressed and pinched to thin walls and shape a form. | Small bowls, cups, ornaments. | | Coil | Long, rolled “snakes” of clay are stacked and blended to create walls. | Vases, pots, larger containers. | | Slab | Flat sheets are rolled out, cut, and joined together. | Boxes, tiles, architectural elements. | Other methods—such as press molding, template building, or sculpting—are extensions of these fundamentals, but the core idea stays the same: the clay is manipulated directly with the hands, and the shape emerges from manual construction rather than centrifugal force. Key Vocabulary (First Appearance) - Clay body – The specific mixture of raw materials (clay, water, additives) that makes up the workpiece. - Bisque – The first firing of a piece, turning raw clay into a porous, hard material ready for glazing. - Glaze – A liquid suspension of silica, fluxes, and colorants that melts into a glassy coating during a second firing. - Firing – The process of heating a piece in a kiln to transform the clay and/or glaze into their final, durable forms. A Brief History of Hand‑Building Hand‑building predates the invention of the potter’s wheel by thousands of years. Early humans fashioned containers from clay using simple tools—sticks, stones, and even their own fingers. Archaeological sites across the world, from the Neolithic settlements of the Near East to Mesoamerican pottery villages, have yielded hand‑built shards that reveal the technique’s long heritage. - Pre‑wheel era (c. 25,000 – 4,000 BCE) – All pottery was hand‑built, primarily using coil and slab methods. - Wheel introduction (c. 3,500 BCE) – The potter’s wheel spread across the Near East and later to Europe and Asia, offering faster production of symmetrical vessels. …

2. 2. Clay Types, Bodies, and Preparation

From a Fresh Lump to a Ready‑to‑Build Clay Body Imagine you have just unpacked a 20‑lb sack of “red clay” from the local art supply store. The material feels dry and crumbly in the bag, yet the next page of your notebook shows a beautiful coil vase you want to throw together tonight. How do you turn that lump into a smooth, pliable medium that will hold its shape while you pinch, coil, and slab? The answer lies in understanding clay types, how their inherent properties affect hand‑building, and how to condition the clay so it behaves the way you need it to. Below we will: 1. Compare the three most common clay bodies—earthenware, stoneware, and porcelain—so you can choose the right one for your project. 2. Show how to test and adjust plasticity, the “feel‑good” quality that lets the clay be stretched, rolled, or molded without cracking. 3. Introduce common additives (grog, ball clay, fluxes, etc.) and explain when each is useful. --- 1. The Three Main Clay Bodies | Clay Body | Typical Color | Firing Range (°F / °C) | Common Uses | Key Characteristics | |-----------|---------------|------------------------|-------------|----------------------| | Earthenware | Reddish, brown, or buff | 1,800–2,100 °F / 980–1,150 °C | Functional tableware, low‑fire decorative pieces | Very plastic, easy to work, porous after firing (needs glaze), expands and contracts more than stoneware. | | Stoneware | Gray, tan, or brown; may turn white after high fire | 2,200–2,400 °F / 1,200–1,300 °C | Dinnerware, mugs, outdoor ceramics, sculpture | Less plastic than earthenware, vitrifies (becomes glass‑like) at high fire, strong and water‑tight even without glaze. | | Porcelain | White or off‑white; sometimes pinkish | 2,300–2,600 °F / 1,260–1,430 °C | Fine dinnerware, delicate art objects, translucent pieces | Very low plasticity, fine particle size, highly vitrified, may crack if not handled carefully. | Why the Differences Matter for Hand‑Building - Plasticity (the ability to be shaped without cracking) is highest in earthenware, moderate in stoneware, and lowest in porcelain. If you are just starting out, earthenware is the most forgiving for coil and slab work. - Firing temperature determines how much shrinkage you can expect. Stoneware and porcelain shrink more than earthenware, so plan for a slightly smaller final size. - Porosity after bisque firing (the first firing) affects whether a piece can stand on its own or needs glaze for waterproofing. Earthenware is porous, stoneware is less so, and porcelain is essentially non‑porous when fully vitrified. Quick tip: If you are unsure which body to buy, start with a mid‑range stoneware. It offers a balance of workability and durability, and many studios sell “all‑purpose” stoneware that will fire in both low and high ranges. …

4. 4. Core Hand-Build Techniques

From Clay to the Kitchen Counter: A Beginner’s First Bowl Imagine you’ve just unpacked a bag of stone‑ware clay, your work surface is set up as described in Chapter 3, and the scent of fresh earth fills the room. You reach for a simple, everyday object—a tiny ceramic bowl to hold fresh herbs while you cook. No wheel, no templates—just your hands, a few basic tools, and three timeless hand‑building methods. By the end of this chapter you will have created that bowl, learned how to join coils into a sturdy pot, and produced flat slabs that lock together like puzzle pieces. --- 1. The Pinch Bowl – Shaping with a Single Hand The pinch technique is the most intuitive way to turn a lump of clay into a functional form. It relies on the natural strength of your fingers and the plasticity of the clay you prepared in Chapter 2. 1.1 Preparing the Clay Chunk 1. Cut a manageable piece – Using a rib or a bench knife, cut a ball roughly the size of a tennis ball (about 150 g for a small bowl). 2. Wedge lightly – Give the ball a few gentle kneads to eliminate any trapped air pockets that could cause cracking later. Tip: If the clay feels too dry, cover it with a damp cloth for a few minutes; if it’s too sticky, sprinkle a thin layer of dry powdered clay and knead again. 1.2 Forming the Base 1. Center the ball on your work surface. 2. Press the thumb into the centre, creating a small indentation. 3. Rotate the ball slowly with the other hand, using the thumb to pinch the surrounding wall outward. Key point: Keep the walls even in thickness (about 5–7 mm). Uneven walls can lead to weak spots during drying or firing. 1.3 Shaping the Bowl 1. Raise the rim – With each rotation, gently pull the wall upward, maintaining a smooth, continuous motion. 2. Check symmetry – Periodically place the bowl on a rotating turntable (or simply spin it by hand) to ensure the rim stays even. 1.4 Refining the Form - Smooth the interior with a damp sponge or a soft finger. - Trim excess from the rim using a wire cutter or a pottery knife if the edge is uneven. 1.5 Common Pinch Pitfalls & Fixes | Issue | Likely Cause | Quick Fix | |-------|--------------|-----------| | Cracks near the rim | Walls too thin or uneven drying | Reinforce by adding a thin coil (see Chapter 1.2) and slip‑join | | Warped bowl | Uneven drying or uneven wall thickness | Re‑wet the outside, reshape gently, and allow to dry evenly covered with a plastic …

5. 5. Building Simple Forms

From Sketch to Sip: Planning a Small Mug Imagine you are sitting at a kitchen table, a freshly‑sanded mug in your hand, steam curling from a steaming cup of tea. The mug didn’t emerge from a factory—it was built by you, using only coils, slabs, and a pinch‑built bowl as its interior. Before the first coil is rolled, the success of the piece begins with a simple plan. 1. Define the function – a mug must hold liquid, be comfortable to grip, and have a stable base. 2. Choose a scale – for a beginner’s mug, a capacity of 250 ml (≈ 8 oz) works well; the overall height is roughly 10 cm, and the mouth diameter about 7 cm. 3. Sketch the profile – draw a side view showing the wall thickness (≈ 6 mm), the height of the handle, and the slope of the side walls. A quick pencil sketch clarifies where the coil will start, where the slab will form the base, and how the handle will attach. Tip: Keep the sketch loose; you’ll adjust dimensions as the clay behaves. The sketch serves as a visual checklist rather than a strict blueprint. Coil‑and‑Slab Construction of the Mug Body The mug’s body combines two of the core hand‑building techniques introduced earlier: coil building for the walls and slab building for the base. This hybrid method gives you control over wall uniformity while providing a solid, flat bottom. 1. Preparing the Coils 1. Roll even coils – using the Primary Tool (the rolling pin) from Chapter 3, roll a coil of clay 6 mm in diameter. 2. Measure coil length – for a 10 cm‑high mug, you’ll need roughly 15–16 coils, each about 2 cm long, stacked to reach the desired height. 3. Score and slip – lightly score the top of each coil and apply slip (a thin slurry of clay and water) before adding the next coil. This creates a chemical bond that prevents separation during drying. 2. Forming the Wall 1. Lay the first coil – shape it into a gentle “C” on the work surface, aligning the ends to form a closed circle. 2. Stack and blend – continue stacking coils, scoring and slipping each layer. After the third coil, begin smoothing the interior with a damp finger or a rib tool, gradually blending the seams into a uniform wall. 3. Now picture pressing a fresh‑cut leaf onto its side, watching the delicate veins imprint a natural map, then adding a thin line of bright orange slip that follows the curve of the leaf’s edge. The vase instantly becomes a narrative of place, season, and personal touch. This is the power of surface decoration and texture—simple techniques that transform a functional object into a work that speaks. In the sections that follow, we will explore three core ways to add visual interest to hand‑built pottery: 1. Carving patterns into leather‑hard clay – using primary tools to cut, incise, and sculpt surface designs. 2. Applying slip and underglaze – introducing color accents that can be brushed, splattered, or painted. 3. 7. 7. Drying, Trimming, and Preparing for Firing

A Pottery Problem in Real Time You’ve just finished a coil‑built vase that looks exactly like the photograph in Chapter 5. You set it on the shelf, step back, and admire the smooth surface you achieved with the slip‑brush technique from Chapter 6. Two days later, a thin crack appears across the rim, and the base feels soft when you press it. What went wrong? The answer lies in the three critical stages of drying, the timing of the trim, and how the piece is packed for the upcoming bisque firing. This chapter walks you through each stage, shows you how to trim confidently, and details a simple system for labeling and packaging your work so it arrives at the kiln in perfect condition. --- 1. The Three Drying Stages When clay leaves the workbench it is green—a term that does not refer to its color but to its “raw” condition. As water evaporates, the clay passes through two intermediate states before it is ready for the kiln. | Stage | What it looks/feels like | Typical time (depends on size & environment) | |-------|--------------------------|----------------------------------------------| | Green | Very soft, pliable, easily deforms under its own weight. A gentle press leaves an indentation that never fully recovers. | Hours to a couple of days for thin slabs; several days for thicker forms. | | Leather‑hard | Firm enough to hold its shape, but still slightly damp. You can press a fingerprint into the surface and it will retain the impression, yet the clay won’t collapse. | 1 – 3 days after the green stage for most hand‑built pieces. | | Bone‑dry | Completely dry to the touch; surface feels cool and non‑porous. When tapped, it produces a clear, crisp “clack.” | Usually 3 – 7 days total, but can be longer for large or thick pieces. | How to Test Each Stage 1. Touch test – Lightly press a fingertip on an inconspicuous area. - Green: Clay gives way and leaves a lasting dent. - Leather‑hard: Dent remains but the surface feels firm. - Bone‑dry: No dent; the clay feels hard and dry. 2. Sound test – Gently tap the piece with a wooden tool. - Green: Dull thud, no clear note. - Leather‑hard: Slightly muted note. - Bone‑dry: Sharp, resonant “clack.” 3. Weight test – For larger pieces, lift the work. If it feels noticeably heavier than expected, it is likely still in the green or early leather‑hard stage. 8. 8. Bisque Firing and Basic Glazing

A First Look at the Kiln: From Soft Clay to Solid Pot Imagine you have just finished a small, coil‑built bowl. It is completely dry, the coils are smooth, and the surface texture you added in Chapter 6 looks exactly as you intended. The next step—turning that fragile, porous form into a sturdy “greenware” that can survive handling and glaze—requires the kiln’s heat. This is where bisque firing begins the transformation, and shortly after, glazing will add color and waterproofing. --- Why Bisque? The Purpose of the First Firing A bisque (or “biscuit”) firing is the initial heat treatment for any hand‑built piece. Its primary functions are: Drive off moisture and organic material – all remaining water and any combustible binders evaporate, preventing explosive steam explosions later. Strengthen the clay body – the particles sinter together, turning the soft, fragile greenware into a hard, porous ceramic that can be handled without breaking. Create a porous surface – the tiny network of pores left after the first firing allows a glaze to adhere evenly during the second firing. Stabilize shape – most warping and shrinkage occur during bisque, so any dimensional changes are taken care of before glaze is applied. Because the bisque stage removes the “weak link” of residual water, it protects both the piece and the kiln during the higher‑temperature glaze firing that follows. --- Typical Temperature Ranges for Bisque The exact temperature depends on the clay body you chose in Chapter 2 (e.g., stoneware vs. earthenware). A practical guide for beginners: | Clay Type | Common Bisque Temperature | Cone Equivalent | |-----------|---------------------------|------------------| | Earthenware (low‑fire) | 1,800 °F – 1,950 °F (982 °C – 1065 °C) | 06 – 04 | | Stoneware (mid‑fire) | 2,100 °F – 2,300 °F (1150 °C – 1260 °C) | 04 – 06 | | Porcelain (high‑fire) | 2,300 °F – 2,500 °F (1260 °C – 1370 °C) | 06 – 10 | \Cone numbers are a standard ceramic scale indicating the amount of heat absorbed; a higher cone means a higher temperature. For most beginner hand‑builders using a versatile stoneware body, aim for 2,200 °F (≈ 1200 °C), which corresponds to Cone 06. This temperature is hot enough to vitrify the body sufficiently for glaze adhesion while staying within the safe operating range of most electric kilns. --- Preparing Your Pieces for Bisque 1. 9. 9. Troubleshooting and Next Steps

A Real‑World Puzzle: The Cracked Bowl Maria has just finished her first hand‑built bowl using the coil method. She let it dry slowly, bisque‑fired it, and applied a glossy glaze. When she opened the kiln, the bowl was split cleanly down the middle, leaving a jagged edge that ruined the piece. She asks, “Why did this happen and how can I stop it?” This scenario illustrates three of the most common problems beginners encounter: cracking, warping, and glaze runs. The following sections walk you through diagnosing what went wrong, adjusting your process, and planning future projects with confidence. --- 1.
