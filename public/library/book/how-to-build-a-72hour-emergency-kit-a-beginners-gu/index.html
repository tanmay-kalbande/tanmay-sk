1. Introduction to Emergency Preparedness

The Moment of Impact Imagine you are sitting in your living room on a Tuesday evening. Suddenly, the power cuts out. Within minutes, your phone loses signal. A loud siren begins to wail outside, and a local emergency alert flashes on your battery-powered radio: “Evacuate immediately. A flash flood warning is in effect for your area. Leave now.” In this moment, your brain enters a state of high stress. Your heart rate climbs, and your ability to think logically decreases. You have perhaps ten minutes to leave your home. Do you spend those ten minutes frantically searching for your passport, wondering where the flashlights are, or trying to remember if you have any bottled water? Or do you simply grab a pre-packed bag from the closet and walk out the door? The difference between these two experiences is Emergency Preparedness. Preparedness is not about predicting the future or living in fear; it is about reducing the "cognitive load" (the mental effort required to make decisions) during a crisis. By establishing a system now, you ensure that when a disaster strikes, your survival is determined by your preparation, not by your ability to remember where you stored the batteries under pressure. Understanding the 72-Hour Rule When people begin preparing for emergencies, the first question they usually ask is: "How much stuff do I need?" The industry standard answer is based on the 72-Hour Rule. The 72-Hour Rule is the principle that an individual or household should be completely self-sufficient for a minimum of three days (72 hours) following a disaster. This means having enough food, water, medication, and shelter tools to survive without any external assistance from the government, utility companies, or neighbors. Why Three Days? You might wonder why the benchmark is 72 hours. Why not 24 hours, or two weeks? The three-day window is based on the logistical reality of Emergency Response. In the immediate aftermath of a large-scale disaster—such as an earthquake, hurricane, or major grid failure—emergency services (police, fire, and EMS) are often overwhelmed. They must prioritize "life-safety" calls, such as rescuing people trapped in rubble or extinguishing active fires. The 72-hour window accounts for three critical phases of disaster recovery: 1. The Chaos Phase (0–24 Hours): Communication networks are often down, roads may be blocked by debris, and first responders are triaging the most critical emergencies. 2. The Mobilization Phase (24–48 Hours): Government agencies (like FEMA in the US or similar global bodies) begin deploying resources, setting up staging areas, and establishing supply chains for food and water. 3. 2. Risk Assessment and Customization

The "One Size Fits None" Problem Imagine two people preparing their kits. Sarah lives in a high-rise apartment in downtown Miami. Mark lives in a rural cabin in the mountains of Colorado. If Sarah and Mark both followed a generic "Top 10 Items" list from the internet, they might both pack a heavy-duty wool blanket and a handheld fire starter. For Mark, in a Colorado winter, that blanket is a lifesaver. For Sarah, in the humid heat of Florida, that blanket is wasted space in her Go Bag, and a fire starter is useless (and potentially dangerous) in a concrete apartment complex. This is the "One Size Fits None" problem. A 72-hour kit is not a product you buy off a shelf; it is a strategy you build. To make your kit effective, you must first perform a Risk Assessment. A Risk Assessment is the process of identifying the specific threats you are likely to face and analyzing how those threats interact with your unique living situation. Instead of preparing for "everything," which is impossible and leads to an overweight, impractical kit, you will prepare for the most likely and most dangerous scenarios in your specific zip code. Identifying Regional Hazards The first step in customization is looking outward. Geography dictates the type of emergencies you will face. While any area can experience a power outage or a localized accident, most regions have "signature" hazards. Natural Hazards Natural hazards are disasters caused by environmental factors. These generally fall into three categories: Geological Hazards: These are caused by the earth's movement. Earthquakes: Common in coastal regions or near fault lines. These require a focus on stability and immediate evacuation. Landslides/Mudslides: Common in hilly or mountainous areas, especially after heavy rain. Hydrological Hazards: These involve the movement and distribution of water. Floods: Whether from rising rivers, storm surges (coastal), or flash floods (urban/desert), water is one of the most common emergency drivers. Tsunamis: A specific risk for those living within a few miles of the ocean in seismically active zones. Meteorological Hazards: These are weather-related. Wildfires: Common in "wildland-urban interfaces" (where housing meets forest or grassland). These often require rapid evacuation via a Go Bag. Hurricanes/Cyclones/Typhoons: Long-duration events that often involve both high winds and flooding. Blizzards/Extreme Cold: Hazards that prioritize the Stay-at-Home Kit and heat retention. Tornadoes: Short-warning events that require immediate shelter-in-place strategies. Human-Made Hazards Not all risks come from nature. Depending on where you live, you may need to account for: Industrial Accidents: Living near a chemical plant, refinery, or nuclear facility. 3. Hydration Strategies

The Criticality of Water Imagine you are in the middle of Scenario B: The Mandatory Evacuation. You have your Go Bag packed and you’ve successfully exited your neighborhood. After six hours of walking and navigating traffic, the adrenaline begins to fade, and you realize your mouth is dry. You reach for your water bottle, only to find it’s nearly empty. Within a few hours of dehydration, your brain begins to fog. Simple decisions—like which street to take or how to read a map—become difficult. Your muscles cramp, and your energy plummets. While we often think of food as the primary concern in a crisis, the human body can survive for weeks without food, but only a few days without water. In an emergency, water is not just about quenching thirst; it is the fuel for your cognitive function and the primary tool for maintaining the health of your skin and eyes. Calculating Your Water Requirements When preparing your 72-hour kit, the most common mistake is underestimating how much water you actually need. Many beginners pack a single liter bottle and assume it will last. In reality, your body requires water for more than just drinking. The "Rule of One" for Drinking For a healthy adult in a temperate climate with moderate activity, the baseline requirement is one gallon (approximately 3.78 liters) per person per day. This "Rule of One" is a simplified safety margin. It accounts for: Hydration: Keeping your organs functioning. Basic Metabolism: The water your body uses to process energy. Unexpected Loss: Water lost through perspiration or respiration. Factoring in Hygiene and Utility Drinking is the priority, but you cannot ignore basic hygiene. To prevent infections and maintain skin integrity, you need additional water for: Handwashing: Essential for preventing illness when you don't have access to running water. Wound Care: Rinsing a scrape or cut. Oral Hygiene: Basic rinsing of the mouth. The Calculation Formula: To determine your total needs for a 72-hour period, use this formula: (1 Gallon for drinking + 0.5 Gallons for hygiene) x 3 Days x Number of People = Total Water Requirement. For a single person, this equals 4.5 gallons for the duration of the 72-hour rule. Variables That Increase Water Demand The "Rule of One" is a baseline. You must increase your allocation if any of the following apply to your Risk Assessment and Customization: 1. Extreme Heat: High temperatures increase sweating, requiring more fluid replacement to avoid heatstroke. 2. High Physical Exertion: If your scenario involves hiking or carrying a heavy Go Bag over long distances, your water needs will spike. 3. 4. Emergency Nutrition and Food Storage

The Energy Gap: Why "Normal" Food Fails in a Crisis Imagine you are in the middle of Scenario A: The Power Outage. Your refrigerator has stopped humming, the stove won't ignite, and the stress of the situation has your heart racing. You open your pantry and see a bag of dried lentils and a box of raw pasta. To a hungry person, these look like food. To a survivalist, these are "liabilities." To eat those lentils, you need clean water to boil them and a heat source to cook them for 40 minutes. If you are relying on the limited water supplies established in Hydration Strategies, spending a liter of water just to cook a meal is a dangerous trade-off. Furthermore, the energy spent cooking and cleaning up may outweigh the calories you gain from the meal. In an emergency, your goal isn't a balanced culinary experience; it is Caloric Density. You need the maximum amount of energy (calories) for the minimum amount of weight and preparation effort. Understanding Caloric Density and Shelf-Stability Before picking out cans and bars, you need to understand two fundamental concepts: Caloric Density and Shelf-Stability. Caloric Density Caloric Density refers to the number of calories per gram of food. In a Go Bag, where every ounce matters, you cannot afford to carry "heavy" calories (like canned soups, which are mostly water). Instead, you want "dense" calories—foods high in fats and proteins. For example: Low Density: A can of vegetable soup. Much of the weight is water, providing relatively few calories. High Density: A handful of almonds or a tablespoon of peanut butter. These provide a massive burst of energy in a tiny, lightweight package. Shelf-Stability Shelf-Stable foods are those that can be stored at room temperature for long periods without spoiling. These foods typically have low moisture content or are preserved through canning, drying, or vacuum-sealing. When selecting foods for your 72-hour kit, prioritize those that do not require refrigeration and have a "Best By" date at least six months into the future. High-Energy Food Categories To build a balanced kit that keeps your brain sharp and your muscles moving through The Stabilization Phase (48–72 Hours), categorize your food into these four groups: 1. Complex Carbohydrates (The Fuel) Carbs provide the glucose your brain needs to make critical decisions under pressure. Energy Bars/Protein Bars: Look for those with high calorie counts and low sugar to avoid "crashing." Dried Fruits: Apricots, raisins, and dates provide quick energy and essential potassium. Crackers or Pretzels: Provide salt (essential for electrolyte balance) and easy-to-digest energy. 2. 5. First Aid and Medical Supplies

The "Small Cut" Problem Imagine you are in the middle of the Mobilization Phase (24–48 hours) after an emergency. You are moving supplies or clearing debris when you sustain a deep scrape on your forearm. It isn't life-threatening, but it is bleeding and exposed to dirt. In a normal world, you would walk to a bathroom, wash the wound with soap and water, apply an antibiotic ointment, and put on a bandage. But in an emergency scenario, your access to clean running water may be limited, and your "bathroom" might be a tent or a temporary shelter. Without a dedicated medical kit, that small scrape can quickly become an infection. In a survival situation, an infection isn't just a nuisance—it can immobilize you, create a fever, and turn a manageable situation into a medical crisis. The goal of your medical supplies is not to turn you into a doctor, but to provide stabilization. You are buying time and preventing minor injuries from becoming major complications until professional medical help is available. Understanding Your Medical Needs When assembling your kit, you must distinguish between two different types of medical care: Trauma Care and Daily Health Maintenance. Trauma Care focuses on immediate, acute injuries. This includes stopping heavy bleeding, treating burns, or stabilizing a sprained ankle. These are "right now" problems that require fast action. Daily Health Maintenance focuses on the chronic and routine. This includes managing blood pressure, treating a headache, or soothing an upset stomach. These are "ongoing" problems that ensure you stay physically capable of following the 72-Hour Rule. Depending on whether you are packing your Go Bag or your Stay-at-Home Kit, your balance of these supplies will shift. Your Go Bag needs to be lightweight and focused on trauma and immediate stability; your Stay-at-Home Kit can afford to be more comprehensive, including larger quantities of maintenance medications. The Basic First Aid Kit: Essential Components A first aid kit is only useful if you know exactly what is in it and how to use it. For a beginner, the best approach is to build a kit based on categories of care. Wound Care and Infection Prevention The primary goal of wound care is to stop bleeding and keep bacteria out of the body. Antiseptics: These are substances that stop or slow the growth of microorganisms. Alcohol Prep Pads: Best for cleaning the skin around a wound or disinfecting a tool (like tweezers). Do not use these directly inside a deep cut, as they can damage healthy tissue. Povidone-Iodine or Chlorhexidine: These are more gentle antiseptics used to clean the wound itself. 6. Shelter, Warmth, and Clothing

The Invisible Danger: Exposure Imagine you have successfully packed your Go Bag with the nutrition and hydration strategies discussed in previous chapters. You have your first aid kit ready. But as you evacuate your home, a sudden autumn rainstorm hits, and the temperature drops to 40°F (4°C). Within an hour, your cotton t-shirt is soaked. You begin to shiver uncontrollably; your fingers become numb, and you find it difficult to focus on the map in your hand. You aren't injured, and you aren't starving, but you are in danger. This is exposure. Exposure occurs when your body loses heat faster than it can produce it. While we often associate "cold" with freezing winter temperatures, hypothermia—the dangerous drop in core body temperature—can happen in 50°F weather if you are wet and windy. In an emergency, your primary goal is not "comfort" in the luxury sense, but thermoregulation: the ability of your body to maintain its internal core temperature (roughly 98.6°F or 37°C) regardless of the environment. Protecting your core temperature is a biological priority. If your core temperature drops, your brain slows down, your decision-making fails, and your ability to utilize the other tools in your 72-hour kit vanishes. The Science of Staying Warm To choose the right clothing and shelter, you must understand how you lose heat. Your body loses warmth through four primary mechanisms: 1. Conduction: Heat transfer through direct contact. (Example: Sitting on a cold concrete floor). 2. Convection: Heat loss caused by moving air or wind. (Example: A chilly breeze stripping away the layer of warmth surrounding your skin). 3. Evaporation: Heat loss as liquid turns to gas. (Example: Sweat drying on your skin or wet clothes chilling you). 4. Radiation: Heat escaping from your body into the surrounding air. (Example: Your body heat radiating away into a cold room). Your 72-hour kit should contain items that act as barriers against these four forces. Clothing: The Layering System When packing your Go Bag or Stay-at-Home Kit, avoid the temptation to pack one massive, heavy coat. Heavy coats are bulky, difficult to pack, and often too hot for the "Chaos Phase" of an emergency when you are physically exerting yourself. Instead, use a layering system. Layering allows you to add or remove clothing to regulate your temperature, preventing you from overheating (which leads to sweat and evaporation) or freezing. The Base Layer (Moisture Management) The base layer is the clothing touching your skin. Its sole purpose is to wick moisture away from your body. What to choose: Synthetic fabrics (like polyester) or Merino wool. What to avoid: Cotton. In the survival community, there is a common saying: "Cotton Kills." 7. Tools and Utility Essentials

The "Wrong Tool" Dilemma Imagine you are in the Mobilization Phase (24–48 hours) of an emergency. You have successfully moved to a safer location, but you need to secure a piece of loose tarp over a broken window to maintain the warmth and shelter you established in the previous chapter. You reach into your Go Bag and realize you have a heavy-duty hammer and a set of industrial pliers, but no way to actually cut the fabric or tighten a screw. You have tools, but you don't have utility. In an emergency, the goal isn't to have a fully stocked workshop; it is to have the minimum amount of gear that provides the maximum amount of capability. This is the essence of utility: the state of being useful or profitable. When packing your 72-hour kit, every ounce of weight must earn its place by solving multiple potential problems. Multi-Tools vs. Single-Purpose Tools When selecting gear, you will face a constant tug-of-war between the Multi-Tool and the Single-Purpose Tool. A Multi-Tool is a single device that combines several functions—such as a knife, pliers, screwdriver, and can opener—into one handle. A Single-Purpose Tool is a tool designed to do one specific job exceptionally well, such as a dedicated folding knife or a standalone flashlight. The Case for the Multi-Tool The primary advantage of a multi-tool is efficiency of space. In a Go Bag, where portability is the primary goal, a multi-tool allows you to carry ten different capabilities while only taking up the space of one. Common multi-tool features and their emergency uses: Pliers: Gripping small objects, pulling nails, or twisting wire. Wire Cutters: Cutting zip ties or electrical wires. Screwdriver: Tightening loose equipment or opening battery compartments. Can Opener: Essential for the nutrition strategies discussed in previous chapters. Small Blade: Precision cutting, trimming threads, or preparing first aid materials. The Case for the Single-Purpose Tool While multi-tools are convenient, they often suffer from "compromise." Because the tool must be small enough to fold, the blade is often shorter and the pliers are less powerful than their full-sized counterparts. When to choose a single-purpose tool: Durability: If you know you will be performing heavy-duty tasks (like chopping wood for warmth), a dedicated hatchet is safer and more effective than a multi-tool saw. Ergonomics: Using a tiny screwdriver on a stubborn screw for an hour is exhausting; a dedicated handle provides better leverage. Criticality: If a tool is "mission-critical"—meaning your safety depends entirely on it working—a dedicated, high-quality version is usually more reliable. 8. Communication and Documentation

The Silence of the Grid Imagine you have successfully packed your Go Bag with the tools and supplies discussed in previous chapters. You have your water, your food, and your first aid kit. Suddenly, an evacuation order is issued. You grab your bag and step outside, only to find that your smartphone has "No Service." You try to call your spouse or parents to coordinate a meeting point, but the call won't go through. You want to know if the main highway is flooded or if a shelter has opened nearby, but the internet is down. In this moment, you realize that while your physical supplies keep your body alive, information is what keeps you safe and connected. During the Chaos Phase (0–24 Hours), the lack of reliable information creates panic. When the cellular network crashes—either due to physical damage to towers or sheer volume of people trying to call at once—you are effectively blind and mute. This chapter focuses on building a "bridge" of information. We will ensure you can receive critical alerts from authorities, maintain a lifeline to your loved ones, and possess the legal proof of who you are, even if your digital life disappears. Redundant Communication Systems In emergency preparedness, we use the term redundancy. Redundancy is the practice of having more than one way to accomplish a critical task. If your primary method of communication (your smartphone) fails, you need a secondary method. If that fails, you need a tertiary method. The Limitations of Smartphones Most people rely entirely on their phones for calls, texts, maps, and news. However, smartphones have three major points of failure: 1. Battery Life: They drain quickly, especially when searching for a weak signal. 2. Infrastructure Dependency: They require functioning cell towers and electricity. 3. Fragility: A single drop or a splash of water can render the device useless. The NOAA Weather Radio Your first line of redundant communication should be a NOAA Weather Radio. NOAA stands for the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. These radios receive broadcasts from a network of government transmitters that operate independently of the cellular network. A dedicated weather radio provides: Real-time Alerts: Immediate warnings about tornadoes, floods, or wildfires. Emergency Alerts (EAS): Instructions from local or federal governments regarding evacuations or "shelter-in-place" orders. Reliability: Because they use radio waves, they often work when the internet and cell service are completely offline. When choosing a radio for your Go Bag, look for one that offers multiple power sources. A radio that can be powered by standard batteries, a hand crank, and a solar panel is the gold standard for redundancy. 9. Hygiene and Sanitation

The Hidden Danger of the Aftermath Imagine you have successfully navigated the first 48 hours of an emergency. You have a secure shelter, you are utilizing the hydration strategies from Chapter 3, and you are eating the rations you planned in Chapter 4. You feel safe. However, on day three, you develop a severe case of food poisoning or a skin infection from a small scratch. In a stable environment, this is a nuisance. In an emergency—where medical facilities may be unreachable and your energy is already depleted—a preventable infection can become a life-threatening crisis. When the power goes out and the water stops flowing, the biggest threat to your health isn't always the event itself; it is the breakdown of sanitation. Sanitation refers to the public health conditions related to clean drinking water and the adequate disposal of human waste. When plumbing fails, the invisible world of bacteria, viruses, and parasites begins to move into your living space. This chapter focuses on how to prevent that movement and keep your body and environment clean when you have no running water. The First Principle of Emergency Hygiene The goal of hygiene in a resource-limited setting is not "beauty" or "freshness"—it is disease prevention. In a normal setting, we use water liberally to wash away germs. In a survival situation, water is a precious resource that must be prioritized for drinking (as detailed in Hydration Strategies). Therefore, the first principle of emergency hygiene is water conservation. You must find ways to stay clean without wasting the water you need to survive. Understanding the Vectors of Infection To maintain hygiene, you must understand how illness spreads. In an emergency, diseases typically move via three main "vectors" (paths of transmission): 1. Fecal-Oral Route: This occurs when microscopic particles of waste (from humans or animals) contaminate food, water, or hands, which are then ingested. This is the most common cause of dysentery and cholera. 2. Skin-to-Surface: Bacteria enter the body through cuts or abrasions (which you should treat using the supplies in First Aid and Medical Supplies) or through prolonged contact with contaminated surfaces. 3. Respiratory Droplets: Germs spread through the air in crowded, poorly ventilated shelters. By controlling these three paths, you significantly reduce the risk of illness during the Stabilization Phase (48–72 Hours). Essential Toiletries for Your Kit When building your Go Bag or Stay-at-Home Kit, you should select toiletries that are multi-purpose, compact, and waterless. 10. Specialized Needs: Family and Pets

When "Standard" Isn't Enough Imagine you have successfully assembled your Go Bag based on the general guidelines provided in the previous chapters. You have your water, non-perishable food, first aid kit, and flashlight. You are ready to move. Then, you look at your six-month-old daughter, your eighty-year-old father who relies on a walker, and your golden retriever. Suddenly, the "standard" kit feels woefully incomplete. A flashlight doesn't soothe a crying infant; a protein bar doesn't help a dog with anxiety; and a generic first aid kit doesn't include the specific batteries needed for a hearing aid. The 72-Hour Rule applies to everyone, but the contents of the kit must vary based on the biological and physical needs of the individuals in your care. This is the process of Specialized Customization: identifying the non-negotiable items that specific family members need to survive and remain stable during the Chaos, Mobilization, and Stabilization phases. Preparing for Infants and Toddlers Infants and toddlers are the most vulnerable members of any household. They cannot communicate their needs verbally, they have underdeveloped immune systems, and they rely entirely on caregivers for nutrition and hygiene. When adapting your kit for a child, you are not just packing for survival, but for stability—meaning items that keep the child calm and healthy to prevent secondary emergencies (like illness or extreme distress). Nutrition and Feeding Standard emergency nutrition (like canned beans or energy bars) is useless for a baby. You must pack specific caloric sources based on the child's age: Formula: If the child is formula-fed, pack enough for at least 72 hours. Include a manual bottle brush and a small container of mild soap. Bottles and Nipples: Pack more than you think you need. In a Go Bag scenario, you may not have the means to sterilize bottles. Baby Food: Prioritize jars or pouches that do not require heating. Clean Water: Reference your Hydration Strategies chapter; remember that formula requires a separate allocation of clean water beyond what the adults are drinking. Comfort Foods: For toddlers, include a few familiar snacks (like crackers or fruit pouches) to provide psychological comfort. Hygiene and Health Infants produce significantly more waste than adults, and their skin is more sensitive to irritants. Diapers and Wipes: Plan for 8–10 diapers per day. Pack a generous supply of fragrance-free baby wipes, as these can double as a way to clean the child's skin when bathing is impossible. 11. Packing, Weight Management, and Storage

The "Tipping Point": Why Organization Matters Imagine this: An evacuation order is issued. You have ten minutes to leave. You grab your Go Bag, swing it over your shoulder, and start walking. Within five minutes, you feel a sharp pull in your lower back. Within ten, you realize you need your First Aid and Medical Supplies, but they are buried at the very bottom, underneath your Shelter, Warmth, and Clothing. To get to them, you have to dump the entire contents of your bag onto the ground in the rain. In a crisis, your kit is only as useful as your ability to access its contents and carry it without injury. A bag full of the right gear—but packed poorly—can become a liability rather than an asset. This chapter transforms your collection of supplies into a streamlined, portable system. Modular Packing: The Power of the Pouch When you throw everything into one large bag, you create a "black hole" effect. You know the item is in there, but you can't find it without emptying the bag. To solve this, we use Modular Packing. Modular Packing is the practice of grouping related items into smaller, color-coded or labeled pouches (modules) before placing them into the main Go Bag. Instead of searching for a single bandage, you simply pull out the "Medical Module." Creating Your Modules Based on the supplies you gathered in previous chapters, organize your gear into the following modules: The Medical Module: All items from your First Aid and Medical Supplies chapter. Use a red or transparent pouch. The Nutrition Module: Your Emergency Nutrition and Food Storage items. Use a durable, leak-proof bag to prevent crumbs or spills from contaminating other gear. The Utility Module: Tools from Tools and Utility Essentials (multitools, flashlights, batteries). A heavy-duty canvas pouch works best here. The Hygiene Module: Everything from Hygiene and Sanitation. Use a waterproof bag to ensure soap or liquids don't leak onto your documentation. The Admin Module: Your Communication and Documentation folder and backup power banks. A waterproof "dry bag" is highly recommended here. The Comfort/Warmth Module: Clothing and shelter items from Shelter, Warmth, and Clothing. These are often bulky and may stay loose in the bag rather than in a pouch. Benefits of the Modular System 1. 12. Maintenance and Testing

The Danger of the "Set It and Forget It" Mentality Imagine this: a severe storm hits, and you have only five minutes to evacuate. You grab your Go Bag, throw it over your shoulder, and head for the door. Once you reach safety, you realize you are thirsty and reach for your water purification tablets, only to find they expired three years ago and have turned into useless powder. You go to treat a scrape with your first aid kit, but the adhesive on the bandages has dried out, and they won't stick to your skin. You reach for your backup medications, but the prescriptions are for a dosage your doctor changed a year ago. All the effort you put into the previous eleven modules—the careful selection of tools, the calculated nutrition, and the strategic packing—is rendered useless not by a lack of planning, but by a lack of maintenance. An emergency kit is not a static product you buy once; it is a living system. Because food spoils, batteries leak, medications expire, and your own life circumstances change, your kit requires a systematic approach to stay functional. The Semi-Annual Maintenance Cycle The most effective way to ensure your kit is ready is to implement a Semi-Annual Review. This means checking your gear every six months. Many people tie these dates to events that are hard to forget, such as the start of Daylight Saving Time (spring and autumn) or the beginning and end of the school year. By anchoring your maintenance to a recurring calendar event, you remove the need to "remember" to do it. Creating Your Expiration Checklist A checklist prevents "decision fatigue"—the mental exhaustion of trying to remember every single item in your bag. Your checklist should be a simple document (digital or paper) that lists every perishable or time-sensitive item. When building your checklist, categorize items by their shelf life: Short-term (6–12 months): Prescription medications, certain high-protein snacks, and some types of batteries. Medium-term (1–3 years): Over-the-counter medications (aspirin, ibuprofen), most canned goods, and some water purification chemicals. Long-term (5+ years): Freeze-dried meals, high-quality aluminum foil blankets, and certain specialized tools.
