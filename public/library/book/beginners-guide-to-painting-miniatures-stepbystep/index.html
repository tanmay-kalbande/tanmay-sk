1. 1. Getting Started: Understanding Miniatures

What Is a Miniature? Imagine you’ve just opened a cardboard box at a tabletop game store. Inside sits a tiny plastic soldier, only a few centimeters tall, waiting to be placed on a game board. That tiny figure is a miniature – a three‑dimensional representation of a character, creature, vehicle, or piece of scenery that is deliberately made at a reduced size. Miniatures are the core “actors” in many tabletop hobbies: war‑gaming, role‑playing games (RPGs), board games, and even collectible tabletop displays. They are typically produced in three basic formats: | Format | Typical Material | Typical Use | |--------|------------------|-------------| | Plastic | Polystyrene (often injection‑molded) | Mass‑produced hobby kits, widely used in games | | Metal | Lead‑based alloys (e.g., pewter) | Older or premium lines, often for detail‑intensive figures | | Resin | Polyurethane resin, cast in silicone molds | Small‑run, high‑detail models (often for niche or specialty lines) | Regardless of material, a miniature is scaled down from a “real‑world” counterpart. That scaling is what lets us keep entire battlefields, cityscapes, or fantasy scenes on a tabletop that fits comfortably on a coffee table. --- Understanding Scale The Concept of Scale Scale is the ratio between the size of the miniature and the size of the object it represents. In everyday language we might think of “scale” as a measurement of size, but in miniatures it is a proportional relationship. For example, a 28 mm scale figure is not necessarily 28 mm tall; rather, the average human figure in that line measures roughly 28 mm from the base of the foot to the eyes (or sometimes to the top of the head, depending on the manufacturer’s convention). Why does this matter? Scale determines how different types of models interact on the same tabletop. A 28 mm infantryman will look appropriately sized next to a 54 mm monster, but a 15 mm vehicle would appear tiny and out of proportion if placed next to the same infantryman. How Scale Is Expressed Miniature scales are commonly expressed in three ways: 1. Millimeter measurement – e.g., 28 mm, 32 mm, 54 mm. This refers to the height of a standard human figure (often measured to the eyes). 2. Ratio notation – e.g., 1:56, 1:35. This is a true mathematical ratio (1 unit on the model equals 56 units in real life). Ratio notation is most common with resin and metal figures. 3. Descriptive name – e.g., “heroic scale” or “micro‑scale”. These are informal terms that convey a general size range but are not precise measurements. The difference between a chaotic “paint‑and‑hope” approach and a smooth, enjoyable painting session often comes down to three things: 1. The right brush for the job 2. Paint that behaves predictably 3. Safe, clean working habits The sections below walk you through every item you’ll need, why it matters, and how to use it safely. By the end of this chapter you’ll be able to set up a functional painting station that lets you focus on creativity rather than troubleshooting. --- 1. Brushes – Your Primary Extension of the Hand 1.1 Brush Anatomy (First‑time definitions) - Ferrule – The metal band that holds the bristles to the handle. A sturdy ferrule prevents bristles from loosening. - Handle – Usually made of wood, plastic, or metal; it gives you leverage and balance. - Bristles – The part that contacts the paint. Bristles can be natural (animal hair) or synthetic (nylon, polyester, or a blend). 1.2 Why Synthetic Wins for Beginners Synthetic brushes are inexpensive, resist water‑soluble acrylics, and retain their shape longer than natural hair. 3. 3. Preparing the Miniature: Cleaning, Trimming, and Priming

1. Why Preparation Matters Imagine you have just unboxed a brand‑new 28 mm plastic infantry squad. The pieces look perfect in the box, but as soon as you try to lay down a base coat, the paint beads up, slides off, or fills in every tiny groove. The culprit is almost always pre‑painting preparation: leftover sprue plastic, flash (excess material from the molding process), and surface oils all interfere with paint adhesion. A clean, properly trimmed, and well‑primed miniature gives the paint a “handshake” it can trust, turning a frustrating start into a smooth, rewarding experience. 2. Removing Sprues and Flash 2.1. Identify the Problem Areas Sprues – the thin “trees” that hold the individual parts together in the runner. They are usually attached at the base or near joints. Flash – thin, unwanted strands of plastic, metal, or resin that cling to edges where the mold halves met. Both appear as extra material that does not belong to the model’s final shape. Leaving them on can create unrealistic silhouettes and trap paint. 2.2. Tools of the Trade From Essential Tools & Materials you already have: Side‑cutting nippers – ideal for snipping sprues cleanly. Fine‑cut hobby knives – perfect for shaving off flash. Sandpaper (400–800 grit) or fine sanding sticks – for smoothing after cutting. 2.3. Step‑by‑Step Removal 1. Secure the piece – Place the miniature on a soft, non‑slippery surface (a rubber mat or a piece of foam). 2. Snip the sprue – Grip the sprue just above the attachment point with the nippers and make a decisive cut. Avoid “crushing” the part; a clean snap reduces the need for extra sanding. 3. Trim flash – Hold the knife at a shallow angle (about 30°) and gently scrape the flash away, moving from the base toward the tip. 4. Smooth the cut – Lightly run a piece of 600‑grit sandpaper over the cut area. The goal is to remove any burrs without rounding off intentional details. Pro tip: Work in a well‑lit area and use a magnifying lamp if you have one. Small flash pieces can be invisible to the naked eye but will show up later as “paint spikes” after priming. 2.4. Avoiding Damage Don’t force the knife – If the material resists, it may be a harder resin or a delicate feature. Switch to a finer blade or a different angle. Check for hidden connections – Some sprues are hidden behind armor plates or under the feet. Rotate the model and look for any thin strands before you begin cutting. 3. 4. 4. Color Theory Basics for Miniature Painting

A Miniature‑Scale Color Challenge Imagine you’ve just primed a 28 mm fantasy infantryman (see Getting Started: Understanding Miniatures for scale details) and you only have three tubes of acrylic paint in your toolbox: Cadmium Red, Ultramarine Blue, and Titanium White. Can you create a believable skin tone, a striking armor highlight, and a shadow that makes the figure pop on the tabletop? The answer lies in the fundamentals of color theory—the rules that govern how colors interact, how they can be mixed, and how they affect the viewer’s perception of depth. Mastering these basics lets you stretch a modest paint collection into a full‑featured palette. --- The Color Wheel: Building Blocks of Color Primary Colors Red, Blue, and Yellow are called primary because they cannot be created by mixing other colors. In most acrylic ranges, the “pure” primaries are Cadmium Red, Ultramarine Blue, and Cadmium Yellow. Secondary Colors Mixing two primaries in equal parts yields the secondary colors: | Mix | Result | |-----|--------| | Red + Yellow | Orange | | Yellow + Blue | Green | | Blue + Red | Purple | These are the first “new” hues you can generate from a limited set of tubes. Complementary Colors On a traditional 12‑segment wheel, every hue has a complement directly opposite it. Complementary pairs (e.g., Red ↔ Green, Blue ↔ Orange, Yellow ↔ Purple) have the strongest visual contrast. When placed side‑by‑side they vibrate—a useful effect for making a magical glow or a war‑painted scar stand out. When mixed together they neutralize, producing a range of grays or browns that are perfect for shading and washes later in the workflow (see Chapter 5). Pro tip: Because acrylics are transparent when thinned, a thin layer of a complementary color can be used as a glazing technique to shift the underlying hue without fully covering it. --- Warm vs. Cool Hues: Guiding the Eye What Makes a Hue “Warm” or “Cool”? Warm hues contain more red, orange, or yellow. They feel forward and energetic. Cool hues contain more blue, green, or violet. They feel receding and calm. 5. 5. Brushwork Techniques: Basecoat, Wash, and Dry Brushing

A Miniature’s First Impression: Why the First Three Techniques Matter Imagine you’ve just primed a freshly sculpted 28 mm orc warrior. The plastic surface is smooth, the tiny muscles are defined, and the miniature is ready for its first splash of color. The moment you lift the brush, you face three decisions that will shape the whole model: How do you lay down a uniform basecoat without leaving brush marks? Which method will make the deep crevices of the armor look naturally shadowed? How can you make the raised fur and metal edges pop without over‑painting? The answers are the three core brushwork techniques—basecoat, wash, and dry‑brushing—that give every miniature depth and texture. Mastering them turns a flat, single‑tone figure into a miniature that “reads” correctly on the tabletop, even from a distance. --- Basecoat: Laying a Uniform Foundation Why a Good Basecoat Is the Bedrock of All Later Work A basecoat is the first layer of color applied after priming. It establishes the overall hue of a surface and provides a consistent foundation for washes, highlights, and other details. A streak‑free basecoat ensures that shadows created later will look natural rather than the result of uneven pigment distribution. Materials You’ll Need (Referencing Chapter 2) - Acrylic paint in the desired base color (e.g., a mid‑tone green for the orc’s skin). - Thin‑to‑medium‑consistency brush (size 1 or 2) – a synthetic sable brush works well for smooth strokes. - Paint thinner or water (if you prefer a slightly more fluid base). - Palette or a disposable plastic plate for mixing. Tip: If you have a “wet‑on‑wet” brush from the Essential Tools chapter, you can use it to keep the paint fluid longer, which helps avoid brush‑stroke lines. Preparing the Paint 1. Squeeze a small amount of paint onto the palette—no more than a pea‑size amount per color. 2. Add 5–10 % thinner (or a few drops of water) and stir gently. The goal is a paint that flows off the brush tip but still clings to the miniature. 3. Test the consistency on a scrap piece of plastic or a spare model. If the paint beads, add a touch more thinner; if it runs too quickly, add a tiny dab of paint. Step‑by‑Step Application 1. Load the brush lightly. Dip the tip into the paint, then wipe the side of the brush on the palette edge to remove excess. 2. Approach the miniature at a shallow angle (about 30°) and drag the brush across the surface in long, overlapping strokes. 3. Work in sections—start with the largest, flattest areas (e.g., the torso) and finish with smaller, more detailed zones. 4. Allow each pass to dry (usually 2–3 minutes for acrylics) …

6. 6. Detailing: Highlights, Edge‑Highlighting, and Fine Lines

A Spark of Light on the Battlefield Imagine a freshly painted 28 mm Space Marine standing amid a ruined cityscape. The base coat of deep metallic blue armor is solid, the wash has settled into the crevices, and the dry‑brush highlights give the plates a faint sheen. Yet the miniature still feels flat—until a single, thin line of bright silver catches the edge of a shoulder plate and a glint of electric blue appears in the visor. That tiny flash instantly lifts the model from “painted” to “alive.” The difference lies in edge‑highlighting and fine‑line detailing—the skills that turn a competent base‑coat into a miniature that seems ready to step off the table. This chapter walks you through those techniques step by step, assuming you have already completed the preparation, priming, and basic coating stages covered in earlier modules. --- 1. Getting Your Tools Ready 1.1 The Fine‑Point Brush A fine‑point brush (often labeled “size 00” or “size 0”) has a tip that tapers to a point no wider than a human hair. For beginners, a synthetic brush with a soft tip works well; it holds paint without fraying and is easy to clean. Tip: Keep a spare brush in your painting kit. Fine‑point brushes are the most fragile part of the collection and can lose their shape after many washes. 1.2 Paint Selection - Edge‑highlight colors: Choose a hue that is 1–2 values lighter than the base color of the surface you’re highlighting. For metallic armor, a light silver or pale gold works. For fabric, a subtle tint (e.g., a pale tan over brown) keeps the effect natural. - Fine‑line colors: Pure white works for eyes, lenses, and insignia, but you can also use a very light version of the base color for a softer look. 1.3 Palette Setup 1. Load a small amount of the highlight color onto a clean area of the palette. 2. Thin the paint with a drop of water or a dedicated brush medium (if you have one). The consistency should be the same as the base coat—just a little more fluid so it flows off the tip without pooling. 3. Keep a second well of the base color nearby for quick blending. --- 2. 7. 7. Sealing and Protecting Your Work

Why a Finish Matters Imagine you have just spent an afternoon carefully base‑coating, shading, and highlighting a 28 mm infantry model. The colors are vibrant, the tiny sword gleams, and the fabric folds catch the light just right. You set the figure down, step away, and return a week later to find the painted surface dulled, a faint haze covering the highlights, and a few tiny chips where the armor meets the base. The culprit isn’t the paint itself—it’s the lack of a protective finish. A sealant (often called a varnish) forms a thin, transparent skin that locks the pigment in place, shields the surface from handling, dust, and moisture, and can even enhance the visual impact of your work. In the same way a clear coat on a car protects the paint while adding a desired sheen, a miniature’s finish is the final, essential layer that ensures your hard‑earned effort endures. This chapter walks you through the three main types of finishes—matte, satin, and glossy—shows how to apply them without smudging the details you’ve already created, and explains how to store finished miniatures safely. By the end, you’ll be able to choose the right finish for any model, apply it confidently, and keep your painted armies looking fresh for months or years. --- 1. Understanding the Three Main Finishes | Finish | Appearance | Typical Uses | Pros | Cons | |--------|------------|--------------|------|------| | Matte | Flat, non‑reflective; looks like chalk or soft fabric | Low‑light environments, “weathered” or ancient looks, when you want colors to read without shine | Reduces glare, hides surface imperfections, easy to touch up | Can look dull on high‑detail areas; may absorb fingerprints | | Satin | Soft sheen—more light than matte but less than gloss | General purpose, most “standard” miniatures, balanced look for both bright and dark colors | Gives depth without overwhelming shine, good for most skin tones and fabrics | Slightly more prone to fingerprints than matte | | Glossy | Mirror‑like shine; reflects a lot of light | Metallic weapons, glass, water, or when you want a “wet” look | Intensifies colors, makes highlights pop, great for special effects | Highlights dust and fingerprints, can emphasize surface flaws | How the finishes differ chemically All three are typically acrylic‑based, meaning they are water‑soluble before curing and become water‑resistant once dry. The difference lies in the amount and type of additives (such as silicone or polymer resins) that control how much light is reflected off the surface. Matte finishes contain more matting agents that scatter light, while glossy finishes have fewer of these agents, allowing more direct reflection. Choosing the right finish 1. 8. 8. Simple Weathering Effects

Why Weathering Is the Secret Sauce Imagine you’ve just finished a crisp, clean base‑coat on a 28 mm Space Marine. The armor gleams, the insignia pops, and the figure looks ready for a tabletop showdown. Yet, after a few games, the miniature sits beside a battle‑worn veteran that’s covered in scratches, rust, and a thin film of grime. The difference is instantly obvious—weathering tells a story of use, age, and environment. For beginners, the “extra” step of weathering can feel daunting, but the techniques covered here require only a few everyday tools and a little patience. By the end of this chapter you’ll be able to add rust, dirt, and grime with washes and sponges, create subtle chipping with dry‑brushing, and keep the overall look balanced and appealing. --- 1. Getting Ready – The Quick Prep Checklist Before you start adding weathering effects, make sure the miniature is ready: | Item | Why It Matters | |------|----------------| | Fully cured basecoat (as described in Chapter 5) | Washes and sponges will pool in the paint’s pores; a dry surface ensures even flow. | | Sealed surface (see Chapter 7) | A clear matte seal protects the basecoat from being unintentionally lifted by washes or sponge moisture. | | Clean working area | Dust can become unwanted “grime” in your weathering. | | Fine‑pointed brush (size 0 or 00) | Needed for precise wash placement. | | Natural sea‑sponges (or a dedicated hobby sponge) | Provides texture for rust and dirt. | | Dry‑brush (size 0 or 1) | Used for chipping; a dry brush holds minimal paint. | | Palette or disposable plate | To mix washes and thin paints. | | Paper towels or lint‑free cloth | For blotting excess liquid. | Tip: If you plan to apply a final varnish later, wait until after the weathering is completely dry before sealing again. --- 2. 9. 9. Planning a Painting Session & Troubleshooting Common Issues

Setting Up Your Painting Session Imagine this: It’s Saturday morning, you’ve got a fresh miniature in front of you, paints at the ready, and a clear afternoon ahead. You sit down, ready to start—only to realize you forgot to thin your paint, your brush is drying out, and now your carefully planned color scheme looks muddy under the lamp. What should have been an enjoyable 4-hour session turns into a frustrating 2-hour scramble. This scenario happens to every beginner at some point. The difference between a rewarding painting experience and a frustrating one often comes down to preparation and knowing when to pause and troubleshoot. The goal of this chapter isn’t just to help you paint better miniatures—it’s to help you plan better sessions so that frustration stays minimal and progress stays steady. Whether you’re working on a single hero figure or prepping an entire unit for your next game, the way you structure your time and space directly affects the quality of your work. And even with the best plans, things go wrong. Brush strokes show up where they shouldn’t. Colors bleed. Paints dry too fast. Knowing how to fix these issues quickly saves both your miniature and your sanity. Let’s begin by building a realistic painting schedule—one that respects your time, your focus, and the physical demands of working with small details. Then, we’ll walk through the most common issues beginners face, why they happen, and how to fix them without starting over. --- Build a Realistic Painting Schedule Painting miniatures isn’t just about skill—it’s about rhythm. A well-paced session keeps your hands steady, your eyes fresh, and your motivation high. Without structure, even a simple figure can take much longer than expected, especially when you’re learning. Step 1: Assess the Project Before you begin, ask yourself: - How complex is the miniature? A simple human figure with two colors will take less time than a space marine with intricate armor plating and multiple materials (plastic, metal, resin). - How many miniatures are you painting in one go? Painting 6 identical troops is different from painting one hero character. Repetition slows you down at first but speeds you up later. - What techniques will you use? Basecoating is fast. Edge highlighting takes time. Weathering adds even more steps. Each layer builds on the last. For example, if you’re painting a single Space Marine (a common starter model), expect to spend: - 15 minutes cleaning and priming - 45 minutes basecoating armor panels - 30 minutes applying washes - 25 minutes edge highlighting - 15 minutes detailing weapons and accessories - 10 minutes sealing That’s 2 hours 20 minutes for one miniature—assuming no interruptions and steady progress.
