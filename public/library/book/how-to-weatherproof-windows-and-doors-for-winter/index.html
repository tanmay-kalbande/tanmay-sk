1. Fundamentals of Home Heat Loss

Imagine sitting on your sofa on a chilly December evening. The thermostat on the wall reads 70°F (21°C), but you simply cannot get warm. You grab a blanket, assuming the house is just taking time to heat up, until you walk past your front window and feel a distinct, icy breeze hitting your neck. You hold your hand up to the window frame, and there it is: a steady, invisible stream of cold air pouring into your living room. You have just discovered a draft, and with it, the reason your heating system seems to run constantly without ever quite keeping you comfortable. Before you can effectively fight the cold, you need to understand how heat is actually escaping your home. Weatherproofing is not just about slapping tape over a gap; it is about applying specific solutions to specific types of heat loss. In this module, we will build a foundation of how heat moves through windows and doors, learn the language of weatherproofing, and gather the basic tools required to get the job done. The Thermal Envelope: Your Home's Winter Coat To understand heat loss, we first need to look at your house as a complete system. In building science, the boundary between the heated interior of your home and the cold outdoors is called the thermal envelope. Think of the thermal envelope as your home’s winter coat. It includes the roof, exterior walls, foundation, and—crucially—every window and door. When this envelope is thick, continuous, and tightly sealed, your home retains heat efficiently. When the envelope has gaps, cracks, or thin spots, heat escapes, forcing your furnace to work overtime to replace the lost warmth. Windows and doors are inherently the weakest points in your thermal envelope. While an exterior wall might be packed with thick fiberglass or cellulose insulation, a window is just a thin pane of glass held in place by a frame. Because of this, windows and doors are responsible for a disproportionate amount of home heat loss. To stop this loss, we have to understand the two primary ways heat moves through these vulnerable areas. The Physics of Cold: Conduction vs. Convection Heat is a form of energy, and it is constantly trying to move from warmer areas to colder areas to reach an equilibrium. In your home, heat escapes through windows and doors via two distinct physical processes: conduction and convection. Understanding the difference between the two is the key to knowing which weatherproofing strategy to use. Conduction (Heat Transfer) Conduction is the transfer of heat through a solid material. 2. Detecting and Assessing Air Leaks

Picture this: It is late October, the furnace has just kicked on for the first time, and you are sitting on the couch reading a book. Even though the thermostat says 70°F (21°C), you feel a distinct, icy chill on the back of your neck. You know the cold air is coming from somewhere near the window behind you, but when you walk over and look, everything appears closed tightly. Where is that draft coming from? As we established in the first chapter, your home’s thermal envelope is the barrier between your conditioned indoor air and the harsh outdoor elements. When that envelope is compromised, convection occurs—cold outside air pushes its way in, and your expensive heated inside air escapes. But before you can seal these breaches to stop the drafts, you have to find them. Some air leaks are obvious, while others are stealthy, hiding in micro-gaps you cannot see with the naked eye. This chapter will equip you with the techniques to play detective in your own home, allowing you to locate, categorize, and evaluate the severity of your air leaks before you ever pick up a tool. The Visual Inspection: Finding the Obvious Leaks Before you start burning incense or buying specialty tools, start with the easiest and most effective method: a thorough visual inspection. A large percentage of air leaks can be spotted simply by looking closely at the condition of your windows and doors. Preparing for the Inspection Begin your inspection during the daytime. Turn off interior lights so the natural light from outside highlights any gaps. You will want to inspect every window and exterior door in your home. Keep a notebook or a notes app on your phone handy to record what you find. 3. Choosing the Right Weatherstripping Materials

Imagine spending an entire Saturday afternoon carefully peeling and sticking weatherstripping along your drafty living room windows, only to find that the windows won’t latch shut. Or perhaps you apply a thick rubber strip to your front door, and suddenly it takes both shoulders to push it open. These are common DIY frustrations, and they almost always stem from a single mistake: using the wrong weatherstripping material for the specific gap. Weatherstripping is not a one-size-fits-all product. It is the material designed to seal the gaps around moving building components—like the parts of a window that slide open or the edge of a door that swings. While caulk is used to seal the stationary Fixed Joints and Seams around your home, weatherstripping is engineered to handle movement, friction, and repeated compression without failing. To successfully weatherproof your home for winter, you need to understand the materials available, know how to match them to the specific gaps you discovered during your air leak assessment, and learn how to accurately measure and calculate your needs. The Big Four: Comparing Common Weatherstripping Materials Walk into any hardware store, and you will find an entire aisle dedicated to weatherstripping. While the sheer variety can be overwhelming, almost all of them fall into four primary categories: felt, foam tape, V-strip, and rubber gaskets. Each has distinct properties, lifespans, and ideal uses. Felt Felt is one of the oldest and most basic forms of weatherstripping. It is typically sold in rolls, either as plain felt strips or felt attached to a metal or wood spline for nailing. How it works: It relies on being compressed to fill a gap. Pros: Very inexpensive, easy to cut with scissors, and highly visible so you can see when it needs replacing. Cons: It has a short lifespan (usually 1 to 2 years), absorbs moisture (which can lead to rot or mold), and tears easily under friction. Best for: Low-impact, low-friction areas, such as the hinge side of a door where the gap does not experience sliding friction when the door opens. It is generally not recommended for high-endurance areas like sliding window channels. Foam Tape Foam tape is exactly what it sounds like: a strip of compressible foam with an adhesive backing. It comes in varying widths and thicknesses, and is made from either open-cell foam (softer, absorbs water) or closed-cell foam (denser, acts as a moisture barrier). How it works: When a window or door is closed against it, the foam compresses to fill the uneven gap, blocking the draft. In previous modules, we explored how convection pulls warm air out and pushes cold air in through tiny gaps, and we used the Daylight Test to find those leaks. You also learned how to choose the right materials for the job. Now, it is time to put that knowledge to work. Weatherstripping a window is fundamentally about creating a movable seal. Unlike caulking, which permanently seals stationary joints, weatherstripping allows your windows to open and close while blocking air from squeezing through The Gap Between Sash and Frame. If you have never done this before, don't worry. Installing weatherstripping is a highly accessible beginner project that requires no specialized skills—just patience, precision, and a willingness to let the adhesive do the heavy lifting. Gathering Your Tools and Materials Before you begin, clear a workspace near the window and gather your supplies. Having everything within arm's reach will make the process much smoother. 5. Weatherstripping Exterior Doors

Imagine standing in your hallway on a blustery January evening. Your exterior door is closed, locked, and looks perfectly fine from the outside. Yet, you can feel a distinct, chilly breeze brushing against your ankles and neck. You’ve already sealed your windows, but the living room still feels drafty. This is a common frustration. Because doors are moving parts, they cannot be permanently glued or caulked shut like stationary gaps around your home. Instead, they rely on flexible seals that compress when the door closes. Over time, these seals flatten, tear, or fall off, leaving the Door Perimeter—the gap between the door and its frame—wide open to the elements. In this chapter, we will focus on weatherstripping the top and sides of your exterior doors. By the end, you will know how to identify the specific parts of your door frame, choose the right materials for the job, and install them so your door latches securely without letting the winter winds in. Anatomy of an Exterior Door Frame Before you can seal a door, you need to know what you are looking at. Door terminology can be confusing, but understanding the distinct parts of the frame is critical because weatherstripping is applied differently to each one. When you look at your closed exterior door, you are looking at three main components: The Jamb: These are the two vertical sides of the frame. The door is hinged to one side (the hinge jamb) and latches into the other side (the latch jamb). The jamb forms the structural tunnel the door sits inside. The Stop: This is a thin, continuous strip of wood nailed directly to the jamb. It runs along the top and down both sides. When you close the door, the door panel pushes up against the stop, preventing the door from swinging straight through the frame. The stop creates the primary interior ledge that blocks the door. The Threshold: This is the metal, wood, or composite plate fixed to the floor directly beneath the door. It acts as a bottom seal and bridges the gap between the interior floor and the exterior step. Note: While the threshold is a crucial part of the door’s defense against drafts, sealing the bottom of the door requires specialized components like door sweeps and adjustable thresholds. 6. Sealing the Bottom of Doors with Sweeps and Thresholds

You’ve closed all the windows, sealed every crack you could find, and the thermostat reads a comfortable 70 °F. Yet, a cold draft still sneaks in every time you open the front door. When you feel the breeze, you’ll notice it coming from the very bottom of the door, sliding across the floor like a thin sheet of ice. That draft is the result of a gap between the door leaf and the floor – often several‑quarter inches wide. Because cold air is denser than warm air, it can flow under the door even when the rest of the frame is tightly sealed. This is the exact problem the chapter tackles: eliminating the bottom‑door air leak with door sweeps, door shoes, and adjustable thresholds. By the end of the section you will be able to: Differentiate the three main products that seal the door bottom. Measure and cut a sweep so it fits your door perfectly. Install a sweep or shoe without harming the flooring or the door. Adjust an existing threshold to raise it toward the door leaf. --- 1. A Cold Draft in the Night It’s 2 a.m. and the temperature outside has dropped to ‑5 °F. You’re snug under a blanket, but a faint, persistent chill brushes the back of your neck. A quick glance at the window reveals a thin line of frost along the interior edge of the frame. That line marks a stationary gap—a tiny crack where the window frame meets the wall sheathing. Because the crack isn’t moving, the only way to stop the winter air from slipping through is caulking. If you’ve ever felt a draft that seems to come from “nothing,” you’ve already witnessed the impact of unsealed joints on your home’s thermal envelope. This chapter gives you the tools to seal those stationary gaps once and for all. --- 1. Choosing the Right Caulk for the Job Caulk is a flexible, seal‑blocking material that fills gaps and prevents air movement. Not all caulks are created equal. The three most common families are silicone, latex (acrylic), and polyurethane. 8. Applying Window Insulation Film and Seasonal Barriers

A Winter‑Ready Window in a Rental Home Maria just moved into a historic row‑house that she plans to rent out next spring. The original double‑hung windows are beautiful, but the glass is single‑pane and the frames have settled over the decades. Her first heating bill shows a spike of 30 % compared with the previous year. Because the landlord won’t allow permanent modifications, Maria needs a solution that is effective, reversible, and inexpensive. This is the exact situation that window insulation film and seasonal draft barriers are designed to solve. --- How Window Insulation Film Creates an Insulating Air Pocket The Science Behind the Film When you place a clear plastic film over a window and seal it tightly to the frame, you trap a thin layer of still air between the glass and the film. Still air is a poor conductor of heat; it resists conduction and convection much more than the moving air in a room. The trapped air therefore acts like a miniature “blanket,” raising the window’s overall R‑value (the measure of thermal resistance) by roughly 1 – 2 points—a noticeable improvement for a single‑pane window. Think of the film as a second pane of glass, except that the “glass” is the film itself and the “air gap” is the sealed space you create. Because the film is transparent, daylight and the view remain largely unchanged, while the heat loss through the window is reduced. What the Film Is Made Of Polyethylene (PE) or Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) shrink film – thin, clear, and inexpensive. Adhesive backing – usually a double‑sided tape that sticks to the window frame without damaging paint or wood when removed. Heat‑shrink property – the film contracts when heated (by a hair dryer), pulling snugly against the frame and eliminating gaps. These components work together to form a sealed air pocket that stays in place for the winter months and can be removed without leaving residue when spring arrives. --- Installing Window Insulation Film: Step‑by‑Step What You’ll Need | Item | Why It’s Needed | |------|-----------------| | Clear shrink film (6 mil thickness works well) | Forms the barrier and air pocket | | Double‑sided tape (foam‑backed for extra cushion) | Provides the seal around the perimeter | | Measuring tape & marker | Ensures the film is cut to the correct size | | Scissors or utility knife | Trims excess film | | Hair dryer (or heat gun on low) | Shrinks the film for a tight fit | | Clean cloth | Removes dust before applying tape | | Optional: masking tape | Holds the film in place while you work | Preparation 1. 9. Maintenance, Removal, and Spring Preparation

A Spring‑time Wake‑up Call When the first warm breezes of March slip through a cracked window frame, the house that felt snug and quiet all winter suddenly seems drafty again. For many homeowners, the discovery comes after a night of shivering socks and a sudden spike on the heating bill. The culprit is often the same set of tools that kept the home comfortable during the freeze: permanent weatherstripping, caulk, and the shrink‑film barrier you applied in the fall. Now it’s time to inspect, clean, and reset those systems so they’re ready for the next winter—while keeping the house comfortable and energy‑efficient through the sunny months. --- 1. Inspecting Permanent Weatherstripping and Caulk After Winter Why the End‑of‑Winter Check Matters Winter subjects weatherstripping and caulk to temperature extremes, moisture, and repeated compression. By the time the snow melts, the materials may have: Cracked or split from cold‑induced brittleness. Lost elasticity (e.g., foam strips hardening). Separated from the frame where adhesive has weakened. Accumulated dirt that masks gaps and reduces effectiveness. A quick inspection now prevents hidden air leaks from persisting into the warm months, where they can still affect indoor comfort and raise cooling costs. Start at the top of each window and door frame and work clockwise. 2. Run your fingers along the weatherstripping (the rubber, vinyl, or foam edge). You should feel a smooth, continuous seal. Any roughness, gaps, or hard spots indicate wear. 3. Examine the caulk around the perimeter of the sash, the meeting rails, and the thresholds. Look for: Cracks, “crazing” (fine surface cracks), or missing sections. Hard, chalky texture—signs the caulk has lost flexibility. 4.
