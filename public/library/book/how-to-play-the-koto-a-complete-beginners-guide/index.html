1. Introduction to the Koto: History and Basics

A Quiet Instrument That Changed the World Imagine stepping into a small room in Kyoto during the Heian period, around the year 800. The air hums with the sound of a thirteen-string zither being plucked by a court noble in elaborate silk robes. This isn't just music—it's a language of status, refinement, and spiritual connection. The instrument being played? The koto. Fast forward to the 20th century, and this same instrument becomes a symbol of modern Japan. In 1912, a blind koto master named Michiyo Yagi performs at the Imperial Palace, moving audiences with her expressive adaptations of Western classical pieces. By the 1960s, koto is heard in avant-garde jazz fusion and even psychedelic pop, proving it can stretch far beyond tradition. This chapter invites you to begin a journey with the koto—not as a passive listener, but as someone about to place their hands on its polished paulownia wood and coax sound from thirteen taut silk or nylon strings. Before you touch the strings, however, you need to understand what the koto is, where it came from, and how it functions. Knowing this foundation will not only deepen your appreciation but also guide every pluck, every chord, and every glissando you'll play in the chapters ahead. --- The Koto Through Time: A Journey from China to Japan The story of the koto begins not in Japan, but in China, over two thousand years ago. Roots in the Guzheng Around the 3rd century BCE, during China's Warring States period, a plucked zither called the guzheng (古筝) emerged. Made of a long, flat wooden body with movable bridges and strings stretched across it, the guzheng allowed musicians to create subtle pitch variations by pressing down on the strings. This wasn't just an instrument—it was a bridge between sound and silence, between precision and poetry. By the 5th century CE, the guzheng had become a staple of Chinese court music. Then, in 732 CE, during the Tang Dynasty, a Japanese envoy named Ōtomo no Sakanoue no Mochio brought a version of the guzheng back to Japan. This marked the birth of the koto (箏)—a word that comes from the verb koto (音), meaning "sound," and the phonetic sound of the string itself. The Koto in the Heian Court For centuries, the koto remained an elite instrument, played almost exclusively in imperial and aristocratic circles. During the Heian period (794–1185), the koto became part of the gagaku ensemble—Japan’s ancient orchestral music—used to accompany dances, ceremonies, and poetic recitations. One of the earliest koto pieces still played today, "Rokudan no Shirabe" (六段の調べ), dates from this era. 2. Koto Picks and Hand Posture for Beginners

Holding the Koto Picks: Your First Step to Sound Imagine sitting in front of your koto for the first time. The instrument stretches across your lap, its 13 strings gleaming under the soft light. You’ve seen musicians in videos deftly plucking the strings with a flick of their fingers, their hands moving with quiet confidence. But how do they hold those small, curved pieces that make the strings sing? These are the koto picks—called tsume (爪) in Japanese—and mastering how to wear them is your first real step toward making music. The tsume aren’t like guitar picks you might have used. They’re small, curved, and made of plastic or sometimes tortoiseshell (traditionally). Each one is worn on the thumb, index, middle, and sometimes the ring finger of your right hand. They allow your fingers to glide across the strings without pressing too hard, creating clear, resonant tones. Without them, your fingertips would quickly grow sore, and the sound would be dull and muted. But wearing them correctly isn’t just a matter of slipping them on. The position and angle of each tsume affect the clarity, volume, and even the emotional tone of the music. A poorly placed pick can muffle the string or cause it to buzz. So let’s begin at the very beginning: how to wear your koto picks. --- Wearing Your Koto Picks (Tsume) Before you even touch the strings, you need to wear your tsume. Most beginners start with a set of four: one for the thumb, and one each for the index, middle, and ring fingers. Some advanced players use only three, skipping the ring finger, but for now, we’ll use all four. Choosing the Right Size and Shape Koto picks come in different sizes. The thumb pick is usually larger and more curved, while the finger picks are smaller and less pronounced. The curve should match the shape of your fingertip. - Thumb pick (oyazume): Worn on the right thumb. It should curve slightly downward so the tip points toward your palm. - Index (hitosashime), middle (nakayubi), and ring finger (kusazume) picks: These are worn on the pads of the fingers, not the tips. They should be snug but not tight, with the curved edge facing outward, toward the strings. If your picks are too loose, they’ll slide around when you pluck. If they’re too tight, they’ll restrict blood flow and make your fingers numb. Fun fact: The word tsume means “claw” in Japanese, which gives you a sense of how deeply embedded picks are in traditional koto playing. They’re not just tools—they’re an extension of the hand. 3. Basic Koto Plucking Techniques and Sound Control

Getting a Feel for the Strings Imagine sitting before a polished 13-string koto, its strings shimmering like a row of silver threads. You lift your right hand, fingers curved slightly, and pause. The first pluck changes everything—it’s not just about making a sound, but shaping it. A sharp, bright note can carry a story; a soft, rounded tone feels like a whisper. Your goal this chapter is to turn those first tentative touches into deliberate control. The two simplest motions—downward and upward plucks—are your foundation. They’re the letters of the koto alphabet. Once you master them, you’ll build melody, rhythm, and expression. This isn’t about speed. It’s about clarity, consistency, and confidence. --- The Two Core Plucks: Oshi and Hiki Every koto player begins with two fundamental motions: the downward pluck (oshi) and the upward pluck (hiki). These aren’t just directions; they’re choices about tone, timing, and texture. Oshi: The Downward Pluck An oshi is a gentle press-and-release motion where your fingertip moves downward from above the string toward your palm. - Placement: Use the fleshy pad of your index or middle finger, just below the fingernail. - Angle: Approach the string at a slight downward angle (about 30–45 degrees). - Motion: Press down firmly but not forcefully, then release cleanly so the string vibrates freely. Think of it like tapping a drumhead lightly—enough to make it ring, but without pressing too hard. 💡 Tip: Keep your wrist relaxed. Tension in your forearm will make the motion stiff and the sound dull. Hiki: The Upward Pluck The hiki moves in the opposite direction—your fingertip scoops upward from beneath the string. - Placement: Again, use the fleshy pad of your index or middle finger. - Angle: Approach the string from below at a similar 30–45 degree angle. - Motion: Lift your finger quickly upward, letting the string snap back into place. The key is a clean release, not a drag. Imagine flicking a blade of grass upward with your fingertip. The motion should be crisp and confident. 💡 Tip: Keep your finger slightly curled, so only the pad touches the string. Avoid using your nail. --- Matching Motion to String Position The koto’s 13 strings aren’t all the same. The first string (near you) is a high-pitched silver or silk-wrapped nylon, and the 13th (farthest) is a deeper, unwrapped gut or nylon string. Your plucking technique changes slightly depending on string tension and position. - High strings (1–5): Require lighter pressure and faster release. - Middle strings (6–9): Offer a balanced resistance—great for practicing control. - Low strings (10–13): Need more deliberate finger strength to avoid a muted thud. 4. Reading Koto Tabulature (Tablature) and Notation

Understanding the Koto’s Unique Language: Tablature and Notation Imagine holding your koto for the first time, fingers poised over the strings, ready to play. You know how to pluck with your right hand and where to place your fingers on the left—but how do you know which string to pluck, or how to play it? Without a map, the instrument remains a mystery. That map is koto tablature, a visual language designed to guide your fingers precisely across the 13 strings. Unlike Western sheet music, which tells you what note to play through pitch, koto tablature tells you where to play it—string by string, finger by finger. It’s a system rooted in centuries of oral tradition, refined during the Edo period by masters like Yatsuhashi Kengyō, and still the primary way koto music is taught today. For beginners, learning to read this notation isn’t just useful—it’s essential to moving beyond random plucking and into intentional music-making. This chapter will guide you through the structure of koto tablature, decode its symbols, and give you the tools to read and play simple exercises with confidence. --- The Layout of Koto Tablature: Strings, Numbers, and Order Koto tablature is organized vertically, with each line representing one of the koto’s 13 strings. The strings are numbered from 1 to 13, with string 1 (ichi) being the thinnest and highest-pitched string, closest to you when you sit at the koto. String 13 (jūsan) is the thickest and lowest-pitched, farthest from you. This numbering might feel counterintuitive at first—many stringed instruments count strings from the lowest pitch—but it makes sense when you consider how the koto is played. Since you pluck strings with your right hand while damping with your left, the numbering follows the order in which your hands interact with the strings. Fun fact: The koto’s string numbering system reflects its role in gagaku, where melodic lines often moved from high to low registers. This tradition carried over into koto music during the Heian and Edo periods. How to read a tablature line: A standard koto tablature line looks like this: Each number corresponds to a finger: - 0 = Thumb (oyayubi) - 1 = Index finger (hitosashiyubi) - 2 = Middle finger (nakayubi) - 3 = Ring finger (kusuriyubi) - 4 = Pinky (ko-yubi) (rarely used in basic pieces) A dash (—) means the string is not played in that moment. The moment the first note rings out, your whole body relaxes. You’re not just plucking strings anymore. You’re playing music. That’s the moment this chapter is designed to help you reach. Playing a complete song early on isn’t just about learning notes—it’s about building trust in your hands, your ears, and your understanding of the instrument. It transforms practice from repetition into creation. And because the koto has been used for centuries in everything from royal court music to healing rituals, playing your first song connects you not just to the music, but to a lineage of expression that spans over a thousand years. In this chapter, you’ll apply everything you’ve learned so far—reading tablature, using a pick, controlling sound with your right hand, and understanding string positions—to play a short, beautiful melody. Just curiosity and willingness to try. --- Choosing Your First Song: Simple and Meaningful For your first koto song, you want something that: - Uses only a few notes (ideally 4–6) - Has a clear, recognizable melody - Fits within the first 7 strings of the koto (strings 1–7), where your fingers can comfortably reach - Is slow enough to practice slowly and build confidence Two traditional pieces fit these criteria perfectly: Sakura Sakura (桜桜), Japan’s unofficial national song, and Akatonbo (赤とんぼ), a gentle lullaby about a red dragonfly. 6. Left-Hand Techniques: Damping and Glissando

Left-Hand as Your Secret Tool Imagine you’re playing a simple folk song on the koto—just a few notes plucked one after another in a gentle, flowing melody. The sound rings out, clear and bright, but if the strings aren’t managed, the next note begins with a muddy twang from the previous sound still vibrating. That’s where your left hand becomes more than just a support—it becomes a sound sculptor. With a gentle touch, it can silence the string at the perfect moment, leaving the music clean and intentional. Or, with a controlled slide, it can stretch one note into another, creating a smooth, singing quality known in Japanese tradition as yurashi—the art of the glissando. This chapter introduces two foundational left-hand techniques that transform your playing from simple plucking into expressive storytelling: damping and glissando. Together, they give your music clarity, flow, and emotion—qualities that define the koto’s voice across centuries, from the refined elegance of gagaku in the Heian period to the modern compositions of Toru Takemitsu and beyond. You’ve already learned how to pluck the strings and read your first music. Now, you’ll learn how to shape every note with intention, using the hand you may have been resting until now. --- Why the Left Hand Matters In traditional koto playing, the right hand does the plucking, but the left hand is the voice that shapes the music’s character. Think of it as the difference between a spoken sentence and a whispered one. The right hand gives pitch and rhythm, but the left hand controls how each note ends—and how smoothly one begins. This dual role is not unique to the koto. Similar techniques appear in other stringed instruments across East Asia, like the Chinese guzheng, where the left hand also damps and glides. The koto, however, developed its own refined style—especially during the Edo period—where left-hand control became essential to classical repertoire like "Rokudan no Shirabe" by Yatsuhashi Kengyō. So why does the koto have 13 strings? One reason is that the left hand needs space to move—between the strings, across bridges, and along the instrument’s surface. The spacing allows for precise damping and smooth glissandi, turning mechanical plucking into music that breathes. --- Step 1: Mastering Damping Damping (in Japanese, often called oshi or shibashi) means stopping a string immediately after plucking to prevent it from vibrating freely. 7. Introduction to Koto Chords and Harmony

From Melody to Harmony: The Power of the Chord Imagine playing a simple, haunting melody on your koto. It sounds beautiful, but it feels like a lone voice calling out in a vast, empty space. Now, imagine that same melody, but every few beats, a rich, resonant cluster of notes rings out beneath it, filling the room with warmth and emotional depth. That shift is the difference between monophony (a single melodic line) and harmony (two or more notes sounding simultaneously). While traditional koto music often focuses on the linear beauty of the melody, adding chords allows you to create an atmosphere, signal a change in mood, and provide a structural foundation for your music. Understanding the Koto Chord In the simplest terms, a chord is when you pluck multiple strings at the same time (or in very rapid succession) to create a combined sound. Because the koto has 13 strings stretched across a wide bridge system, you have a physical layout that allows you to reach several notes at once using your picks. The Triad: The Building Block of Harmony The most fundamental type of chord is the triad. A triad consists of three specific notes stacked together. In Western-influenced koto arrangements and modern compositions, triads are the primary tool for establishing the "feel" of a piece. There are two main types of triads you will use as a beginner: 1. Major Triads: These generally sound "happy," stable, and bright. They are often used to resolve a piece of music or create a sense of triumph. 2. Minor Triads: These generally sound "sad," mysterious, or tense. They are excellent for creating emotional longing or drama. How to Pluck a Chord Unlike playing a single note, playing a chord requires a coordinated movement of multiple fingers. The Grip: Ensure your picks are seated firmly. To play a triad, you will typically use your thumb, index, and middle fingers simultaneously. The Attack: Aim to strike all three strings at the exact same moment. If one note lags behind, the chord will sound "muddy" rather than crisp. The Release: Because the koto strings vibrate for a long time, be mindful of how long you let the chord ring. You may occasionally use the damping techniques learned in Chapter 6 to stop a chord from bleeding into the next one. 8. Playing with Dynamics and Expression

Breathing Life into the Strings Imagine two people playing the exact same piece of music. The first player hits every note at the same volume, with the same mechanical precision, like a metronome. The second player begins a phrase softly, lets the volume swell like a wave in the middle, and allows the final note to fade into a whisper. Even though the notes are identical, the second player is telling a story; the first is simply reciting a list. Up until now, your focus has been on "what" to play—the correct strings, the right tabs, and the proper finger placement. Now, we shift our focus to "how" to play. This is the transition from playing notes to making music. In the world of the koto, this is where the instrument stops being a wooden box with strings and begins to function as an extension of your own voice. Mastering Volume and Tone In music, dynamics refers to the variation in loudness between notes and phrases. On the koto, dynamics are not just about how hard you pluck, but where and how you interact with the string. Controlling Volume (Loud and Soft) To vary your volume, you must consciously control the force and depth of your pluck. Piano (Soft): To play softly, reduce the distance your pick travels. Instead of a deep, sweeping motion, use a shallow, delicate flick of the finger. Imagine you are barely skimming the surface of the string. Forte (Loud): To play loudly, engage more of your arm and wrist. A deeper, more decisive pluck that displaces the string further will create a more powerful sound. However, be careful not to "hit" the string harshly, which can result in a percussive "thwack" rather than a resonant tone. Shaping the Tone (Bright and Dark) While dynamics deal with volume, tone (or timbre) deals with the quality of the sound. You can change the "color" of a note by altering your plucking point. 1. Bright Tone: Pluck the string closer to the bridge (the ji). The sound will be sharper, more piercing, and have a quicker decay. This is useful for highlights or moments of tension. 2. Dark/Warm Tone: Pluck the string further away from the bridge, moving toward the center of the instrument. The sound becomes rounder, mellower, and more "hollow." This is ideal for melancholic passages or gentle introductions. Scenario: The Emotional Shift Imagine you are playing a piece that describes a storm clearing. You might start with Forte plucks near the bridge to simulate the crashing of thunder (Bright/Loud), and as the sun comes out, transition to Piano plucks toward the center of the strings to simulate a calm breeze (Dark/Soft). 9. Intermediate Plucking Techniques: Finger Roll and Tremolo

Adding Texture and Motion to Your Sound Imagine listening to a recording of a master koto player. You’ll notice that the music isn't just a series of isolated notes; it often sounds like a shimmering waterfall or a humming vibration that sustains a single note far longer than a standard pluck would allow. These "shimmering" effects are not magic—they are the result of specific mechanical techniques that move the hand and fingers faster than a standard melody. Up until now, you have focused on precision: hitting one string at a time or playing a clear chord. Now, we shift our focus from precision to fluidity. By mastering the finger roll and the tremolo, you transition from playing simple melodies to creating atmospheric textures. The Finger Roll (Kumi-uchi) The finger roll, known in Japanese as kumi-uchi, is a technique used to play a rapid succession of notes, usually ascending or descending. Instead of thinking of these as separate notes, think of the finger roll as a single, fluid gesture. It creates an arpeggio-like effect, where the notes bleed into one another to create a wash of sound. The Mechanics of the Roll In basic plucking, you use a deliberate "pluck-and-release" motion. In kumi-uchi, you utilize the natural momentum of your hand. 1. The Setup: Position your hand over the group of strings you intend to play. Your wrist should be relaxed and slightly elevated, similar to the posture you learned in the early chapters. 2. The "Raking" Motion: Rather than lifting each finger individually and placing it back down, you allow the fingers to "roll" across the strings. 3. The Sequence: For an ascending roll, you typically start with the thumb, followed by the index and middle fingers in quick succession. 4. The Follow-Through: The movement should feel like you are brushing your fingers across the strings, moving from the outer strings toward the center (or vice versa). Common Pitfalls to Avoid The "Staccato" Trap: Beginner learners often try to "hit" each note with too much force, which makes the roll sound like a series of disconnected pops. The goal is a smooth, connected stream of sound. Tension in the Wrist: If your wrist is stiff, the roll will sound clunky. If you feel a "catch" in your movement, stop, shake out your hand, and restart with a lighter touch. Over-Plucking: Avoid digging the picks too deep into the strings. A shallow, grazing touch creates the most professional, "water-like" sound. Exercise: The Three-String Wave To practice kumi-uchi, we will start with a simple three-string sequence. 1. The Soul of the Koto: From Village Squares to Imperial Courts Imagine standing in a quiet Japanese garden during the autumn peak. Traditional koto music is generally divided into two worlds: the Min'yō (folk songs) of the common people and the Sōkyoku (classical compositions) of the refined arts. Understanding the difference between these two is key to how you approach the instrument. The Folk Tradition: Min'yō Min'yō refers to the traditional folk music of Japan. Historically, these songs were the heartbeat of rural life, used during rice planting, fishing, or local festivals. Because they were often sung by people while they worked, the melodies are catchy, rhythmic, and deeply tied to the geography of Japan. When playing a folk piece on the koto, the goal is not clinical perfection, but "flavor." You want to evoke the atmosphere of the region the song comes from. 11. Improvisation and Creative Playing on the Koto

The Courage to Play "Wrong" Imagine you are sitting with your koto. For the last ten chapters, you have followed the map: you read the tablature, you placed your fingers exactly where the notation told you, and you played the notes in the precise order intended by the composer. But what happens if you decide to take a detour? What if you play a note simply because it feels right in the moment, rather than because it is written on the page? This is the essence of improvisation: the act of composing music spontaneously while performing it. For many beginners, the word "improvisation" feels intimidating. It sounds like something reserved for jazz masters or virtuosos. However, improvisation is actually the most natural way humans interact with sound. When you hum a tune in the shower or whistle a random melody, you are improvising. On the koto, improvisation allows you to move from being a "player of pieces" to a "creator of music," using the instrument as a direct extension of your emotions. The Safety Net: The Pentatonic Scale The fear of hitting a "wrong" note is the biggest hurdle to creative playing. The secret to overcoming this is using a Pentatonic Scale. A pentatonic scale is a musical scale consisting of five notes per octave. Unlike the standard seven-note Western scale, the pentatonic scale is designed such that almost any combination of its notes sounds harmonious. In many traditional Japanese styles, the koto is tuned to a pentatonic scale, meaning the "wrong" notes are effectively removed from the board. Understanding Your Scale If your koto is tuned to a standard pentatonic scale (such as the Hira-jōshi scale), you have a built-in safety net. Because the intervals between the notes are mathematically predisposed to sound pleasing together, you can pluck strings in almost any order without creating harsh dissonance. To begin improvising, try this exercise: 1. Limit your range: Pick five strings (one octave) on your koto. 2. Randomized plucking: Pluck any of those five strings in no particular order. 3. Listen: Notice how, regardless of the sequence, the music maintains a consistent "mood" or atmosphere. By restricting yourself to a specific scale, you free your mind from the technical worry of "correctness" and allow yourself to focus on "expression." Building Melodic Phrases Improvisation is not just about playing random notes; it is about creating phrases. A phrase is like a sentence in a conversation. It has a beginning, a middle, and an ending. If you only play random notes, the music sounds like a list of ingredients. If you play phrases, the music tells a story. 12. Caring for Your Koto and Maintenance

The Silent Investment: Why Maintenance Matters Imagine you have finally mastered the intricate finger rolls and expressive glissandos discussed in earlier chapters. You sit down to play "Rokudan no Shirabe," but as you pluck the first string, it snaps with a sharp crack. Or perhaps you notice a dull, muffled tone that wasn't there yesterday, only to discover that the wood of your instrument has developed a hairline crack due to a sudden change in room temperature. A koto is more than just a musical instrument; it is a living piece of art crafted from kiri (paulownia wood). Because it is made of organic materials, it breathes, expands, and contracts based on its environment. Unlike a digital keyboard, a koto requires a relationship of stewardship. Proper care doesn't just prevent expensive repairs—it preserves the "voice" of the instrument, ensuring that the resonance remains crisp and the tuning stays stable. Storage and Environmental Control The greatest enemy of the koto is instability. Rapid shifts in temperature and humidity can cause the paulownia wood to warp or crack, and can cause the strings to slip or lose tension. Managing Humidity and Temperature The koto is highly sensitive to the air around it. Avoid Heat Sources: Never place your koto directly next to a radiator, heater, or in direct sunlight. Extreme heat dries out the wood, leading to cracks (splitting) and potentially causing the glue in the body to fail. Avoid Dampness: High humidity can cause the wood to swell and may lead to mold or mildew. Avoid storing the instrument in basements or near humidifiers. The "Goldilocks" Zone: Aim for a stable, moderate indoor temperature. If you live in a climate with extreme seasons, using a room humidifier in the winter or a dehumidifier in the summer can protect the instrument. Proper Resting Positions When not in use, your koto should be stored in a way that minimizes stress on the frame. The Koto Case: Whenever possible, store the koto in its designated hard case. This provides a physical barrier against dust and accidental bumps. Flat Surface: If the koto is kept out on a stand, ensure the stand is sturdy and level. The "Plateau" and How to Break Through Imagine you have spent the last few weeks mastering "Rokudan no Shirabe" and feeling confident with your plucking techniques. Suddenly, you hit a wall. You feel like you are playing the same three songs on loop, your fingers aren't getting faster, and you aren't sure if your expression is actually improving or if you've just become used to the sound. This is the "intermediate plateau," and it is a natural part of every musician's journey. The difference between those who stop playing and those who become lifelong koto practitioners is not innate talent, but the transition from guided learning (following a book or a teacher) to autonomous practice (designing your own growth). Moving forward requires a shift in mindset: you are no longer just learning "how to play," but are now deciding "what kind of player" you want to be. Designing Your Personalized Practice Routine Consistency beats intensity. Practicing for 20 minutes every day is significantly more effective for muscle memory than practicing for five hours once a week. Because the koto requires specific physical coordination—balancing the precision of the picks with the fluid movement of the left hand for damping and glissando—your routine should be balanced. The Three-Tier Practice Structure To ensure you don't neglect the fundamentals while chasing new songs, divide your practice sessions into three distinct phases: 1. The Warm-Up (5–10 Minutes): Physical Calibration Finger Independence: Play simple ascending and descending scales to wake up your picking hand. Left-Hand Flexibility: Practice simple pitch bends and damping exercises to loosen the wrist. Tuning Check: Always ensure your bridges are correctly placed and the strings are in tune before you begin; practicing on a koto that is slightly out of tune trains your ear to accept incorrect pitches. 2. The "Work" Session (20–40 Minutes): Deliberate Practice Targeted Troubleshooting: Don't just play the whole song from start to finish. Instead, isolate the "problem measure"—the specific two or three notes where you always stumble—and play them slowly and perfectly ten times in a row. Technique Drilling: Spend time on the more complex movements introduced in earlier chapters, such as the finger roll or tremolo, applying them to a simple melody to build confidence. Notation Fluency: Practice reading your Tablature in real-time without humming the melody first. This strengthens the direct link between the page and your fingers. 3. The "Play" Session (10–20 Minutes): Creative Exploration Improvisation: Use the creative techniques from the improvisation chapter to "jam" with yourself. Explore different moods or try to evoke a specific image (like rain or a mountain landscape). Repertoire Review: Play a piece you have already mastered.
