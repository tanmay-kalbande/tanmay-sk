1. Introduction to Sourdough and Pizza Basics

The Living Ecosystem in Your Flour Imagine tearing into a slice of pizza where the crust is encased in a blistered, dark-gold shell, giving way to a soft, airy interior that tastes faintly of toasted nuts and warm bread. It has a depth of flavor that makes you pause. You aren't just tasting tomato and cheese; you are tasting the dough itself. This complex, satisfying flavor profile is the hallmark of sourdough pizza, and it comes from a process that predates modern baking by thousands of years. If you have ever baked with packets of commercial yeast, you are likely familiar with the reliable, predictable rise it provides. Sourdough takes a different approach. Instead of relying on a single, laboratory-isolated strain of yeast, sourdough relies on a thriving, living ecosystem that you cultivate in your own kitchen. At the heart of this ecosystem is the sourdough starter—a simple mixture of flour and water left to ferment. To understand why a sourdough starter transforms pizza dough into something extraordinary, we need to look at the microscopic life forms that call it home. Wild Yeast: The Natural Leavening Agent When you mix flour and water and leave it on your counter, you are creating an environment for wild yeast to wake up. Wild yeast is everywhere: in the air we breathe, on the surfaces of our kitchens, and most importantly, inside the grains of wheat used to make flour. Unlike commercial yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), which is bred specifically for rapid, aggressive gas production, wild yeast strains (such as Candida milleri or various Saccharomyces species found in flour) are more relaxed. They work slowly and steadily. When wild yeast feeds on the natural sugars present in flour, it exhales carbon dioxide gas. In pizza dough, these gas bubbles get trapped in the gluten network (the stretchy web formed when flour and water mix), causing the dough to rise and creating the light, holey structure we crave in a great crust. Lactobacilli: The Flavor and Texture Makers Yeast is only half the story. Sharing the sourdough starter’s habitat are lactobacilli—a type of lactic acid bacteria. These bacteria are the unsung heroes of fermentation. As they consume the same sugars as the yeast, they produce two types of acid: lactic acid and acetic acid. Lactic acid provides a mild, yogurt-like tang. It also helps relax the gluten in the dough, making it easier to stretch out into a pizza base without it springing back. Acetic acid is sharper and more acidic, providing that distinct "sour" punch. It is more prevalent when the starter is kept in cooler environments. 2. Creating and Maintaining a Sourdough Starter

The Living Heart of Your Pizza Dough Imagine opening a clear container on your kitchen counter and being greeted by a bubbly, frothy mixture that smells distinctly of tangy yogurt and toasted wheat. You scoop out a spoonful, mix it with fresh flour and water, and a few hours later, it has doubled in size. You haven't added a single grain of commercial yeast. What you are witnessing is a microscopic metropolis in action—the sourdough starter. As we discussed in the introduction, a sourdough starter is a living culture of wild yeast and lactobacilli. These microbes work together to ferment your dough, producing carbon dioxide gas for lift, and lactic acid and acetic acid for that signature tangy flavor. For home pizza bakers, this biological powerhouse is what unlocks deep, complex flavor, improved dough extensibility, and an enhanced crust texture that commercial yeast simply cannot match. But to get those benefits, you need a healthy, active starter. Building one from scratch requires nothing more than flour, water, and a bit of patience. Building Your Starter From Scratch Creating a sourdough starter is a process of cultivation. Flour naturally contains dormant wild yeast and lactobacilli; water wakes them up. By providing a consistent supply of fresh food (flour) and a moist environment (water), you encourage the beneficial microbes to multiply and dominate. While you can eventually maintain your starter at different consistencies, the easiest environment for a beginner to build and monitor is a 100% hydration starter. This simply means your starter is made of equal parts flour and water by weight. The First Seven Days Building a starter takes roughly 7 to 10 days. The process involves "feedings"—discarding a portion of the culture and adding fresh flour and water. To ensure accuracy, you will use the digital kitchen scale introduced in Chapter 1. Measuring by volume (cups) is highly inaccurate because flour compacts; weighing in grams is the gold standard. Day 1: The Foundation 1. Mix thoroughly with your spatula or dough whisk until no dry flour remains. It will look like a thick, sticky paste. 4. 3. Flour, Hydration, and Baker's Percentages

The Flour Spectrum: Choosing Your Foundation Imagine pulling a pizza from your home oven. The crust is beautifully golden, but when you take a bite, it’s tough, dense, and requires serious jaw strength to chew. What went wrong? You followed the recipe, timed your fermentation perfectly, and used your healthy, active sourdough starter. The culprit almost certainly isn't your technique—it’s your flour. In Chapter 1, we briefly touched on flour as the structural backbone of your dough. But not all flours are created equal. The type of flour you choose dictates how much water your dough can absorb, how it stretches, and how the final crust textures will develop. To understand why, we need to look at what flour actually is. Protein and Gluten: The Building Blocks When you mix flour and water, two proteins in the flour—glutenin and gliadin—combine to form gluten. As you mix and knead, these gluten proteins link up to create a stretchy, web-like network. Think of this network as the scaffolding of your pizza. It traps the carbon dioxide produced by the wild yeast in your sourdough starter, allowing the dough to rise. The strength and elasticity of this scaffolding determine whether your crust will be tender and airy or dense and cracker-like. Different flours contain different amounts of these proteins. Generally, the higher the protein content, the stronger the gluten network. For pizza, we are looking for a sweet spot: high enough to stretch into a circle without tearing, but not so high that the dough snaps back like a rubber band. Decoding the Flour Aisle Walk down the baking aisle of any grocery store, and you’ll see a wall of options. Here is how the three most common flours compare for pizza making. All-Purpose (AP) Flour Protein Content: Typically 9% to 11% Characteristics: As the name suggests, AP flour is designed to do a bit of everything, from cookies to pancakes to bread. For Pizza: You can make pizza with AP flour, and it is a great accessible starting point. However, because its protein content is on the lower end, it doesn't form a very strong gluten network. It also doesn't absorb much water. A pizza made with AP flour will be softer and less chewy. If you stretch it too thin, it is prone to tearing. Bread Flour Protein Content: Typically 12% to 14% Characteristics: Bread flour is milled specifically for, well, bread. It has a higher protein content that creates a strong, resilient dough. For Pizza: Bread flour is an excellent choice for home pizza makers. It creates a sturdy crust with a satisfying chew. 4. Mixing, Kneading, and Bulk Fermentation

The Alchemy of Mixing: Water Meets Flour Imagine sitting down to eat a slice of your homemade sourdough pizza. You take a bite, and instead of a satisfying, chewy crust, the dough tears in your mouth like wet paper. It has no structure, no air pockets, and it sits heavy in your stomach. What went wrong? The ingredients were perfectly measured, but the magic of pizza dough doesn’t happen just in the measuring—it happens in the mixing, the kneading, and the waiting. In the previous modules, you built a thriving sourdough starter and learned the mathematical language of baker’s percentages. Now, it is time to bring those elements together. Mixing is the moment your flour, water, salt, and starter physically merge. But it is also the beginning of a biological process. When water hits flour, two critical things happen simultaneously. First, the water hydrates the starches and proteins in the flour. Second, two specific proteins—glutenin and gliadin—begin to bond together to form gluten. Gluten is the invisible, elastic web that gives pizza dough its signature chew and its ability to stretch without tearing (extensibility). If you were making a traditional loaf of bread, you might aggressively knead the dough on a counter for ten minutes to force this gluten network into existence. But for sourdough pizza, especially at the higher hydrations we discussed in Module 3, aggressive kneading can make the dough tight, tough, and difficult to stretch into a pizza base later. Instead, we rely on a gentler, more time-efficient method. The Autolyse: A Brief Pause Before we mix everything together, many sourdough bakers use a technique called an autolyse (pronounced auto-leeze). An autolyse is simply the act of mixing just your flour and water together, then letting it rest for 20 to 60 minutes before adding your salt and sourdough starter. During this short rest, the water fully saturates the flour. More importantly, enzymes naturally present in the flour begin breaking down the proteins, giving them a massive head start on gluten development. An autolyse also makes the dough feel incredibly smooth and stretchy, which will make the subsequent steps much easier. While optional, an autolyse is highly recommended for beginner sourdough bakers because it makes the dough easier to handle and reduces the amount of physical kneading required. The Initial Mix Once your flour and water have rested (or if you are skipping the autolyse and mixing everything at once), it is time to add your active sourdough starter and salt. 5. Dividing, Balling, and Cold Fermentation

From Bulk to Balls: Setting the Stage for Great Pizza You’ve mixed your flour, water, salt, and sourdough starter. You’ve kneaded the dough and let it rest through bulk fermentation, watching it grow airy, jiggly, and alive. Right now, you have a single, large mass of fermented dough. But you aren’t making one giant pizza—you are making several. The transition from one large bulk dough to individual pizza crusts is a surprisingly delicate process. If you handle the dough too roughly right now, you will destroy the fragile gas bubbles that built up during bulk fermentation. If you leave it out on the counter too long, the wild yeast and lactobacilli will consume all the available sugars, leading to an over-fermented, sour-tasting, unmanageable mess. This stage requires you to act decisively. You need to divide the dough, shape it into tight, round balls, and drop the temperature rapidly to hit the pause button on fermentation. Mastering this sequence gives you individual portions that are easy to stretch later, and a cold rest that transforms the flavor and texture of your final crust. Dividing the Bulk Dough When your dough has finished bulk fermentation, it is full of trapped carbon dioxide gas. Think of the dough as a delicate sponge. Your goal during dividing is to portion the dough without punching all the air out of it. To do this, you will use your Dough Scraper (Bench Scraper). This rigid, flat tool is the perfect extension of your hand because it cuts cleanly through the dough without tearing it the way your fingers would. Calculating Your Portion Sizes Before you make your first cut, you need to know how much each dough ball should weigh. This depends entirely on the size of the pizzas you want to make and the thickness of your final crust. A standard home oven pizza, typically baked on a stone or steel, usually requires a dough ball weighing between 250 and 280 grams. This will give you roughly a 10- to 12-inch pizza. If you want larger, 14-inch pizzas, you might aim for 300 to 320 grams per ball. To figure out how many portions you can get from your bulk dough, simply weigh the entire mass on your Digital Kitchen Scale, then divide by your desired portion size. For example, if your bulk dough weighs 1,000 grams, and you want 250-gram pizzas, you will divide the dough into four equal pieces. The Dividing Technique When dividing, precision matters. If one dough ball is 200 grams and another is 300 grams, they will stretch to completely different sizes later, making your baking times inconsistent. 6. Dough Stretching and Shaping

The Magic Moment Picture this: You open your refrigerator. Sitting inside is a container holding the sourdough pizza dough you carefully mixed, kneaded, and balled days ago. During its cold fermentation, the wild yeast and lactobacilli have been slowly working away, transforming a simple mixture of flour, water, salt, and starter into a living, breathing matrix of flavor. Now, it is time for the magic moment—the moment you transform that dense, sleepy ball into a light, airy, circular pizza base. For many home bakers, stretching dough is the most intimidating part of the pizza-making process. We have all experienced the frustration of dough that snaps back like a rubber band, or worse, dough that tears a giant hole right in the center. But stretching pizza dough is not a test of brute strength. It is a gentle negotiation. Your goal is to coax the dough outward, using gravity and gentle pressure, while fiercely protecting the gas bubbles created during fermentation. These bubbles are the secret to a light, airy crust. By the end of this chapter, you will understand exactly how to prepare your dough balls, how to use your hands to form a perfect circle, and how to move that delicate base onto a pizza peel without losing its shape. Preparing Your Dough Balls for the Stretch You cannot stretch cold dough. If you pull a dough ball straight from the fridge and try to stretch it, two things will happen: it will aggressively resist your touch (snapping back immediately), and it will tear rather than expand. To stretch successfully, you must bring your dough to the correct temperature. This process is called tempering. The Tempering Process Tempering means allowing the cold dough to rest at room temperature until it relaxes and becomes pliable. The internal temperature you are aiming for is around 65°F to 70°F (18°C to 21°C). 1. Prepare your surface: Lightly dust your work surface with a fine layer of flour. All-purpose flour or bread flour works well here. Do not use semolina yet; save that for the pizza peel. 2. Remove from the container: Gently transfer your dough balls from their cold fermentation containers to the floured surface. Leave several inches of space between them, as they will spread slightly as they warm. 3. Cover loosely: Place a lightly oiled piece of plastic wrap, or an inverted bowl, loosely over the dough. 7. Sourcing and Preparing Ingredients

The Canvas and the Paint: Why Ingredients Matter You have spent days feeding your sourdough starter, carefully mixing your dough, calculating baker's percentages, and patiently waiting through bulk fermentation and cold fermentation. You have learned how to coax that cool, fermented dough into a beautifully stretched circle. But what happens next can either elevate that carefully crafted crust or destroy it. A sourdough crust is a delicate balance of air pockets, fermentation flavors, and structural integrity. If you pile it with heavy, wet ingredients, the dough will collapse into a soggy, dense pancake. If you bury it under too many competing flavors, the complex, tangy notes of your wild yeast and lactobacilli will completely disappear. The ingredients you put on top of your pizza are just as important as the flour and water you put into it. Sourcing the right tomatoes, choosing the correct cheese, and preparing your toppings to manage moisture are the final, critical steps before your pizza ever sees the heat of the oven. The Classic No-Cook Tomato Sauce A common mistake beginners make is treating pizza sauce like pasta sauce. Pasta sauce is simmered for hours to develop deep, caramelized flavors and evaporate liquid. Pizza sauce, however, lives on a pizza for only a few minutes under intense heat. If you cook it beforehand, it will overcook in the oven, losing its bright, fresh flavor. The secret to a vibrant pizza sauce is using high-quality ingredients and leaving them completely raw. Why San Marzano Tomatoes? When sourcing tomatoes for your sauce, you will frequently see the term San Marzano. San Marzano tomatoes are a specific variety of plum tomato grown in the volcanic soil at the base of Mount Vesuvius in Italy. They are prized for having fewer seeds, less water, a thicker flesh, and a naturally sweet, low-acid flavor profile. Because the sauce is raw, the quality of the tomato is the quality of the sauce—you cannot hide mediocre tomatoes behind long cooking times or heavy spices. When shopping, look for cans labeled "D.O.P." (Denominazione d'Origine Protetta). This is an Italian certification guaranteeing that the tomatoes were actually grown and processed in the San Marzano region. If you cannot find D.O.P. San Marzanos, look for high-quality, canned plum tomatoes grown in California or Italy. Avoid diced tomatoes, which are treated with calcium chloride to keep them firm and will not break down into a smooth sauce. 8. Baking in a Home Oven

The Home Oven Challenge Professional pizzerias use wood-fired brick ovens or specialized deck ovens that reach temperatures between 600°F and 900°F (315°C to 480°C). At these extreme temperatures, a pizza can cook in as little as 90 seconds, producing a crust that is simultaneously crisp on the outside and beautifully soft and airy on the inside. Your standard home oven, however, likely tops out at 500°F or 550°F (260°C to 290°C). If you simply put a pizza on a standard baking sheet and slide it into a 500°F oven, the dough will slowly bake, drying out and turning into a hard, cracker-like crust long before the toppings are fully cooked. The sourdough flavor you worked so hard to develop in earlier chapters will be lost in a tough, dry bite. To bridge the gap between a commercial oven and a home kitchen, we have to use thermal mass—the ability of a heavy material to absorb, hold, and transfer intense heat. By preheating a heavy baking surface, we create a searing hot environment that instantly shocks the dough, mimicking the floor of a professional pizza oven. Combined with a specific oven setup, you can achieve a perfectly baked sourdough pizza in a standard kitchen. Choosing Your Baking Surface The surface you bake your pizza on is the single most important factor in your home oven setup. You have two primary options for your baking surface: a pizza stone or a baking steel. Both serve the same purpose—holding intense heat to transfer to the dough—but they do so in slightly different ways. The Pizza Stone A pizza stone (often made of cordierite or firebrick) is a thick, porous ceramic slab. How it works: It absorbs heat slowly and releases it steadily. The porous nature also draws moisture out of the bottom of the dough, helping to create a crispy base. Pros: Affordable, widely available, and excellent for making crispy crusts. Cons: Ceramic is brittle and can crack if exposed to sudden temperature changes (thermal shock). It also takes longer to fully heat up. The Baking Steel A baking steel is a solid slab of food-grade steel, usually around 1/4 to 1/2 inch thick. How it works: Metal conducts heat much faster and more efficiently than ceramic. Because steel is so highly conductive, it transfers a massive amount of heat to the dough in seconds. Pros: Cooks pizzas incredibly fast, yielding a crust that closely mimics a professional wood-fired oven. It is virtually indestructible and won't crack. Cons: More expensive than a stone. 9. Troubleshooting and Advanced Adjustments

You pull your pizza from the oven. The crust looks beautifully golden, and the kitchen smells incredible. But when you take that first bite, the experience falls flat. The outer crust is tough and exhausting to chew, and the center of the pie is gummy and wet. You followed the recipe exactly—so what went wrong? Welcome to the reality of sourdough baking. Because a sourdough starter is a living ecosystem of wild yeast and lactobacilli, it does not behave like commercial yeast. A recipe is not a set of rigid rules; it is a baseline. To consistently bake excellent pizza, you must learn to read the dough and adjust on the fly. This module is your diagnostic toolkit for fixing common baking issues and adapting your dough to ever-changing variables. Diagnosing and Fixing Common Dough and Baking Issues When a pizza doesn't turn out right, the problem usually falls into one of three categories: fermentation, hydration, or baking technique. Let's break down how to identify and fix the most frequent culprits. The Gummy Crumb A gummy crumb refers to the inside of the crust (the crumb) being wet, sticky, and undercooked, rather than light and airy. The Causes: The most common cause is under-fermentation. If the wild yeast hasn't had enough time to process the starches and develop the gluten structure, the dough won't bake through properly. Alternatively, the dough might be too wet (high hydration) for your home oven to bake through in time, or the oven simply wasn't hot enough. The Fix: First, check your oven temperature. Home ovens are notoriously inaccurate. Invest in an oven thermometer to ensure your baking surface is fully preheated. If your oven is accurate, you may need to extend your bulk fermentation or cold fermentation times. Finally, if you are baking at 60% hydration or higher and still getting a gumming center, try dropping your water content by 5% next time. Overly Chewy or Tough Crust While a good sourdough crust should have a satisfying bite, it should never require a trip to the dentist. The Causes: An overly chewy crust is almost always a result of either too much gluten development or over-fermentation. If you kneaded the dough too aggressively during the mixing phase, the gluten becomes overly tight. Conversely, if the dough over-ferments, the lactic acid and acetic acid produced by the lactobacilli will begin to break down the gluten network, resulting in a dense, rubbery texture once baked. The Fix: If you are kneading by hand, switch to a gentler approach, relying on the "stretch and fold" method to build strength over time rather than forcing it.
