1. Introduction to Origami: Tools and Materials

What is Origami, Really? Origami is the art of paper folding—simple in concept, profound in practice. At its heart, origami transforms a flat sheet of paper into a three-dimensional shape using only folding techniques, with no cutting or gluing. But beyond the mechanics lies a deeper story: a tradition passed down for centuries, a meditative practice, a symbol of patience and persistence, and a universal language of creativity. Imagine holding a single square of paper. In your hands, it could become a crane—a bird symbolizing peace and longevity in Japanese culture. Or it could fold into a jumping frog, a blooming flower, or a sleek airplane. The same sheet might even tell a story, convey emotion, or become a gift for someone you care about. Origami turns the ordinary into the extraordinary—one fold at a time. This book begins with the crane because it is one of the most iconic origami models in the world. But before you fold even one corner, you need to understand what tools and materials will support your journey—and how to care for them. After all, the crane that flies in your imagination can only take flight in reality if your paper is crisp, your hands are steady, and your workspace is ready. So let’s start where every crane begins: with the paper in your hands. --- The Paper: Your Foundation At the heart of every origami model is the paper. But not all paper is created equal—especially when it comes to folding. The right paper can make the difference between a crisp, elegant crane and a crumpled, frustrating mess. What Makes Origami Paper Special? Origami paper is typically lightweight, thin, and strong, designed to fold cleanly without tearing. It’s often double-sided, meaning it has a different color or pattern on each side, which adds visual depth to your models. While you can use regular paper in a pinch (more on that later), origami paper is engineered for precision. It’s usually square and comes in standard sizes like 15 cm x 15 cm (6 inches x 6 inches), though smaller and larger sheets are also available. Key characteristics of good origami paper: - Weight: Measured in grams per square meter (gsm). Lighter paper (typically 50–70 gsm) is best for beginners because it’s easier to fold and less likely to tear. - Finish: Most origami paper has a smooth, crisp texture that allows folds to hold their shape. - Size: Standard sizes range from 7.5 cm (3 inches) for tiny models to 20 cm (8 inches) for larger ones. 2. Origami Basics: Folds and Symbols

The First Fold: Turning Paper Into a Crane Imagine you are sitting at a quiet kitchen table, a fresh sheet of kami paper in front of you, and a simple goal: fold a crane. The first thing you reach for isn’t a complicated diagram or a special tool—it’s the most basic movement you can make with the paper: a fold. Whether the paper bends upward like a tiny mountain or downward like a quiet valley, that single action determines every line that follows. Mastering these two elementary folds, and learning how they are represented in the symbols that guide you, is the foundation for every origami model you will ever create. In this chapter we will: Define the valley fold and mountain fold, and show how to execute each with confidence. Decode the most common origami symbols—arrows, dashed lines, and dotted lines—so you can read any beginner diagram. Practice making clean, precise creases and understand the difference between a fold (the act) and a crease (the result). Apply these folds to produce simple geometric shapes—squares and triangles—that become the building blocks of the classic crane base. --- 1. Valley and Mountain Folds: The Two Faces of Paper 1.1 What Is a Fold? A fold is the action you perform on the paper: you bring one edge toward another, or you bend the sheet along a line you have imagined. After you release the paper, the line you created is called a crease. Think of a fold as the motion, and a crease as the memory left behind. Quick tip: A clean crease is the secret to crisp, sharp models. Rough, uneven creases make the paper look sloppy and can cause later folds to misalign. 1.2 Valley Fold (V‑Fold) A valley fold makes the paper bend downward, forming a V‑shaped trough. When you look at the model from the side, the crease resembles a valley. How to perform a valley fold: 1. Identify the line where you want the paper to bend. Most diagrams will mark this line with a solid black line and an arrow indicating the direction of the fold. 2. Pinch the two edges that meet at the line with your thumb and forefinger. 3. Bring the edges together so the paper folds toward you (or away, depending on orientation). The paper should lie flat on the table, with the folded side facing upward. 4. Press firmly along the entire length of the line using the heel of your hand or a bone folder (if you have one). Hold the pressure for a few seconds to set the crease. The Crane in History and Symbolism 1.1 From Folklore to Paper Art The crane (鶴, tsuru) has long been a creature of myth in East Asia. In Japanese folklore, cranes are said to live for a thousand years and bring good fortune. The practice of folding a crane from a single sheet of paper—origami—emerged during the Edo period (1603‑1868) when paper became more affordable. Early manuals such as Senbazuru Orikata (1910) popularized the crane as a symbol of perseverance and hope. 1.2 Modern Cultural Meaning - Peace and Healing: After World War II, the crane became an international emblem of peace, especially after the story of Sadako Sasaki, who folded 1,000 paper cranes while battling leukemia caused by the atomic bomb. - Wish‑Making: In many Japanese customs, a single crane is offered as a prayer for health, success, or safe travel. - Collective Projects: The “thousand‑crane” (senbazuru) tradition—folding a thousand cranes and tying them together—remains a popular group activity in schools and community centers. Understanding this cultural backdrop adds purpose to each precise fold you will learn. The crane base is not merely a technical step; it is the foundation of a tradition that carries wishes across generations. --- 2. Spotting the First Signs Before you even begin to fix a problem, you need to recognize it. Common Issues and Their Causes 2.1 Uneven Wings - Root cause: One side of the valley fold (the fold that opens inward) is sharper than the other, or the paper has been stretched during a previous fold. - Result: One wing droops while the other stays level, breaking the bird’s iconic silhouette. 2.2 Crooked Beak - Root cause: The mountain fold that creates the beak’s tip was not aligned with the central axis of the base, or the paper slipped while folding. - Result: The beak points off‑center, making the crane look lopsided. 2.3 Floppy Paper / Soft Tail - Root cause: Paper that is too thin, overly damp, or has lost its crisp memory from repeated creasing. - Result: The tail and other fine points cannot hold their shape, giving the crane a limp appearance. 2.4 Misaligned Body - Root cause: Inconsistent paper tension—some sections are pulled tighter than others during the base folds. - Result: The whole crane leans or wobbles, and the wings may not sit flat. Understanding these causes lets you choose the right corrective technique rather than guessing. --- 3. Strengthening Weak Creases A strong crease is the backbone of a crisp crane. Extending the Classic Pattern The classic crane pattern is a versatile foundation. Most variations require only small, targeted changes to the base folds. Below are three of the most rewarding modifications for beginners. 2.1. Long‑Neck and Beak Shapes A longer neck gives the crane an elegant, swan‑like silhouette, while a differently shaped beak can suggest a different bird species or a stylized look. Why it works The neck and beak are formed from the same set of valley folds that create the head. By unfolding (reversing) the final “head‑fold” and extending the underlying creases, you can redistribute paper length without disturbing the wings. Step‑by‑step modification 1. Finish the classic crane through the steps in the previous chapter, but stop before the final head‑fold (the small reverse‑fold that creates the beak). 2. Locate the central valley crease that runs from the head down the body to the tail. 3. Gently pull the tip of the head outward, aligning the crease with the existing wing edge. You will notice a small triangular flap that can be slid forward. 4. Lock the new neck position by making a tiny inside reverse‑fold (a “crimp fold”) at the point where the neck meets the body. This fold secures the neck length without adding bulk. 5. Shape the beak: - Sharp beak: make a narrow inside reverse‑fold at the tip, folding the tip inward to a point. - Rounded beak: perform a gentle outside reverse‑fold that softens the tip into a curve. 8. Advanced Techniques: Modular and Linked Cranes

A Hand‑Made Marvel: From One Crane to a Hanging Garden You’ve already folded a single classic crane that can soar in a child’s imagination. Imagine now that you could turn a dozen of those cranes into a single, elegant mobile that sways gently in the breeze—a miniature garden of paper birds that can be gifted, displayed, or even used as a meditation aid. That transformation is the power of modular origami, where a repeatable unit is folded many times and then interlocked without any glue or tape. This chapter walks you through that process, from the very first Sonobe unit to a finished linked‑crane sculpture. --- 1. What Is Modular Origami? Modular origami (sometimes called unit origami) is a technique in which a single, usually simple, folded piece—called a module—is duplicated and assembled into a larger, often three‑dimensional structure. Unlike traditional origami, which uses one sheet to become a single model, modular origami relies on interlocking folds that hold the whole together. Why Use Modular Cranes? - Scalability – Add or remove units to change size or shape. - Strength – Interlocking edges distribute stress, making the final piece sturdy. - Creativity – One unit can be combined in countless ways: chains, rings, lattices, or sculptural forms. - Accessibility – The basic unit is easy to learn, so beginners can achieve impressive results quickly. The most popular building block for modular cranes is the Sonobe unit, named after its creator, Mitsuo Sonobe. Its geometry naturally forms the angles needed for a crane’s wings and tail, and it can be linked to create a single crane or an entire hanging mobile. --- 2. Choosing the Right Paper for Modular Cranes Before you start folding, pick a paper that will hold the repeated folds well. 9. Cultural and Symbolic Context of the Crane

In her hands she folds a single paper crane, then another, and another—until the pile grows into a fragile forest of white wings. The story of Sadako’s determination to fold senbazuru—one thousand origami cranes—has become a global symbol of hope, healing, and peace. Origins of the Legend - Senbazuru (千羽鶴) literally means “one thousand cranes.” - The belief that a crane can grant a wish or bring good health dates back to Heian period (794‑1185) poetry, where the crane was praised for its graceful flight and longevity. - The most widely known modern narrative is Sadako Sasaki’s, popularized by the 1977 book “Sadako and the Thousand Paper Cranes.” While the exact number she folded is debated, the legend endures because it links a simple paper fold to a profound human desire for recovery. Why One Thousand? - In traditional Japanese numerology, 1,000 (sen) represents completeness and abundance. - Folding a thousand cranes is a ritual of perseverance; the act itself becomes a meditation, reinforcing the wish’s intention. How the Legend Shapes Learning When you begin folding the classic crane (see Chapter 5), each fold is a step toward a larger narrative. Understanding the legend adds emotional depth to the mechanical process you practiced in Understanding the Traditional Crane Base and Folding the Classic Origami Crane. --- The Crane in Japanese Culture and Traditions The crane (鶴, tsuru) is more than a bird; it is a cultural keystone that appears in art, folklore, and ceremony.
