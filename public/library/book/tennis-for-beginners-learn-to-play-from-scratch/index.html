1. Tennis Fundamentals: Court, Equipment & Terminology

Stepping Onto the Court Picture this: You are standing at the back of a tennis court, the summer sun warming your back. Across the net stands your opponent, bouncing a fuzzy yellow ball. You hear the sharp squeak of their shoes on the hard surface, followed by the crisp pop of the ball hitting their racquet strings. The ball sails over the net, landing neatly inside the lines. You sprint forward, swing your racquet, and send it right back. It looks effortless when experienced players do it, but that single exchange relies on a deep understanding of the environment, the tools, and the language of the game. Before you can run, hit a forehand, or strategize a match, you need to know the stage you are playing on and the props you are using. Tennis is a sport of precision. Playing a ball that lands one inch outside a line means the difference between winning and losing a point. Using a racquet that is too heavy or a ball designed for the wrong surface can turn an enjoyable workout into a frustrating struggle. Furthermore, tennis carries a unique lexicon—words like "deuce," "let," and "ace" that sound like a foreign language to the uninitiated. By mastering the fundamentals of the court's geometry, selecting the right equipment, and learning the core terminology, you build a solid foundation for everything that follows. Let’s break down the anatomy of the game. The Anatomy of a Tennis Court A tennis court is a large rectangle divided by a net. While the exact dimensions are strictly regulated, the surface material can vary widely—from hard courts (like concrete or asphalt) to clay, grass, and even carpet. Regardless of the surface, the lines painted on the court dictate the rules of engagement. The Net and Net Post Running directly across the middle of the court is the net. It is attached to two net posts on either side. The net stands exactly 3 feet (0.914 meters) high at the center, where it is strapped down, and 3.5 feet (1.07 meters) high at the posts. The net is the ultimate barrier: every shot must clear it to stay in play. The Baseline The baseline is the thick line drawn parallel to the net at the furthest boundary of the court. This is the line you stand behind when you serve, and it generally serves as the home base for rallying (hitting the ball back and forth) during a point. If a ball lands beyond the baseline, it is considered "out." Singles vs. Doubles Sidelines Tennis can be played one-on-one (singles) or two-on-two (doubles). 2. Grips: How to Hold the Racquet

Why Your Grip Is the Foundation of Every Shot Imagine grabbing a tennis racquet like you’re holding a hammer and trying to hit a forehand. You quickly realize the face of the racquet is pointing sideways toward the fence. To get the strings facing the net, you have to contort your wrist at an unnatural angle. By the time you make contact with the ball, your arm is in a compromised position, and the ball flies erratically into the adjacent court. This scenario illustrates a fundamental truth in tennis: how you hold the racquet dictates the angle of the racquet face, which in turn dictates where the ball goes. A grip is not just about holding onto the equipment so it doesn't fly out of your hand; it is the mechanical bridge between your body and the ball. In Chapter 1, we discussed finding the right grip size for your hand to ensure comfort and prevent injury. Now, we must look at how you actually position your hand around that handle. There is no single "correct" way to hold a tennis racquet. Instead, there is a specific set of grips designed for specific strokes. Using the right grip for the right shot is the first critical step to playing effective tennis. The Anatomy of the Handle and the Bevel System Before learning the specific grips, you need to understand the physical shape of the racquet handle. If you look directly down at the end of the handle (the butt cap), you will notice it is not perfectly round. It is an octagon—a shape with eight flat sides. In tennis terminology, these eight flat sides are called bevels. To communicate effectively about grips, tennis players and coaches use a standard numbering system to identify these bevels. To find Bevel 1, hold the racquet out in front of you with the edge of the frame pointing straight up toward the sky (so the strings are facing sideways, perpendicular to the ground). The bevel facing straight up is Bevel 1. From there, the bevels are numbered clockwise around the handle: 1. Bevel 1: The top edge (facing the sky). 2. Bevel 2: The top-right side (upper-right diagonal). 3. Bevel 3: The right side (facing right). 4. Bevel 4: The bottom-right side (lower-right diagonal). 5. Bevel 5: The bottom edge (facing the ground). 6. Bevel 6: The bottom-left side (lower-left diagonal). 7. Bevel 7: The left side (facing left). 8. Bevel 8: The top-left side (upper-left diagonal). 3. Footwork & Movement: The Foundation of All Strokes

The Invisible Engine of Tennis Imagine watching a professional tennis match. Your eyes naturally follow the ball and the player swinging the racquet. But if you look only at the ball, you are missing half the game. The players who look the most effortless on the court are not necessarily the ones with the most aggressive swings; they are the ones who arrive at the right place, at the right time, with the best balance. Tennis is a game of constant disruption. Your opponent is actively trying to hit the ball away from you. To counter this, you must move. Before you can learn how to swing a forehand or a backhand—which we will cover in upcoming chapters—you must learn how to use your feet to put your body in a position where swinging is actually possible. Good footwork is the invisible engine that drives every stroke in tennis. The Ready Position: Your Starting Point Every single point in tennis begins from a specific physical state known as the ready position. Whether you are returning a serve or reacting to a groundstroke, the ready position is the foundation that allows you to move instantly in any direction. To execute the ready position: 1. Feet: Stand with your feet about shoulder-width apart. If you are right-handed, you can slightly stagger your feet, with your left foot a half-step ahead of your right. (Left-handers do the opposite). 2. Knees: Bend your knees slightly. You should feel a bit of tension in your thighs, as if you are about to sit back into a chair. 3. Posture: Keep your back relatively straight but lean slightly forward from the waist. Your weight should be distributed on the balls of your feet—not back on your heels. You should be able to lift your heels slightly off the ground. 4. Hands: Hold the racquet out in front of your body, gripping the throat (the narrow part of the handle just below the head) lightly with your non-dominant hand. Your dominant hand holds the grip you established in the previous chapter. Your elbows should be comfortably tucked near your hips, not flared out. The non-dominant hand plays a crucial role here. By resting it on the throat of the racquet, you take the weight of the racquet off your dominant arm, preventing premature fatigue. It also leaves your dominant hand free to quickly adjust its grip—shifting from a forehand grip to a backhand grip—as soon as you see where the ball is going. 4. The Forehand: Your Primary Groundstroke

Why the Forehand Matters Picture this: You are positioned just behind the baseline. Your opponent hits a deep shot that lands near the center mark. The ball bounces high, and you step forward, pivot your hips, and swing. The ball explodes off your strings, crossing high over the net and landing deep in the corner of the service box. You just hit a forehand. The forehand is the heartbeat of your tennis game. For most players, it is the most natural stroke because you swing with your dominant arm facing the ball. It is also your primary groundstroke—a shot hit after the ball has bounced once, usually from the baseline. Because it generates the most power and is the easiest to control, the forehand is the stroke you will use to dictate points and keep your opponent on the defensive. The Building Blocks: Preparation A successful forehand begins long before the racquet swings forward. The most common mistake beginners make is rushing the swing. To hit the ball cleanly, your body must be prepared before the ball even bounces on your side of the court. The Unit Turn When you see the ball coming toward your forehand side, your first move should not be a frantic run to the ball, nor should it be swinging your arm back. Your first move is the unit turn. Instead of taking the racquet back with just your arm, you turn your entire upper body—shoulders, hips, and arms—as a single, connected unit. As your shoulders rotate to the side, your non-dominant hand (your left hand if you are right-handed) comes up to lightly rest on the throat of the racquet. This does two things: 1. It coils your upper body, storing energy like a spring. 2. It ensures your racquet stays in front of your body, preventing you from taking a long, looping backswing that causes you to be late to the ball. Racquet Preparation As the unit turn completes, your arms naturally separate. Your non-dominant hand extends out in front of your body to help track the incoming ball, while your dominant hand drops the racquet head slightly below the level of the incoming ball. At this stage, your body is sideways to the net. If you are right-handed, your right shoulder is pointing toward the target. Your knees are slightly bent, and your weight is loaded onto your back foot (the right foot). You are now in the "ready position" for the strike. 5. The Backhand: One-Handed and Two-Handed Techniques

Why the Backhand Matters Imagine this scenario: you are rallying from the baseline, and your opponent deliberately hits the ball deep to your left side (or right side, if you are left-handed). They are attacking your backhand. If you lack a reliable shot on that side of your body, you will be forced to run around the ball to hit a forehand—a tactic that quickly exhausts you and leaves the court wide open. The backhand is your defensive anchor and your counter-punching weapon. While the forehand (covered in Module 4) is naturally stronger for most players, a solid backhand prevents your opponents from exploiting your weak side. Unlike the forehand, which has a fairly universally accepted technique, the backhand comes in two distinct flavors: one-handed and two-handed. Choosing between them is one of the most significant stylistic decisions you will make as a beginner. One-Handed vs. Two-Handed: The Great Debate As you watch professional tennis, you will see players using both styles effectively. Roger Federer, Stan Wawrinka, and Carlos Alcaraz wield elegant one-handed backhands, while Novak Djokovic, Rafael Nadal, and Serena Williams dominate with two-handed backhands. Neither style is objectively "better," but they each offer distinct advantages and disadvantages for beginners. The Two-Handed Backhand The two-handed backhand is by far the most common stroke among modern beginners and professional players alike. As the name suggests, you hold the racquet with both hands. Advantages: Extra stability: The second hand acts as a brace, making it much easier to control the racquet face when meeting a heavy, fast-moving ball. Easier for beginners: Because you have two hands on the grip, the swing path is more compact and requires less raw wrist strength to control. Better disguise: Because the preparation is compact, it is harder for your opponent to read where you are aiming the ball until the last second. Disadvantages: Shorter reach: Two hands on the racquet naturally shorten your wingspan, meaning you have to move your feet closer to the ball to make clean contact. Harder to hit low balls: Getting down low for a slice or a short ball is more physically demanding when both hands are on the grip. The One-Handed Backhand The one-handed backhand is a fluid, classic stroke that requires excellent timing and racquet control. Advantages: Extended reach: With one hand on the racquet, you can stretch out much further to get to wide balls. Easier transition to the net: The one-handed backhand naturally sets up the racquet face for volleys (which we will cover in Module 7), making the transition to net play smoother. 6. The Serve: Starting Every Point

The Only Stroke That Starts in Your Hand Imagine stepping up to the baseline with a bucket of balls. You toss one into the air, swing with all your might, and watch the ball sail directly into the net. You try again, adjusting your aim, only to hit the ball six feet past the service box. Frustrating, right? Unlike your forehand and backhand, which are reactions to an incoming ball, the serve is the only stroke in tennis where you completely control the variables. You dictate exactly when to start, where to toss the ball, and how fast to swing. This total control is a double-edged sword: it means you can hit a perfect serve every time, but it also means every serving error is entirely on you. The serve is the catalyst for every point. A strong, reliable serve gets you out of trouble, sets up your offensive shots, and prevents you from handing free points to your opponent. By breaking the serve down into mechanical, repeatable steps, you can transform this notoriously frustrating stroke into your greatest weapon on the court. The Foundation: Stance and Grip Before you ever throw a ball into the sky, your body needs a stable, balanced foundation. How you stand and how you hold the racquet will dictate the path of your swing. The Continental Grip If you read the earlier chapter on grips, you know that different strokes require different hand positions. For the serve, you must use the Continental grip. If you try to serve with the forehand grip (the Eastern Forehand), your wrist will be locked in a position that forces the racquet face open toward the sky. This causes the ball to fly out of bounds. The Continental grip keeps the racquet face neutral and allows your wrist to act like a hinge, snapping through the ball to generate speed and spin. To find the Continental grip, hold the racquet out in front of you with the edge pointing toward the sky (like you are holding a hammer). Place the heel of your hand and the base knuckle of your index finger on the top-right bevel of the racquet handle (if you are right-handed). It will feel slightly awkward at first, but it is non-negotiable for a functional serve. Foot Placement Behind the Baseline To serve, you must stand completely behind the baseline. Your feet cannot touch or cross the baseline until after you have made contact with the ball; doing so is called a foot fault. There are two primary stances for serving: the Platform stance and the Pin-Point stance. 7. Net Play: Volleys & Overheads

Why Play the Net? Imagine this: You are standing three feet from the net. Your opponent is stranded at the baseline, frantically scrambling to track down a short ball. They manage to lob it high into the air, but it’s floating. You step forward, raise your racquet, and smash the ball straight down into the court. The ball bounces over the back fence before your opponent can even take a step. Point over. Playing at the net is the most aggressive, decisive position in tennis. When you are at the baseline (the far end of the court), you are trying to out-rally your opponent. When you are at the net, you are trying to end the point. Volleys and overheads are the weapons that allow you to finish a point quickly, putting immense pressure on your opponent to hit perfect, passing shots to get the ball past you. The Continental Grip: The Net Player’s Best Friend Before we move to the technique of the volley, we must address how to hold the racquet. In the earlier chapters on the forehand and backhand, you likely used specific grips optimized for groundstrokes. At the net, however, you do not have time to switch your grip between a forehand and a backhand. The ball comes at you too fast. To solve this, you will use the Continental grip for all net play. Sometimes called the "hammer grip" or "chopper grip" (because it mimics how you hold a hammer or a hatchet), the Continental grip allows you to hit both forehand and backhand volleys without changing your hand position. How to find the Continental grip: 1. Hold the racquet out in front of you, edge-on to the ground, as if you were holding a hammer to drive a nail into a wall. 2. The V-shape formed by your thumb and index finger should rest slightly to the side of the top bevel (the flat edge on top) of the racquet handle, biased toward your non-dominant side. 3. Your knuckle on the index finger should align with the top-right bevel (for a right-handed player). If you are used to your forehand grip, the Continental grip will initially feel awkward and slightly unstable. However, it is the absolute foundation of net play. Committing to this grip now will save you countless missed volleys in the future. 8. Scoring, Rules & Etiquette

The Tennis Scoring System: Points, Games, and Sets Imagine you are watching a tennis match. The players have been rallying for twenty minutes, hitting forehands and backhands with the techniques you’ve been practicing. Suddenly, the umpire speaks into the microphone: "Fifteen-thirty." To a complete beginner, this sounds like a bizarre math equation. Why fifteen? Where did thirty come from? Tennis scoring is undeniably quirky. Unlike sports that count by simple one-point increments (1, 2, 3, 4), tennis uses a historical system that can feel like a secret code at first. However, once you break it down into its three nesting layers—points, games, and sets—it becomes entirely logical. Scoring a Single Game Every game in tennis starts at zero. Instead of saying "zero," tennis uses the term love. (The exact origin of this is debated, but it is widely believed to come from the French word l'oeuf, meaning "egg," which represents a zero's shape). When a player wins their first point, the score moves to 15. The second point brings the score to 30. The third point brings the score to 40. The fourth point wins the game. Here is how the server’s score is called out as they win points: 1. Love (0 points) 2. 15 (1 point) 3. 30 (2 points) 4. 40 (3 points) 5. Game (4 points) When announcing the score, the server's score is always said first. If you are serving and win the first point, the score is "15-love." If you lose the first point, the score is "love-15." Deuce and Advantage If tennis stopped at four points, the game would be simple. But what happens if both players win three points? The score would be 40-40. In tennis, a 40-40 tie is called deuce. Because a player must win a game by a margin of two points, deuce means the game isn't over yet. From deuce, the scoring system shifts: If the server wins the next point, the score becomes Advantage-In (or "Ad-In" for short). The server is now one point away from winning the game. If the returner wins the next point from deuce, the score becomes Advantage-Out (or "Ad-Out"). The returner is now one point away from winning the game. If a player has the Advantage and wins the very next point, they win the game. However, if the player with the Advantage loses the next point, the score resets back to deuce. This back-and-forth can theoretically go on forever until one player manages to win two consecutive points from deuce. 9. Basic Strategy & Tactics: Singles & Doubles

Imagine this: You are in the middle of a baseline rally. Your opponent hits a forehand that lands near the service line. Instead of hitting the ball right back to them, you step forward, angle your racquet face slightly, and push the ball sharply out wide toward the singles sideline. Your opponent stumbles to their right, stretches desperately, and manages to hit a weak, floating reply. Because you anticipated this, you are already moving toward the net. You easily hit a winning volley into the wide-open court. You didn’t win that point with a blazing 100-mile-per-hour serve or a physically overpowering forehand. You won it by using your brain. Up until now, you have learned the mechanics of tennis: how to hold the racquet, how to swing, and how to move. Now, it is time to learn how to play. Strategy and tactics are the mental frameworks that allow you to use your physical skills to outmaneuver your opponent. The Geometry of the Court: Your Strategic Canvas Tennis is essentially a game of geometry. The rectangular boundaries of the court dictate the angles available to you and the distances you must cover. Understanding court geometry is the foundation of all tennis strategy. The most important geometric concept in tennis is the cross-court shot. If you stand at the center of the baseline and hit the ball diagonally across the net into the opposite corner of the court, the ball travels over the lowest part of the net (the middle) and lands in the longest possible part of the court. By contrast, hitting down-the-line means hitting the ball straight ahead, parallel to the singles sideline. A down-the-line shot must clear the net at its highest point (the net posts) and travels over a shorter distance, meaning it has less margin for error. High-Percentage vs. Low-Percentage Shots - High-percentage shots are shots with a high probability of landing inside the lines. Cross-court shots are high-percentage because they pass over the lower part of the net and have more court length to work with. - Low-percentage shots are riskier. Down-the-line shots, sharp angles, and trying to hit the lines perfectly are low-percentage. They are often used to attack or finish a point, but relying on them too heavily will lead to unforced errors. As a beginner, your primary strategic goal is to hit high-percentage shots until your opponent gives you a short, weak ball that allows you to hit a low-percentage, aggressive shot for a winner. 10. Practice Drills & Skill Development

The Bridge Between Knowing and Playing Imagine you’ve just finished reading a manual on how to ride a bicycle. You understand the mechanics perfectly: you know you must push the pedals, grip the handlebars, and balance your center of gravity. But if I handed you a bicycle right now and asked you to ride it down a hill, you would likely wobble and crash. Why? Because intellectual understanding and physical mastery are two entirely different things. Tennis is no different. By reaching Module 10, you understand the grips, the footwork, and the mechanics of the forehand, backhand, serve, and volley. You know how to play. But to actually play well, you must transform that intellectual knowledge into muscle memory—the ability to execute a complex physical movement automatically, without conscious thought. Muscle memory is built through high-quality repetition. This module provides the structured drills and practice routines you need to bridge the gap between knowing how to hit a tennis ball and actually playing the game. Whether you have an hour with a partner or thirty minutes alone, you will learn how to design your practice, track your progress, and simulate match conditions. Solo Practice: Wall Drills and Shadow Swings You don’t always need a partner or a court fee to improve your tennis game. Some of the best practice you can do as a beginner happens entirely on your own, using nothing but a flat wall and your own body weight. Shadow Swings: Grooving the Stroke A shadow swing is the act of swinging your racquet without a ball. It sounds tedious, but it is the fastest way to isolate and fix mechanical flaws. When you hit a ball, your brain is distracted by the goal of making contact. When you shadow swing, your brain can focus 100% on your body’s movement. To perform an effective shadow swing: 1. Find your space: Stand in front of a mirror or a shadow-casting window if possible, so you can check your form. 2. Execute the full motion: Go through your unit turn, racquet drop, contact point, and follow-through exactly as you learned in the forehand and backhand chapters. 3. Check your finish: Freeze at the end of your follow-through. Is your racquet over your opposite shoulder? Is your weight on your front foot? If not, correct it and repeat. 4. Build reps: Aim for 20 perfect shadow swings for your forehand and 20 for your backhand. 11. Injury Prevention & Physical Conditioning

You’ve just spent the last hour rallying from the baseline, practicing your forehand and backhand, and maybe even testing out your serve. You feel great, but the next morning, you wake up with a sharp pain on the outside of your elbow, and your shoulders feel incredibly stiff. You might be experiencing the early stages of tennis elbow, a condition so common it bears the sport's name. Tennis is a physically demanding sport that requires explosive bursts of speed, rotational power, and repetitive arm movements. As a beginner, your muscles, tendons, and joints are adapting to entirely new stresses. Without proper preparation, the mechanics you learned in earlier chapters—like the kinetic chain of your serve or the lateral movement required for footwork—can quickly lead to strain. This module shifts focus from how to hit the ball to how to prepare your body to play safely, recover effectively, and stay on the court for years to come. The Pre-Match Warm-Up Routine Jumping straight onto the court and immediately hitting serves at full speed is a recipe for injury. A proper warm-up gradually increases your heart rate, raises your core body temperature, and lubricates your joints. For tennis, your warm-up should be divided into three phases: dynamic stretching, agility exercises, and progressive hitting. Dynamic Stretching Dynamic stretching involves active, controlled movements that take your muscles and joints through their full range of motion. This is vastly different from static stretching (holding a stretch for 30 seconds), which is better suited for cooling down after a match. Dynamic stretching "wakes up" your nervous system and prepares your muscles for the rapid contractions required in tennis. Before you touch your racquet, spend 5 to 7 minutes performing these movements: Arm Circles: Stand tall and extend your arms out to your sides. Make small, forward circles, gradually making them larger. After 15 seconds, reverse the direction. This warms up the rotator cuff (the group of muscles and tendons stabilizing your shoulder). Torso Twists: Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart and knees slightly bent. Slowly rotate your upper body to the right, then to the left, letting your arms swing naturally. This prepares your core for the rotational forces of your forehand and backhand. Walking Lunges: Step forward with your right foot and lower your hips until both knees are bent at a 90-degree angle. Push off your right foot to bring your left foot forward into the next lunge. This activates your quads, glutes, and hamstrings. Leg Swings: Stand next to the net posts for balance.
