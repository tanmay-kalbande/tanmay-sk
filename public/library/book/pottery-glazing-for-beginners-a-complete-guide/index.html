1. Introduction to Glazes

The Magic of the Melt Imagine you have just spent hours meticulously throwing a bowl on the wheel. You’ve trimmed the foot, let it dry to leather-hard, and fired it once in the kiln. You now hold a piece of bisqueware—pottery that has been fired once to make it durable but remains porous, feeling like a terracotta pot or a piece of chalk. If you poured water into this bowl, the water would slowly seep into the walls of the clay. If you tried to eat soup from it, the porous surface would trap food particles and bacteria, making it unsanitary. Then, you apply a liquid, milky substance to the surface and put it back into the kiln. After several hours of intense heat, that liquid transforms. It melts, flows, and chemically bonds to the clay, turning into a hard, colorful, glass-like coating. This is the essence of glazing. A glaze is not simply "paint" for pottery; it is a layer of glass that is fused to the ceramic body through heat. It transforms a fragile, porous object into a functional, waterproof, and aesthetically striking piece of art. What is a Glaze? At its simplest level, a glaze is a mixture of minerals that, when heated to a specific temperature, melts into a glass. While we often think of glazes in terms of color—cobalt blue, celadon green, or matte white—the color is actually the least important part of the glaze's chemical structure. The primary purpose of a glaze is to create a seal. The Primary Functions of Glaze Glazing serves three main purposes in ceramics: 1. Functionality (Vitrification): Raw clay, even after its first firing, is porous. A glaze creates a non-porous surface. This process of making a piece "water-tight" is essential for anything intended to hold liquids, such as mugs, vases, or bowls. 2. Hygiene: Because a glazed surface is smooth and glass-like, it does not have the microscopic pores that trap food, oils, or bacteria. This makes the piece "food-safe" and easy to clean. 3. Aesthetics: Glazes allow the potter to introduce color, texture, and light. Whether you want a high-gloss finish that reflects light or a matte finish that feels like stone, the glaze determines the final visual and tactile experience of the piece. 2. Understanding Kiln Temperatures

The Danger of the Wrong Temperature Imagine you’ve spent three weeks perfecting a delicate porcelain vase. You’ve carefully applied a beautiful, glossy blue glaze. You place it in the kiln, set the temperature to what you think is correct, and wait. When you open the kiln, you don’t find a vase; you find a puddle of blue glass fused to the kiln shelf. Your masterpiece has literally melted. Conversely, imagine firing a piece of high-fire stoneware in a low-fire kiln. The piece comes out looking matte and chalky, and when you fill it with water, it slowly leaks through the walls of the pot, even though there are no visible cracks. These aren't accidents caused by "bad luck." They are the result of a mismatch between the clay body and the firing temperature. In pottery, temperature isn't just a number on a dial—it is a chemical catalyst that determines whether your work becomes a functional piece of art or a heap of shards. Heat Work and the Mystery of the "Cone" When beginners enter a pottery studio, they often hear people talking about "Cone 6" or "Cone 04." To the uninitiated, this sounds like a specific temperature (like 2,000°F), but it is actually a measure of heat work. Temperature vs. Heat Work Temperature is a snapshot of how hot the kiln is at a single moment. Heat work, however, is the combination of time and temperature. Think of it like baking a cake. If you put a cake in the oven at 400°F for only two minutes, it won't be cooked, even though it reached the "correct" temperature. To get the cake to bake, you need the heat to act on the batter over a specific period of time. In ceramics, the clay and glaze don't just react to the peak temperature; they react to how long they have been soaking in that heat. A kiln that rises slowly to 2,200°F might actually achieve more "heat work" than a kiln that flashes up to 2,230°F very quickly. What is a Pyrometric Cone? To measure this, potters use pyrometric cones. These are small, triangular pyramids made of specific ceramic materials designed to melt (bend) at a very precise amount of heat work. When the tip of the cone bends to a specific angle, the potter knows the kiln has achieved the required heat work. While most modern kilns use digital controllers (pyrometers), these controllers are actually programmed to mimic the bending of these physical cones. 3. Glaze Preparation and Safety

The Invisible Danger: Why Safety Comes First Imagine walking into a pottery studio and seeing a fine, white dust coating the tabletops and floating in the shafts of sunlight. To the untrained eye, it looks like harmless flour or powdered sugar. In reality, that dust is often a mixture of silica and other metal oxides. When inhaled, these microscopic particles can settle deep in the lungs, causing permanent scarring or chronic respiratory issues over time. Because the most dangerous materials in a glaze lab are often odorless, colorless, and invisible until they are airborne, safety isn't just a set of rules—it is the foundation of your practice. Before you mix your first bucket of glaze, you must transform your workspace from a "dust zone" into a controlled environment. The Golden Rule: Wet Cleaning The most critical concept in glaze safety is the avoidance of airborne dust. You should never use a broom or a handheld duster to clean a glaze area, as this simply launches the particles back into your breathing zone. Always use "wet cleaning" methods: Damp Sponges: Wipe down surfaces with a wet sponge. Mop and Bucket: Clean floors with a wet mop. HEPA Vacuums: If you must vacuum, use a vacuum equipped with a High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filter. Standard shop vacs often blow the finest, most dangerous particles straight through the filter and back into the air. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Even with wet cleaning, raw powders are volatile. You need a barrier between your body and the chemicals. 1. The Respirator: A standard surgical mask is insufficient for raw glaze materials. Use an N95 mask or, ideally, a reusable half-face respirator with P100 particulate filters. Wear this whenever you are weighing dry powders or mixing a new batch. 2. Gloves: While not always necessary for every material, nitrile gloves protect your skin from caustic materials (like certain fluxes) and prevent your skin from absorbing heavy metals. 3. Clothing: Wear an apron and closed-toe shoes. Glazes are messy, and raw materials can be irritating to the skin. Ventilation and Workspace Setup Your mixing area should be physically separated from your "clean" areas (like where you eat or store finished work). If possible, mix glazes outdoors or under a professional ventilation hood. If you are working in a home studio, place a fan in a window blowing outward to pull dust away from your face. --- From Powder to Liquid: Mixing Your First Glaze Most glazes are sold or formulated as a "recipe"—a list of dry ingredients by weight. 4. Surface Preparation for Glazing

The Hidden Bridge: Why Surface Prep Matters Imagine spending hours throwing a perfect vase, carefully selecting a stunning cobalt blue glaze, and meticulously applying it. You fire the kiln, open the door with anticipation, and find that the glaze has "crawled"—meaning it has pulled away from the clay in random spots, leaving ugly, bare patches of bisque. Or worse, you find your piece has permanently fused to the kiln shelf, requiring a hammer and chisel to remove it, leaving a jagged scar on the bottom of your work. In both scenarios, the failure didn't happen during the firing or because of the glaze chemistry. It happened before the glaze ever touched the pot. Glaze is essentially a layer of glass. For glass to bond permanently to a ceramic surface, the surface must be chemically "hungry" and physically clean. Any barrier—be it a fingerprint, a layer of dust, or a stray piece of packing foam—acts as a contaminant that prevents the glaze from adhering. Surface preparation is the process of ensuring that the bond between the clay body and the glaze is unbreakable. The Role of the Bisque Fire Before we can prepare the surface, we must ensure the piece has undergone its first transformation: the bisque firing. As discussed in previous sections, bisqueware is clay that has been fired once to a relatively low temperature. While the piece looks finished and feels hard, it is actually in a state of "controlled porosity." This means the clay is hard enough to handle without breaking, but it still contains millions of microscopic holes (pores) that act like a sponge. Why we glaze bisque instead of raw clay While "single-firing" (glazing raw clay) is possible, beginners should almost always use the bisque-fired method for two critical reasons: 1. Absorption: Because bisqueware is porous, it sucks the water out of the liquid glaze quickly. This "pull" is what anchors the glaze to the surface, allowing a thick, even layer to build up without sliding off the pot. 2. Structural Integrity: Raw clay is fragile and collapses easily. Bisqueware is sturdy, allowing you to dip, brush, or pour glaze without risking the piece warping or cracking under the weight of the liquid. If you attempt to glaze a piece that hasn't been bisque fired, the glaze will simply sit on the surface like paint on a plastic cup; it will not bond, and it will likely peel or flake off during the second firing. 5. Basic Application Techniques

The Moment of Truth: From Chalky to Glassy Imagine you have spent hours throwing a bowl on the wheel and another few hours trimming it to a perfect profile. You have fired it to bisqueware, and you have carefully cleaned the surface. Now, you hold a bucket of liquid glaze. In a matter of seconds—whether by a quick dip or a careful brushstroke—you will determine whether that piece looks like a professional gallery work or a streaky, uneven experiment. Applying glaze is where chemistry meets artistry. While the ingredients (the silica, flux, and alumina) do the work inside the kiln, your application technique determines how those ingredients sit on the surface. A glaze that is too thin may leave the clay porous and unhygienic; a glaze that is too thick may run off the pot and fuse it to the kiln shelf. Dipping: The Gold Standard for Uniformity Dipping is the most common method for achieving a consistent, professional finish. It involves submerging the entire piece (or a significant portion of it) into a container of glaze. The Mechanics of the Dip When you dip bisqueware, the porous clay acts like a sponge, instantly sucking the water out of the glaze suspension. This leaves a thin, uniform layer of glaze particles clinging to the surface. This process happens incredibly fast—often in less than three seconds. The Step-by-Step Process: 1. Hold and Steady: Grip your piece firmly using glaze tongs (metal pincers designed to hold pottery without leaving fingerprints). 2. The Submersion: Lower the piece into the glaze bucket in one smooth, confident motion. 3. The Count: Hold the piece in the glaze for 3 to 5 seconds. Too short: The coating will be too thin, leading to "dry" spots. Too long: The coating will be too thick, which may cause the glaze to crack or run during firing. 4. The Exit: Lift the piece straight up and give it a gentle shake to remove excess drips. Managing the Glaze Bath Because dipping involves a large volume of liquid, you must manage the viscosity (the thickness or "flowiness") of the glaze to ensure consistent results. Stirring: Glaze particles are heavy and settle at the bottom of the bucket quickly. Always stir your glaze thoroughly with a whisk or paddle before every single dip. Specific Gravity: This is a measure of the density of your glaze. If the glaze is too watery, it won't cling; if it is too thick, it will apply too heavily. Most potters use a hydrometer to check this, but for beginners, the "finger test" works: if the glaze feels like heavy cream, it is usually in the right range for dipping. 6. Layering and Combining Glazes

The Magic of the Overlap Imagine you have two jars of glaze: one a deep, stable cobalt blue and the other a creamy, opaque white. If you dip a pot in the blue and leave it, you get a blue pot. If you dip it in the white, you get a white pot. But what happens if you dip the top third of the pot in white and the bottom two-thirds in blue? Where those two colors meet, a third, entirely new color often emerges. You might see a streak of turquoise, a frothy sea-foam green, or even a metallic edge. This is not a mistake; it is the result of glaze interaction. Until now, you have likely viewed glaze as a "paint" that stays where you put it. In reality, glaze is a chemical cocktail that melts into a liquid state inside the kiln. When two different chemical cocktails meet and melt together, they create a new chemical reaction, leading to colors and textures that are impossible to achieve with a single glaze. Understanding Glaze Interaction To understand why glazes interact, we have to look back at the three main components we discussed in the Introduction to Glazes: silica, flux, and alumina. Every glaze has a different ratio of these components. When you layer Glaze A over Glaze B, you are essentially changing the chemistry of the surface. You are adding more flux to one area or increasing the alumina in another. Fluidity and Movement The most visible part of glaze interaction is movement. Because glazes have different levels of viscosity (their resistance to flowing), they don't always stay put. Fluid Glazes: Some glazes have a high concentration of flux, making them "runny" when melted. If you layer a fluid glaze over a stable one, the top layer will slide down the pot, creating "drips" or "runs." Stable Glazes: Other glazes have more alumina, which acts as a stabilizer. These stay exactly where they are applied. When a fluid glaze runs over a stable one, it creates a gradient—a smooth transition from one color to another. If both glazes are fluid, they may blend completely, creating a marbled or variegated effect. Chemical Color Shifts Color in pottery comes from metal oxides. When two glazes overlap, the oxides from the top layer mix with the oxides of the bottom layer. For example, a glaze containing copper (which often looks green) layered over a glaze containing iron (which often looks brown) might produce a deep, iridescent purple or a dark forest green at the intersection. 7. Troubleshooting Common Glaze Defects

The "Perfect" Piece That Isn't Imagine this: You’ve spent hours throwing a bowl, carefully trimmed the foot, and applied a beautiful blue glaze using the techniques from Basic Application Techniques. You wait with anticipation as the kiln cools, open the door, and find your bowl is a stunning shade of cobalt. But as you pick it up, you hear a faint tink-tink-tink sound, and upon closer inspection, you see a network of tiny, spider-web cracks covering the entire surface. Or perhaps you find a small puddle of glass fused to your kiln shelf, threatening to ruin the furniture. For a beginner, these moments can feel like failure. In reality, these are "glaze defects," and they are the most valuable teaching tools you have. Every crack, bubble, or run is a piece of data telling you exactly what happened inside the kiln. Troubleshooting is simply the process of decoding that data to improve your next piece. Crazing: The Spider-Web Effect Crazing is the most common glaze defect. It appears as a network of fine cracks in the glaze layer. These cracks occur because the glaze has shrunk more than the clay body during the cooling process, causing the glaze to pull itself apart to fit the pot. The Science of the Fit To understand crazing, we have to look at the relationship between the clay and the glaze. Think of the glaze as a tight-fitting sweater. If the sweater is too small for the person wearing it, the fabric will stretch and eventually rip. In pottery, this is called "coefficient of thermal expansion." Every clay body and every glaze expands when heated and contracts when cooled. For a successful fit, the glaze should contract slightly less than the clay. This puts the glaze under slight compression, which actually makes the finished piece stronger. When the glaze contracts more than the clay, it enters tension and snaps, resulting in crazing. Is Crazing a Problem? Whether crazing is a "defect" or a "feature" depends on your goal: Aesthetics: In some traditions, like Japanese Raku or certain decorative ceramics, crazing is intentional and prized for its visual texture. Functionality: If you are making a piece for food or drink, crazing is a serious issue. Bacteria can lodge in the cracks, making the piece unhygienic. Furthermore, because the glaze is under tension, the piece is structurally weaker and more prone to breaking. 8. Developing a Glaze Log

Why Your Next Glaze Should Begin with a Note Picture this: You’ve just finished glazing a set of mugs—carefully brushing on three layers of a deep cobalt blue over a white slip, following a recipe you found online. The kiln fires to cone 6 overnight, and when you unload it the next morning, the results are… underwhelming. The color is murky. The surface is dull. One mug even developed tiny bubbles that pop when touched. Frustrated, you try the recipe again, tweaking the water content and brushing speed. Same result. Now you’re left guessing what went wrong and how to fix it. This kind of experience is common for beginners—and even experienced potters—because glazing is part science, part alchemy, and part trial and error. The missing piece? A glaze log. A glaze log isn’t just a notebook. It’s your personal guide to turning random experiments into repeatable results. It turns guesswork into strategy. Without one, every glaze application is a leap of faith. With one, every firing becomes a lesson. By the end of this chapter, you’ll know how to build a system that records not just what you did, but why you did it—and how it turned out. That way, when you fire your next batch, you won’t be starting from scratch. You’ll be building on knowledge. --- The Core Purpose of a Glaze Log A glaze log serves three essential functions: 1. Documentation – Record the exact ingredients, proportions, and processes used in each glaze. 2. Analysis – Compare outcomes across multiple firings to spot patterns. 3. Replication – Recreate successful glazes with confidence, and avoid repeating mistakes. Without a log, you’re relying on memory and intuition—both unreliable when dealing with variables like kiln temperature fluctuations, glaze viscosity, and clay body differences. 📝 Think of your glaze log as a recipe book for your kiln. Just as a chef wouldn’t bake a cake without noting the oven temperature and baking time, a potter shouldn’t fire a glaze without recording how it was applied and what it looked like afterward. --- What to Record: The Essential Fields Your glaze log should be simple but thorough. It doesn’t need to be fancy—spreadsheets, notebooks, or digital apps all work. What matters is consistency.
