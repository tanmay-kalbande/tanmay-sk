1. Introduction to Origami Materials

Why Your Paper Choice Matters Imagine spending an hour meticulously folding a complex crane, only to have the paper tear at the final crucial step, or worse, find that the paper is so thick the model refuses to stay closed. You might assume the fault lies in your folding technique, but more often than not, the culprit is the material. In origami, the paper is not just a surface; it is your primary engineering material. Different papers possess different "memory" (the ability to hold a crease), different tensile strengths (resistance to tearing), and different textures. Choosing the wrong paper for a specific project is like trying to paint a watercolor masterpiece on a piece of cardboard—it is technically possible, but you are fighting the medium rather than working with it. The Origami Paper Palette While you can fold almost any piece of paper, origami practitioners generally categorize papers based on their properties and origins. For a beginner, understanding the distinction between Kami, Tant, and Washi is the first step toward consistent results. Kami: The Standard Beginner’s Choice Kami is the generic term for the standard, affordable origami paper most people encounter in starter kits. It is typically thin, lightweight, and often colored on one side and white on the other. Characteristics: It is easy to fold and holds a sharp crease well. Because it is thin, it doesn't add bulk to the model, which is essential when you have to fold the same area of paper multiple times. Best For: Learning basic folds, practicing simple models, and projects where you want a clear distinction between the "front" and "back" of the paper. Downside: It is prone to tearing if you unfold and refold the same crease too many times. Tant: The Professional’s All-Rounder Tant is a dyed-through paper, meaning it is the same color on both sides. It is slightly thicker and has a distinct, slightly textured feel compared to Kami. Characteristics: Tant is renowned for its "memory." Once you make a fold in Tant, the paper wants to stay in that position. It is also much more durable and resistant to tearing. Best For: Models that need to stand upright or maintain a specific 3D shape without sagging. It is excellent for beginners who find themselves accidentally ripping thinner papers. Downside: Because it is thicker, it can become "bulky" in complex models where many layers of paper overlap. Washi: The Artistic Tradition Washi is traditional Japanese paper handmade from long fibers (such as the gampi tree or mulberry). Unlike mass-produced papers, Washi is prized for its strength and beauty. Characteristics: Washi is incredibly strong for its weight because the fibers are interwoven. 2. Understanding Origami Symbols and Diagrams

The Silent Language of the Fold Imagine you have just acquired a beautiful sheet of Washi and a detailed instruction manual for a traditional crane. You open the book, expecting a step-by-step written guide, but instead, you find a series of drawings. There are dashed lines, dotted lines, strange arrows that loop back on themselves, and small symbols that look like a secret code. To the untrained eye, an origami diagram looks like a geometry textbook. However, once you understand the "visual shorthand" used by folders worldwide, these diagrams become a universal language. Whether the instructions were written in Japan, France, or the United States, the symbols remain the same. Learning to read these symbols is the bridge between simply folding paper and being able to create any model in existence. The Foundation: Valley and Mountain Folds Every complex origami model is composed of a series of creases. In the previous chapter, we defined the crease as the permanent line left in the paper. In the world of diagrams, not all creases are created equal. There are two primary directions a fold can go: toward you or away from you. The Valley Fold The Valley Fold is the most common movement in origami. It occurs when you fold the paper toward yourself, creating a "valley" or a V-shape in the paper. The Symbol: In a diagram, a valley fold is represented by a dashed line (a series of short segments: - - - -). The Action: You are pushing the paper inward. If you were to look at the fold from the side, it would look like a dip. The Mountain Fold The Mountain Fold is the exact opposite of a valley fold. Instead of folding the paper toward you, you fold it away from you (or flip the paper over and perform a valley fold). This creates a peak, similar to a mountain ridge. The Symbol: A mountain fold is represented by a dash-dot-dash line (a long segment followed by a small dot: — . — . —). The Action: You are pushing the paper outward. The ridge of the fold points up toward the ceiling. Pro Tip: If you find a mountain fold difficult to execute, try flipping your paper over, performing a valley fold, and then flipping it back. The result is identical. Directional Arrows and Rotation Lines tell you where to fold, but arrows tell you how to move the paper. Because diagrams are two-dimensional representations of a three-dimensional object, arrows provide the necessary depth and direction. 3. The Fundamental Base Folds

The Blueprint of a Model Imagine you are building a house. You wouldn't start by trying to install the windows or painting the walls; you would first pour a concrete slab or frame the foundation. If the foundation is crooked, the roof will eventually sag, no matter how skilled the carpenter is. Origami works exactly the same way. Almost every traditional model—from a simple crane to a complex dragon—starts with a Base. In origami, a Base is a specific, pre-folded configuration of paper that serves as the structural starting point for a model. Instead of inventing a new way to fold every time you start a project, you use these universal foundations. Once you have a base, you have effectively "mapped out" where the limbs, head, or petals of your model will emerge. If you can master the three bases covered here, you will be able to recognize the starting point of hundreds of different diagrams. The Preliminary Base The Preliminary Base (sometimes called the Square Base in older texts, though we will distinguish them here) is the most common starting point for animals and insects. It transforms your large square of Kami into a smaller, four-layered square. How to Fold the Preliminary Base 1. The X-Crease: Start with your square paper. Perform a Diagonal Fold from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner. Unfold. Repeat from the top-right to the bottom-left. Unfold. You should now have two intersecting dashed lines forming an "X". 2. The Plus-Crease: Flip the paper over. Fold the paper in half horizontally (edge to edge). Unfold. Fold it in half vertically. Unfold. 3. The Collapse: This is where the magic happens. Bring the center point of the paper up. As you do this, the side edges will naturally want to fold inward along the creases you just made. 4. Flatten: Press the paper flat. You should now have a small square that is exactly one-quarter the size of your original sheet, consisting of four layers of paper. When to Use It The Preliminary Base is the "general purpose" foundation. Because it creates four distinct flaps (one at the top and one at each side), it is the primary choice for: Birds: The flaps become the wings and the head/tail. Insects: The flaps become the legs and antennae. Simple Animals: Like the traditional dog or cat face. The Waterbomb Base While the Preliminary Base creates a square, the Waterbomb Base creates a triangle. It is the foundation for models that require a central "point" or a wide, stable bottom. 4. Basic Folding Techniques

The Anatomy of a Fold Imagine you are staring at a complex diagram of a crane or a lotus. You recognize the symbols for the base folds, but as you move into the body of the model, the instructions start asking you to "squash," "petal," or "crimp." Suddenly, the paper feels like it's resisting you. You might find yourself forcing a fold, only to have the paper tear or the proportions shift, leading to the "Creep Effect" we discussed in the first chapter. The difference between a model that looks "homemade" and one that looks professional isn't the quality of the Kami you use; it is the precision of these specific manipulation movements. While base folds provide the skeleton of a model, folding techniques are the muscles and joints. They allow you to change the volume of the paper, create three-dimensional depth, and transition from a flat sheet into a living shape. Master the Directionals: Valley and Mountain Folds Every single movement in origami is a variation of two opposing directions. If you master the physical execution of these two, every other technique in this chapter becomes significantly easier. The Valley Fold The Valley Fold is the most intuitive movement in origami. As you recall from the symbols chapter, this fold creates a "valley" or a trough in the paper. How to execute for precision: 1. Fold the paper toward you, bringing the edge to meet the target line. 2. Use your fingertips to align the corners perfectly. 3. Slide your finger—or a bone folder for a sharper edge—firmly across the crease. The goal is a crisp, clean line. A "soft" valley fold often leads to instability in the model, causing it to spring open unexpectedly. The Mountain Fold The Mountain Fold is the exact inverse of the valley fold. Instead of a trough, you are creating a peak. While the result is simply a fold facing the opposite direction, the physical execution requires a different mindset to avoid distorting the paper. Two ways to achieve a Mountain Fold: The Flip Method: Flip your paper over, perform a standard valley fold, and flip it back. This is the safest method for beginners to ensure symmetry. The Direct Method: Fold the paper away from you. Use your thumb to push the crease downward while your index finger holds the back of the paper flat against the table. Pro Tip: When a diagram calls for a mountain fold, the resulting crease should look like a ridge. If you see a "dip" instead of a "peak," you have accidentally created a valley fold. 5. Intermediate Folding Maneuvers

From Flat Shapes to 3D Forms Have you ever wondered how a flat, square piece of Kami transforms into a bird with a pointed beak or a fox with a slender snout? Up until now, your folds have mostly been about dividing the paper into halves, quarters, or triangles. You have mastered the Valley Fold and the Initial Fold, but these techniques primarily change the size of the paper. To create detail—like the narrow neck of a swan or the pointed ears of a cat—you need to change the direction of the paper's flow. This is where intermediate maneuvers come in. Instead of just folding a flap over, you will now learn to tuck, invert, and pivot the paper. These maneuvers allow you to narrow a wide section of paper into a precise point without adding extra cuts or glue. The Magic of Reverse Folds A reverse fold is essentially a way to change the direction of a point. Imagine a piece of paper pointing straight up; a reverse fold allows you to "snap" that point downward or sideways while keeping the paper's structure intact. There are two primary types of reverse folds: the Inside-Reverse Fold and the Outside-Reverse Fold. The difference lies in where the "extra" paper ends up. The Inside-Reverse Fold An Inside-Reverse Fold occurs when you push a corner or a point into the body of the model. This creates a clean, seamless look because the fold is hidden inside the paper. When to use it: This is the gold standard for creating heads, tails, or legs where you want a sharp angle but a smooth exterior. How to execute it: 1. Pre-crease: Using the techniques from Basic Folding Techniques, create a Valley Fold where you want the angle to change. Unfold it. 2. Open the Model: Gently open the sides of your model (the "pocket") to create a small gap. 3. The Push: Push the point inward along the pre-creased line. 4. Flatten: Press the sides of the model back together to lock the point in its new, inverted position. Pro Tip: If you feel the paper resisting or bunching up, stop. Check your pre-crease. An inside-reverse fold is only as clean as the crease that guides it. The Outside-Reverse Fold An Outside-Reverse Fold is the mirror image of the inside version. Instead of pushing the point into the model, you fold the point over the outside of the paper. 6. Traditional Simple Models

From Flat Sheets to Familiar Forms Imagine standing in a room with a single sheet of Kami. To an observer, it is just a square of colored paper. But to an origami practitioner, that square is a dormant sculpture. The magic of origami isn't found in the paper itself, but in the transition—the moment a series of geometric creases transforms a two-dimensional plane into a three-dimensional object that the rest of the world recognizes as a bird, a boat, or a hat. Up until now, you have been learning the "alphabet" of origami: the Fundamental Base Folds and the Basic Folding Techniques. You have practiced making precise creases and managing the "Creep" Effect. Now, it is time to write your first sentences. By combining these isolated movements, you will create traditional models that have been folded for generations. The Art of Precision and Alignment Before diving into the models, we must address the difference between a "rough fold" and a "finished model." In previous chapters, you practiced the Initial Fold to understand the mechanics. In this chapter, the goal shifts toward Alignment. Alignment is the practice of ensuring that two edges or two corners meet exactly, with no overlap or gap. In simple models, a misalignment of just one millimeter at the start can compound, leading to a model that is lopsided or refuses to close. Pro-Tips for High-Precision Models: The "Ghost" Fold: Before pressing down a crease, lightly pinch the paper to align the corners. Once you are certain they match perfectly, slide your finger (or a bone folder) across the fold to lock it in. Check Your Symmetry: Periodically hold your model up to the light. If one side looks bulkier than the other, you likely have a misalignment in your base folds. The Centerline Anchor: Many traditional models rely on a center crease. Always refer back to this line to ensure your folds are mirrored accurately on both sides. The Classic Paper Airplane (The Dart) The paper airplane is often the first experience many people have with paper folding. While it doesn't always start with a perfect square, it utilizes the core principles of symmetry and aerodynamics. 7. Modular Origami Basics

The Magic of Multiplication Imagine you want to create a complex, geometric sculpture—something with twenty faces or a perfectly symmetrical star. If you tried to fold this from a single sheet of kami, you would quickly run into a physical limit: the paper would become too thick to fold, or you would run out of surface area to create the necessary detail. This is where Modular Origami changes the game. Instead of one large sheet, you fold several smaller, identical pieces of paper. These individual components are called modules. Once you have a collection of these modules, you fit them together like LEGO bricks or a 3D puzzle. The result is a structure that is often stronger, larger, and more intricate than anything possible with a single sheet. The most surprising part? In true modular origami, no glue, tape, or fasteners are used. The paper holds itself together through friction and geometry. Understanding the Module A module is a single unit of a larger construction. In modular origami, the goal is usually to create multiple copies of the exact same module. Because you are repeating the same set of folds several times, you develop a "muscle memory" that makes the process faster and more accurate as you go. The strength of a modular build relies on two primary components: Tabs and Pockets. The Tab: A flap of paper (usually a pointed or rectangular edge) that acts as the "connector." The Pocket: A fold or slit created within the module that receives the tab. When a tab is slid firmly into a pocket, the tension of the paper creates a mechanical lock. If your folds are crisp—utilizing a bone folder as discussed in Introduction to Origami Materials—the friction between the two surfaces will keep the model from falling apart. The Sonobe Unit: The Universal Building Block The Sonobe unit is perhaps the most famous module in the world. It is the "gold standard" for beginners because it is simple to fold but can be used to create everything from a basic cube to complex polyhedra. To begin, you will need six square sheets of paper. While kami is ideal for the crisp creases required here, Tant is an excellent choice if you want a more durable, colorful finished product. 8. Wet Folding and Shaping

Beyond the Sharp Edge Imagine you have just finished a model of a bird. You followed every diagram perfectly, using the Fundamental Base Folds and Basic Folding Techniques you've mastered. But as you look at it, you notice a problem: the bird looks like it was made of cardboard. Its chest is a flat plane, its beak is a rigid spike, and its wings are stiff triangles. In nature, almost nothing is a perfect 90-degree angle. Up until now, origami has been about the "crease"—the act of creating a sharp, permanent line in the paper. Wet folding changes the goal. Instead of treating paper like a series of hinges, wet folding treats paper like a sculpting medium, similar to clay. By strategically dampening the fibers, you can move away from geometric shapes and toward organic, lifelike forms. The Science of Dampened Paper To understand why wet folding works, we have to look at what paper actually is. Paper is a web of cellulose fibers pressed together. When the paper is dry, these fibers are locked in place by hydrogen bonds. When you make a Crease, you are essentially breaking some of those bonds to force the paper into a new position. When you introduce water, you are temporarily "unlocking" those fibers. The water molecules slide between the cellulose chains, acting as a lubricant. This allows the paper to stretch, bend, and curve without cracking or resisting. Once the paper dries, those hydrogen bonds reform in their new, curved positions. This creates a "set" that is much stronger than a standard dry fold. While a dry fold can be easily unfolded (the Unfold), a properly dried wet-folded curve is semi-permanent; it resists springing back to its original flat state. The Danger of Over-Saturation There is a critical difference between dampening and soaking. Dampening: The paper feels cool and flexible, but does not leak water. The fibers are loose enough to move, but the structural integrity remains. Soaking: The paper becomes "mushy." The fibers lose their grip on one another entirely. If you over-saturate your paper, it may tear under the slightest pressure or warp uncontrollably as it dries. Choosing the Right Paper Not all papers react to water the same way. If you attempt wet folding with standard Kami, you will likely encounter a disaster. Because Kami is thin and often treated with sizing (a starch-like coating), it tends to warp, wrinkle, or tear the moment it gets wet. The Ideal Candidates For wet folding, you need paper with high "internal sizing" and a thicker weight. This allows the paper to hold a curve without collapsing.
