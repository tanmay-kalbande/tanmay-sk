1. Pronunciation and the Spanish Alphabet

The Foundation of Spoken Spanish Picture yourself stepping off a bus in a small town in Mexico. You approach a fruit vendor and ask the price of an apple. You say the word perfectly—grammatically correct, vocabulary spot on—but the vendor looks at you with a confused expression. You try again, perhaps a little louder. Still nothing. Finally, you point, the vendor smiles, and says the price out loud. You realize instantly what went wrong: the word you practiced in your head sounded completely different coming out of your mouth. In English, we are used to a chaotic relationship between spelling and sound. Consider the letters "ough" in the words though, through, rough, and cough. Each combination is pronounced entirely differently. English spelling is a historical patchwork, meaning you often have to memorize how a word sounds independently of how it is written. Spanish is fundamentally different. It is a phonetic language, meaning words are pronounced exactly as they are written. Once you learn the rules of the Spanish alphabet and its sounds, you can look at almost any word in a Spanish dictionary and pronounce it flawlessly on your first try, even if you have no idea what it means. This predictability is one of the greatest advantages for beginners. To build this skill, we need to start with the smallest building blocks of the language: vowels. The Five Spanish Vowels In English, vowels are slippery. The letter "a" sounds different in cat, cake, car, and call. In Spanish, a vowel has one sound, and only one sound. There is no "long" or "short" version. Spanish has exactly five vowels: A, E, I, O, U. To pronounce them correctly, you must keep your mouth, tongue, and lips relatively tense and fixed in one position while making the sound. Do not let your mouth slide into another shape, which is what we naturally do in English. Here are the five pure vowel sounds: A (ah): Open your mouth wide, as if a doctor is asking you to say "ah." It sounds like the "a" in father. Example: amigo (friend), agua (water) E (eh): Keep your mouth slightly open, lips relaxed to the sides. It sounds like the "e" in pet or bet, but shorter and crisper. Example: entender (to understand), leche (milk) I (ee): Stretch your lips into a slight smile. It sounds like the "ee" in machine or see. Example: sí (yes), día (day) O (oh): Make your lips into a tight circle. It sounds like the "o" in boat, but without the "w" sound at the end. 2. Greetings, Introductions, and Polite Phrases

The Social Compass: Formal vs. Informal You step into a café in Madrid. The bartender smiles and says, "¡Hola! ¿Qué tal?" A few minutes later, an older businessman walks in, and the bartender shifts to a noticeably different tone, saying, "Buenos días, señor. ¿Cómo está usted?" Why the switch? Spanish-speaking cultures place a high value on social respect. Before you even learn how to say your name, you need to understand the social compass that guides almost every interaction in Spanish: the difference between informal (casual) and formal (polite) speech. You use the informal register when speaking with friends, family, peers, children, and pets. You use the formal register when speaking to elders, people in positions of authority (like a police officer or a boss), or anyone you have just met where you want to show respect. The simplest way to trigger this shift is by choosing the right word for "you." Tú: The informal "you." Use this with your amigo (friend). Usted: The formal "you." Use this with a stranger or an elder. Throughout this chapter, you will see how this formal/informal distinction changes the greetings and questions you use. Greetings Based on the Time of Day In English, "Hello" and "Hi" can be used at any time of the day or night. In Spanish, while there is a general greeting, the most common way to greet someone is by acknowledging the time of day. Because Spanish is a highly phonetic language—meaning words are pronounced exactly as they are written based on the rules you learned in the previous chapter—mastering these greetings is mostly a matter of remembering the vowels (A, E, I, O, U). Morning Greetings To say "Good morning," you use: Buenos días Let's break it down using your phonetic knowledge: Buenos: Bweh-nohs. The u is silent, and the e makes the "eh" sound, while the o makes the "oh" sound. Días: Dee-ahs. The í makes the "ee" sound, and the a makes the "ah" sound. Notice that the emphasis is on the first syllable (DEE-ahs) because of the accent mark over the í. Afternoon Greetings To say "Good afternoon," you use: Buenas tardes Buenas: Bweh-nahs. Tardes: Tar-dehs. Remember, Spanish does not have that puff of air after the t like English does. Keep it crisp. Evening/Night Greetings To say "Good evening" or "Good night," you use: Buenas noches Noches: Noh-chehs. The ch in Spanish sounds just like the "ch" in the English word "chair." You might have noticed that buenos ends in an "s" (for morning), but buenas ends in an "a" (for afternoon and night). 3. Nouns, Gender, and Articles

The Concept of Grammatical Gender Imagine you are sitting at an outdoor café in Madrid. You want to ask the waiter for the bill. You might think to yourself, "I'll just ask for the bill." But in Spanish, you cannot simply say "the bill." You have to know whether the word for bill (cuenta) is a "masculine" word or a "feminine" word before you can use the word "the." In English, we rarely worry about the gender of objects. A table is an "it." A book is an "it." A dog is an "it" (unless we know the dog's specific sex). Spanish, however, is a gendered language. This means every single noun—whether it represents a person, a place, a thing, or an idea—is categorized as either masculine or feminine. This concept is known as grammatical gender. It is important to understand right away that grammatical gender is mostly just a system of categorization. A table (mesa) is feminine, but the table itself does not possess female biological traits. A book (libro) is masculine, but it is not biologically male. It is simply a feature of the language, much like a sorting hat that puts every word into one of two buckets. Before we can form basic sentences, we need to learn how to identify which bucket a noun belongs to, and how to pair those nouns with words like "the" or "a." What is a Noun? Before we dive deeper into gender, let's define our core building block. A noun (in Spanish, sustantivo) is a word that represents a person, place, thing, or idea. Person: amigo (friend), perro (dog) Place: casa (house), escuela (school) Thing: libro (book), ojo (eye) Idea: tiempo (time), amor (love) As we learned in previous chapters, Spanish is a highly phonetic language, meaning words are spelled exactly as they sound. This phonetic predictability will be your best friend when learning noun genders, because the gender of a noun is almost entirely determined by how the word ends. Spotting Masculine and Feminine Nouns How do you know if a noun is masculine or feminine? You look at the last letter of the word. While there are exceptions (which we will look at shortly), there are two golden rules that cover about 80% of Spanish nouns: 1. Nouns ending in -o are generally masculine. 2. Nouns ending in -a are generally feminine. The Masculine "-o" Ending If you see a noun that ends with the letter o, you can safely assume it is masculine. 4. Subject Pronouns and the Verb 'Ser'

The Foundation of Identity Imagine you are at a café in Madrid. A friend waves you over to their table, where two new people are sitting. Your friend says, "¡Hola! Este es Marco. Él es profesor. Ella es Ana. Ella es doctora." Without knowing a single word of Spanish, you might guess from the context and gestures that you are being introduced to Marco, a teacher, and Ana, a doctor. But to actually hold this conversation yourself, you need the tools to say who someone is—or who you are. In Spanish, expressing identity requires two fundamental building blocks: the words we use to replace people's names (subject pronouns) and the verb that means "to be" when talking about permanent traits (the verb ser). Because Spanish verbs change their spelling depending on who is doing the action, we must learn the subject pronouns before we can use any verb correctly. Subject Pronouns: Who Are We Talking About? In English, we use subject pronouns all the time to avoid repeating a person's name over and over. Instead of saying, "Maria is my friend. Maria is from Spain," we say, "Maria is my friend. She is from Spain." Spanish works exactly the same way. A subject pronoun is a word that replaces the name of the person or thing performing the action in a sentence. Here is the complete list of Spanish subject pronouns. Notice how they are organized into two categories: singular (referring to one person) and plural (referring to more than one person). Singular Subject Pronouns (One Person) Yo — I Tú — You (informal, speaking to one person) Él — He Ella — She Usted — You (formal, speaking to one person) Plural Subject Pronouns (Multiple People) Nosotros / Nosotras — We Vosotros / Vosotras — You all (informal, speaking to multiple people; primarily used in Spain) Ellos / Ellas — They Ustedes — You all (formal or general, speaking to multiple people) Understanding the Nuances A few of these pronouns have specific rules you need to know right from the start: 1. Formal vs. Informal "You" English has just one word for "you," which we use whether we are talking to a toddler, a close friend, or a judge. Spanish, however, distinguishes between relationships. Use tú when speaking to a friend, a peer, a family member, or a child. Use usted (often abbreviated as Ud.) when speaking to an elder, a boss, or someone you just met to show respect. 5. Numbers, Time, and Calendar Basics

Counting from 0 to 100 Picture yourself at a bustling street market in Madrid. You point to a bag of fresh strawberries, and the vendor holds up two fingers, saying, "Dos euros." To complete the transaction, you need to understand the price, count out the correct change, and perhaps ask for the time if you are meeting a friend later. Numbers are the backbone of everyday communication, and the good news is that Spanish numbers follow highly logical, predictable patterns. Let’s build your counting skills from the ground up, starting with the foundational numbers from 0 to 10. Since Spanish is a phonetic language, you can sound these out using the vowel rules we established in Chapter 1 (A = ah, E = eh, I = ee, O = oh, U = oo). 0 – cero 1 – uno 2 – dos 3 – tres 4 – cuatro 5 – cinco 6 – seis 7 – siete 8 – ocho 9 – nueve 10 – diez Next, let's look at 11 through 15. These have unique names, but you might notice they all end with the same sound: "-ce" (pronounced seh or theh depending on the regional accent). 11 – once 12 – doce 13 – trece 14 – catorce 15 – quince Here is where the first major pattern emerges. From 16 to 19, Spanish essentially says "ten and six," "ten and seven," and so on. They are compound words built on diez. 16 – dieciséis (diez y seis) 17 – diecisiete (diez y siete) 18 – dieciocho (diez y ocho) 19 – diecinueve (diez y nueve) The Tens and Combining Numbers Now let's learn the tens. Memorizing these is essential, as they serve as the base for all numbers up to 100. 20 – veinte 30 – treinta 40 – cuarenta 50 – cincuenta 60 – sesenta 70 – setenta 80 – ochenta 90 – noventa 100 – cien (Note: Use cien only for exactly 100. When counting to 101 and beyond, it changes to ciento, but for now, cien is all you need.) To say numbers like 21 or 35, you simply combine the tens and the units. For numbers 31 through 99, you use the word y (and) between the tens and the units. 6. The Verb 'Estar', Locations, and Basic Adjectives

The Tale of Two "To Be" Verbs In Chapter 4, you learned how to introduce yourself and describe fundamental characteristics using the verb ser. You learned to say Soy estudiante (I am a student) or Ellos son de México (They are from Mexico). But what happens when you want to tell a friend where you are right now? Or how you are feeling today? If you try to use ser for these situations, a Spanish speaker will give you a confused look. You might accidentally say something equivalent to "I am a tired" or "She is a library." Spanish solves this problem by having two distinct verbs for the English word "to be." You already know the first one. It is time to meet the second: estar. When to Use Ser vs. Estar Before we learn how to conjugate estar, it helps to know why we need it. Here is the golden rule for telling these two verbs apart: Use Ser for the permanent, the inherent, and the essential. This includes identities, occupations, origins, physical traits, and the time/date (which you practiced in Chapter 5). Think of ser as the "essence" verb. Use Estar for the temporary and the locational. This includes how you are feeling right now, temporary states, and the physical location of people and objects. Think of estar as the "status" and "location" verb. Let’s look at a concrete scenario to see the difference side-by-side. Imagine a woman named Ana. Ana es alta. (Ana is tall.) - We use ser because her height is a permanent physical trait. Ana está en la biblioteca. (Ana is at the library.) - We use estar because this is her current physical location. Ana está cansada. (Ana is tired.) - We use estar because being tired is a temporary state. Tomorrow, she will be well-rested. A helpful mnemonic device many Spanish learners use is the acronym PLACE. You use estar for: Position (where something is physically placed) Location (where a person or thing is) Action (the progressive tense, which you will learn much later) Condition (temporary states like being happy, sick, or cold) Emotion (how you are feeling right now) For now, we will focus on Location and Condition. 7. Regular Present-Tense Verbs and Sentence Structure

The Engine of Spanish: Regular Verbs Imagine you are planning a dinner party in Madrid. You already know how to greet your guests using phrases from Chapter 2. You know how to identify the people, places, and things around you using the nouns and articles from Chapter 3. You can even tell everyone who you are using the verb ser (Chapter 4) and where the party is happening using the verb estar (Chapter 6). But to actually pull off this dinner, you need action. You need to cook, eat, drink, and share. To express any action in Spanish, you need verbs. Up until now, you have learned two highly irregular verbs (ser and estar). Because they are irregular, they have their own unique puzzle pieces that you simply had to memorize. The great news is that the vast majority of Spanish verbs follow strict, predictable rules. These are called regular verbs. Once you learn the pattern for one regular verb, you can apply it to hundreds of others. The Anatomy of a Verb Before we learn the patterns, let's define a couple of terms so we are on the same page: Infinitive: The basic, unconjugated form of a verb. In English, infinitives usually start with the word "to" (to speak, to eat, to live). In Spanish, infinitives are just a single word that always ends in either -ar, -er, or -ir. Conjugation: The act of changing a verb's ending so that it matches the person doing the action (the subject) and the time frame (the tense). In the present tense, conjugation tells us who is doing the action right now. Let's look at the three families of regular verbs, starting with the largest family: the -ar verbs. Conjugating Regular -ar Verbs About 80% of all Spanish verbs end in -ar. To conjugate them, we use a simple two-step process: 1. Drop the -ar ending to find the verb's root (or stem). 2. Add a new ending that matches the subject pronoun. Let's use the verb hablar (to speak) as our model. We drop the -ar to get the root habl-. 8. Common Irregular Verbs and Expressing Preferences

You are chatting with a new friend in Madrid. You want to tell them that you are 25 years old, that you are going to a coffee shop later, and that you love reading. If you translate these thoughts directly from English, you might say, “Yo soy 25 años,” “Yo voy a la cafetería,” and “Yo amo leer.” While a native speaker might understand your general meaning, all three of these sentences sound a bit unnatural in Spanish. To talk about your age, your future plans, and your hobbies, you need three essential, high-frequency verbs: tener, ir, and gustar. Unlike the regular verbs we studied in the previous module, these three verbs follow their own unique patterns. Let’s look at how they work. The Verb "Tener": Expressing Age and Possession In English, we use the verb "to be" to talk about age ("I am 25 years old"). In Spanish, we use the verb tener, which literally means "to have." So, in Spanish, you do not are an age; you have an age. Tener is an irregular verb in the present tense. This means it does not follow the standard conjugation rules we learned in Chapter 7. Notice how the "e" changes to "ie" in most forms, and the "yo" form is completely unique. Yo tengo (I have) Tú tienes (You have) Él/Ella/Usted tiene (He/She/You formal have) Nosotros/Nosotras tenemos (We have) Vosotros/Vosotras tenéis (You all have) Ellos/Ellas/Ustedes tienen (They/You all have) Expressing Age To tell someone your age, combine the correct form of tener with a number and the word años (years). Tengo veinte años. (I am twenty years old. / Literally: I have twenty years.) ¿Cuántos años tienes? (How old are you? / Literally: How many years do you have?) Expressing Possession We use tener to express possession, just like we use "have" in English. You learned about nouns, gender, and articles in Chapter 3. When using tener to say what you own, you generally use the indefinite articles (un, una, unos, unas - a, some) unless you are talking about something specific. Tengo un perro. (I have a dog.) Ella tiene una bicicleta. (She has a bicycle.) Tenemos unos amigos en México. (We have some friends in Mexico.)
