3. Extruding Shapes into 3D Models

From Flat to Full: Turning 2D Sketches into 3D Objects Imagine drawing a simple rectangle on a piece of paper. Now, imagine lifting that rectangle off the page and giving it thickness—suddenly, it's not just a flat shape anymore. It's a box. That transformation—from flat to full—is what extrusion is all about. In SketchUp, you can take any 2D shape you draw and push or pull it into a 3D object with just a few clicks. This chapter will show you how to do exactly that using the Push/Pull tool, one of SketchUp's most powerful and intuitive features. By the end of this chapter, you'll be able to turn basic shapes like rectangles, circles, and triangles into solid 3D forms. You'll learn how to control the height, depth, and even the direction of your extrusions. Most importantly, you'll start to see how 3D modeling isn't about building impossibly complex objects right away—it's about starting small, thinking in layers, and building up one push at a time. --- Understanding Extrusion in 3D Space Before you start pushing and pulling, let’s clarify what extrusion means in the context of 3D modeling. Extrusion is the process of taking a 2D shape and extending it along a third axis (usually the Z-axis, which points upward in SketchUp) to give it volume. Think of it like stretching a piece of clay: you start with a flat base, then push or pull it to create a 3D form. In SketchUp, this is done using the Push/Pull tool, which is represented by an icon that looks like a small cube with an arrow pointing outward. This tool lets you select a face (a flat surface) and pull it outward or push it inward to create depth. Why Extrusion Matters Extrusion is the foundation of most 3D modeling in SketchUp. Whether you're building a house, a piece of furniture, or a mechanical part, you'll likely start with a 2D shape and extrude it into a 3D object. It’s the simplest way to give your models volume and realism. --- The Push/Pull Tool: Your First Step into 3D The Push/Pull tool is one of the most frequently used tools in SketchUp. 4. Organizing Your Model with Layers and Groups

Why Your SketchUp Model Needs Organization Imagine you’ve just spent an hour carefully drawing a small house—a cozy little cottage with a pitched roof, a front porch, and a tiny garden. You’re proud of your work, but now you want to add a fence. As you draw, you accidentally select part of the roof or porch instead. Suddenly, you’re dragging the entire house across the screen. Frustrating, right? This happens because, by default, SketchUp treats everything in your model as a single, connected piece of geometry. There’s no separation between the roof, walls, or garden. While this “one big blob” approach works for simple sketches, it quickly becomes chaotic as your model grows. That’s where layers and groups come in. They help you organize your work so you can edit parts of your model without affecting everything else. In this chapter, you’ll learn how to use layers to categorize your model’s parts and groups to isolate them for editing. You’ll also see how the Outliner panel becomes your best friend for managing these organizational tools. By the end, your cottage—and any future model—will stay tidy and easy to work with. --- Understanding Layers: What They Are and Why They Matter What Is a Layer? In SketchUp, a layer acts like a transparent sheet of paper that can hold parts of your model. You can turn layers on or off to show or hide certain elements without deleting them. For example, you might put all the walls on one layer, the roof on another, and the furniture on a third. Layers help you: - Hide parts of the model you’re not working on, reducing visual clutter. - Isolate specific elements when you need to focus on one area. - Control visibility across different views or scenes (a concept we’ll explore later). Think of layers as categories rather than containers. They don’t group geometry together like folders; instead, they tag geometry with a label so you can show or hide it as needed. How Layers Work in SketchUp When you start a new model in SketchUp, you begin with a single default layer called "Layer0." Everything you draw is automatically placed on Layer0. 5. Working with Components and the 3D Warehouse

Why Reinvent the Wheel? Building Faster with Reusable Objects Imagine you're designing a small café. You need ten identical wooden tables, each with four legs and a flat top. You could draw each table from scratch—extruding a rectangle for the top, then carefully positioning four legs for each one. But after the first table, your hands ache, your coffee gets cold, and you start wondering if SketchUp has a "copy-paste for furniture" button. Spoiler: it does. It’s called components. Components are like digital Lego pieces—once you build or download one, you can reuse it anywhere in your model without redrawing it. Change the original, and every copy updates automatically. Need 50 identical chairs? Done in seconds. Want to swap all the tables for metal ones later? Edit one, and they all change. And if you don’t want to build them yourself? The 3D Warehouse is SketchUp’s free online library of 3D models—everything from light fixtures to entire buildings—created by users around the world. Need a realistic sofa, a toilet, or a vintage coffee machine? Chances are, someone’s already modeled it and uploaded it for free. This chapter shows you how to create, edit, and manage components, then dive into the 3D Warehouse to find, place, and customize models—without starting from zero. --- Groups vs. Components: What’s the Difference? Before you start reusing objects, you need to understand two key SketchUp concepts: groups and components. Both let you bundle geometry together so you can move, rotate, or copy multiple objects at once. But they behave very differently when you edit them. | Feature | Group | Component | |--------|----------|---------------| | Reusability | Single-use. Copies are independent. | Multi-use. Copies are linked. | | Editing | Changes affect only that group. | Changes affect all instances. | | File size | Each copy is stored separately. | One definition shared across copies. | | Use case | Temporary organization (e.g., a room’s walls). | Repeated elements (e.g., chairs, doors). | When to Use a Group Groups are great for isolating parts of your model temporarily. But if you double-click into the group and edit it, only that instance changes. 6. Modifying Geometry with Move, Rotate, and Scale Tools

The "Almost There" Dilemma Imagine you’ve just spent an hour meticulously modeling a living room. You’ve used the tools from previous chapters to extrude the walls and you've imported a beautiful sofa and a coffee table from the 3D Warehouse. Everything looks great—except the sofa is sitting halfway through a wall, the coffee table is facing the wrong way, and the armchair you modeled is twice the size of a real human being. This is where the "Transformation Tools" come in. In 3D modeling, creating an object is only half the battle; the other half is positioning it. Whether you are designing a kitchen or a city block, you will spend a significant portion of your time moving, rotating, and scaling your geometry to fit the space. The Move Tool: Positioning Your World The Move tool is the most frequently used tool in SketchUp. While it seems simple—picking something up and putting it somewhere else—it is actually a multi-purpose tool used for repositioning, copying, and stretching. Basic Movement in 3D Space To activate the Move tool, click the four-way arrow icon in the toolbar or press the M key on your keyboard. 1. Select the Geometry: Click on the face, edge, or Group/Component you want to move. 2. Set the Starting Point: Click once on the object to "grab" it. 3. Move the Object: Drag your mouse in the direction you want the object to go. 4. Set the Destination: Click again to drop the object in its new location. Locking Your Axis Because SketchUp operates in a 3D environment, it is very easy to accidentally move an object slightly "up" when you meant to move it "sideways." To prevent this, you must use Axis Locking. SketchUp uses a color-coded system to help you stay precise: Red Axis: Moves the object along the X-axis (left/right). Green Axis: Moves the object along the Y-axis (forward/back). Blue Axis: Moves the object along the Z-axis (up/down). Pro Tip: To lock your movement to a specific axis, use the Arrow Keys on your keyboard while moving an object: Right Arrow: Locks to the Red axis. Left Arrow: Locks to the Green axis. Up Arrow: Locks to the Blue axis. Precision Movement Clicking and dragging is great for "eyeballing" a position, but professional modeling requires exact measurements. Once you have started moving an object and locked it to an axis, stop moving your mouse and simply type the distance you want into the Measurements Box (located in the bottom right corner of the screen) and press Enter. 7. Creating and Editing Surfaces with Follow Me and Sandbox Tools

Beyond the Box: Adding Curves and Terrain Imagine you are designing a house. So far, you have mastered the art of the "box"—creating walls, floors, and rectangular rooms using the tools from Extruding Shapes into 3D Models. But real life isn't made of boxes. Think of the crown molding where the wall meets the ceiling, the curved sweep of a banister, the circular rim of a coffee mug, or the rolling hills of a backyard. If you tried to build a curved pipe or a mountain using only the Push/Pull tool, you would be stuck. To create these organic, flowing shapes, you need a different approach. Instead of pushing a face in a straight line, you need to move a shape along a path. This is where the Follow Me tool and the Sandbox toolset come into play. Mastering the Follow Me Tool The Follow Me tool is one of the most powerful features in SketchUp. In simple terms, it takes a 2D shape (the profile) and slides it along a line (the path) to create a 3D volume. Think of it like a bead on a string. The bead is your profile, and the string is your path. As the bead slides along the string, it leaves a solid trail behind it. The Anatomy of a Follow Me Operation To use this tool successfully, you need two distinct elements: 1. The Path: A series of connected lines or arcs that define the direction the shape will travel. This could be a circle, a wavy line, or a complex zig-zag. 2. The Profile: A flat, closed face that represents the cross-section of the object you are creating. For a pipe, the profile is a small circle; for a picture frame, it is the decorative molding shape. Step-by-Step: Creating a Simple Curved Pipe Let's put this into practice by creating a curved pipe. 1. Draw the Path: Use the Line tool or Arc tool to draw a path on the ground plane. For this example, draw a semi-circle. 2. Draw the Profile: This is the most important step for beginners. Your profile must be perpendicular (at a 90-degree angle) to the start of your path. Go to the start point of your semi-circle. Draw a small circle that stands upright, facing the direction the path travels. 3. Select the Path: Click on the semi-circle path to highlight it in blue. Crucial: You must select the path before activating the tool. 4. Activate Follow Me: Find the Follow Me tool (usually tucked under the Push/Pull menu). 5. 8. Applying Materials and Textures to Your Model

From Gray Boxes to Living Spaces Imagine you have just finished modeling a modern living room. You’ve used the tools from previous chapters to extrude the walls, place a sofa from the 3D Warehouse, and arrange a coffee table. Right now, your model looks like a collection of sterile, gray and white geometric shapes. It is mathematically correct, but it doesn't feel like a home. The difference between a technical schematic and a believable visualization is Materials. By applying colors, textures, and images, you transform a generic 3D volume into a space with warmth, weight, and character. You can make a surface feel like cold polished concrete, warm oak wood, or soft linen fabric. Understanding Materials and Textures Before we dive into the tools, it is important to distinguish between two terms you will see frequently: Materials and Textures. Material: In SketchUp, a material is a "paint" that you apply to a surface. A material can be a simple solid color (like a red wall) or a complex image (like a brick pattern). Texture: A texture is the specific image file used within a material to simulate a real-world surface. When you apply a "Brick" material, you are actually applying a photographic image of bricks that repeats across the surface. Materials are applied to Faces—the flat surfaces created when you draw lines and extrude shapes. While you can apply colors to edges, the vast majority of your work will involve painting the faces of your model. The Paint Bucket Tool and Materials Browser The primary tool for adding realism is the Paint Bucket tool. Accessing the Tool Depending on whether you are using the Web App or the Desktop App, the location varies slightly, but the icon is always a tipping paint bucket. When you activate the Paint Bucket tool, the Materials Browser (or Materials Panel) opens. This is your library of every available finish in SketchUp. Navigating the Materials Browser The Materials Browser is organized into categories to help you find what you need quickly. You will see folders such as: Colors: Basic solid hues. Wood: Various grains, planks, and polished finishes. Stone: Marble, granite, and slate. Water: Transparent and reflective blue tones. Landscaping: Grass, mulch, and dirt. Pro Tip: If you can't find a specific look, use the search bar at the top of the browser. Typing "Concrete" will filter out all other categories and show you every concrete-related material available. 9. Adding Realistic Lighting and Shadows

Why Lighting Changes Everything Imagine you have spent hours meticulously building a modern house. You have used the tools from Extruding Shapes into 3D Models to create the structure and applied high-end textures from Applying Materials and Textures to Your Model. Yet, when you look at the screen, the house looks "flat." It feels like a floating plastic toy rather than a building. The missing ingredient is depth. In the real world, our brains perceive depth and scale through the way light hits a surface and the shadows that result. Without shadows, a wall doesn't look like it's standing on the ground; it looks like it's hovering. By mastering lighting, you transform a geometric exercise into a believable space. Understanding the SketchUp Sun System Unlike professional movie software where you manually place "lamps" or "spotlights," SketchUp uses a Global Illumination system based on a single, powerful light source: the Sun. SketchUp simulates the real-world movement of the sun. This means the lighting in your model isn't just a visual effect; it is tied to a specific location on Earth, a specific date, and a specific time of day. When you change the time to 6:00 PM, the sun drops lower in the sky, and your shadows automatically stretch longer—just as they do in your backyard. Accessing and Using the Shadows Panel To begin adding depth to your model, you need to access the Shadows Panel. Depending on whether you are using the Web App or the Desktop App, the location varies slightly, but the controls remain the same. Enabling Shadows By default, shadows are turned off to save computer processing power. To turn them on: 1. Open the Shadows Panel (found in the "Display" or "View" settings). 2. Toggle the Shadows switch to "On." Immediately, you will notice your model change. Surfaces facing the sun will be bright, while surfaces facing away will darken. You will also see Cast Shadows—the dark shapes projected onto the ground or other objects. The "On/Off" Workflow A common beginner mistake is leaving shadows on while doing heavy modeling. Because the computer has to calculate the angle of light for every single face in your model, shadows can cause "lag" (a delay in how the software responds to your mouse). Pro Tip: Keep shadows off while you are drawing, moving, or scaling geometry. Turn them on only when you are checking the "feel" of the space or preparing a final view. Controlling the Sun: Date and Time Since SketchUp treats the sun as a real-world entity, you don't "move" the light with a tool. Instead, you change the Date and Time settings. In the real world, a photographer doesn't just wander randomly around a building; they scout specific "shots" and mark them. In SketchUp, Scenes act as your saved camera bookmarks. Instead of manually orbiting and zooming every time you want to show a specific part of your model, you can click a single button to snap the camera back to a precise, pre-defined vantage point. Mastering Scenes A Scene is a saved snapshot of your model's current state. It doesn't just save where the camera is pointing; it saves the "state" of the window, including which styles are active and whether shadows are turned on. Creating Your First Scene To create a scene, you first need to position your camera exactly where you want it. Open the Scenes Panel: In the Web App, this is found in the right-hand toolbar. In the Desktop App, go to Window Scenes. 3. Add the Scene: Click the (+) icon or "Add Scene." 4. Name the Scene: By default, SketchUp names these "Scene 1," "Scene 2," and so on. Double-click the name to rename it to something descriptive, such as "Front Entrance" or "Interior Kitchen View." 11. Introduction to Section Cuts and Clipping Planes

The "X-Ray Vision" Challenge Imagine you have spent hours meticulously building a detailed 3D model of a two-story house. You’ve used the techniques from Extruding Shapes into 3D Models to build the walls and Working with Components to place furniture in every room. Now, you want to show a client—or check for yourself—exactly how the kitchen cabinets align with the plumbing in the wall, or how the stairs fit into the second-floor landing. If you try to look inside the house using the standard orbit and zoom tools, you’ll find yourself "fighting" the walls. You are either looking at the outside of the building, or you are zoomed in so close to a wall that you can't see the rest of the room. You can't simply "remove" a wall because that would destroy your geometry. This is where Section Cuts come in. In the world of architecture and design, a section cut is like taking a giant, invisible knife and slicing through your model to reveal exactly what is happening inside. Instead of orbiting around the exterior, you can create a Clipping Plane—a boundary that tells SketchUp, "Hide everything on this side of the line so I can see what's inside." Understanding Section Planes A Section Plane is a specialized tool in SketchUp that creates a temporary "slice" through your 3D geometry. It is important to understand that a section plane does not actually delete or cut your geometry. It doesn't "break" your walls or remove pieces of your model. Instead, it simply hides the portion of the model that falls on one side of the plane. Think of it as a magical window. When the plane is active, the software stops rendering the "clipped" part of the model, giving you a clear, unobstructed view of the interior. Why Use Section Cuts? Section cuts are essential for several reasons: Interior Inspection: Checking that furniture fits and that there is enough clearance for walking paths. Technical Verification: Ensuring that floors, walls, and ceilings intersect correctly. Presentation: Creating "dollhouse" views that allow a viewer to see the floor plan and the vertical height of the rooms simultaneously. Complex Geometry: If you are modeling something complex—like an engine or a piece of machinery—section cuts allow you to see the internal mechanisms without deleting the outer casing. This is where Rendering and Exporting come in. While "modeling" is the act of building, "presentation" is the act of communicating. Understanding Rendering vs. Exporting Before we dive into the buttons and menus, it is important to distinguish between these two terms, as they are often used interchangeably but mean very different things in the 3D world. Exporting is the process of saving your model into a different file format so it can be opened by other software. Think of it like translating a book from English to Spanish; the content remains the same, but the "language" (the file extension) changes so a different reader can understand it. Rendering is the process of generating a high-quality image from your 3D scene. While your screen shows a "real-time" preview of your model, a render calculates how light, shadows, and materials interact to create a final, polished picture. In SketchUp, we deal with two types of rendering: 1. Real-time Rendering: What you see while you work (which we've enhanced using the Lighting and Shadows techniques from Chapter 9). 2. External Rendering: Using third-party software to create photo-realistic images (which we will touch upon as a future step, but for now, we will focus on SketchUp's built-in capabilities). Now, you want to add a spiral staircase, a complex curved vaulted ceiling, and a perfectly manicured hedge surrounding the property. If you rely solely on the standard toolset, you might spend hours manually drawing every step of that staircase or struggling to warp a surface into a curve. But what if you could click a single button to generate a staircase based on a height measurement? Or use a tool that "grows" a hedge along a path automatically? This is where Plugins (also known as Extensions) come in. They are essentially "power-ups" for SketchUp—small pieces of software created by developers that add new tools and capabilities to the Desktop App that aren't there by default. Understanding Plugins and Extensions In the world of SketchUp, the terms "Plugin" and "Extension" are used interchangeably. A plugin is an add-on that extends the functionality of the software. While the core tools you've learned—like Extruding Shapes into 3D Models—are powerful, plugins allow you to perform complex geometric operations with a fraction of the effort. How Plugins Work Plugins usually appear in your interface in one of three ways: 1. Toolbar Icons: New buttons that appear in your top menu. 2. Menu Items: New options found under the "Extensions" or "Tools" menu. 3. Context Menus: New options that appear when you right-click an object. The Difference Between Web and Desktop It is important to note that plugins are primarily a feature of the SketchUp Pro Desktop App. Because the Web App runs in a browser, it has limited ability to run third-party external code. If you are using the Web App, you have access to the core tools, but to unlock the full library of community-made extensions, you will need to move to the Desktop version. Installing and Managing Extensions Installing a plugin is a straightforward process, but it requires knowing where to find them safely. The Extension Warehouse The Extension Warehouse is the official "app store" for SketchUp. It is the safest place to get plugins because the extensions are vetted and integrated directly into the software. To install a plugin from the Warehouse: 1. Open the Desktop App. 2. Go to the Extensions menu and select Extension Warehouse. 3. Browse or search for the tool you need. 4. Click Install. The tool will automatically download and add its icons to your screen.
