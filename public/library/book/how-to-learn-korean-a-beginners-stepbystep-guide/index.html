1. The Korean Alphabet (Hangul)

Why Hangul Is Different Imagine trying to learn a language where you have to memorize thousands of unique, complex symbols just to read a basic newspaper. For centuries, that was the reality for speakers of Korean, who relied on Classical Chinese characters. Because these characters were so difficult to learn, only the wealthy and highly educated could read and write. In 1443, King Sejong the Great decided this was unacceptable. He and a group of scholars created a new alphabet from scratch, designed so that "a wise man can acquaint himself with them before the morning is over; and even a stupid man can learn them in the space of ten days." This alphabet is Hangul (한글). Unlike the English alphabet, where letters often look nothing like how they sound (consider the "c" in cat versus ceiling), Hangul is highly phonetic. Even more fascinating, the shapes of the consonants are based on the physical shape your mouth, tongue, and lips make when pronouncing them. Hangul is widely considered one of the most scientific and logical writing systems in the world. In this chapter, you will learn all 40 characters of Hangul, how to piece them together into words, and how to start typing them on your computer or phone. The 10 Basic Vowels Let's start with vowels. In English, vowels are A, E, I, O, U. In Hangul, there are 10 basic vowels. Hangul vowels are drawn using combinations of just two types of strokes: Vertical strokes: ㅣ (pronounced like the "ee" in "see") Horizontal strokes: ㅡ (pronounced like the "u" in "up," but flattened—smile slightly while saying "ee") To create the other eight basic vowels, we simply add a short stroke (either to the right of the vertical line, or above the horizontal line). Here are the 10 basic vowels, their Romanization (English alphabet equivalent), and how to pronounce them: Vertical Vowels These are written vertically. When forming a syllable, vertical vowels will be placed to the right of a consonant. ㅏ (a): Pronounced like the "a" in "father." (A vertical line with a short stroke to the right). ㅐ (ae): Pronounced like the "e" in "pet." ㅑ (ya): Pronounced like "ya" in "yard." (Two short strokes to the right). ㅒ (yae): Pronounced like "ye" in "yes." ㅓ (eo): Pronounced like the "uh" in "up" or the "o" in "son." (A vertical line with a short stroke to the left). ㅔ (e): Pronounced like the "e" in "pet." 2. Pronunciation and Sound Change Rules

The Illusion of the Spoken Word You have memorized the Hangul characters. You know that 가 is pronounced "ga," and that 나 is pronounced "na." You feel confident reading Korean words off a page. But then you hear a native speaker say 감사합니다 (thank you), and suddenly your confidence shatters. Wait, did they just say "gamsahamnida"? It sounded more like "gamsahamnida" on paper, but the "h" seemed to vanish, and the "n" sounded suspiciously like an "m." Welcome to the reality of Korean pronunciation. In English, we are used to words looking very different from how they sound, but we expect phonetic alphabets like Hangul to be strictly "what you see is what you get." While Hangul is incredibly logical, it is designed to reflect the natural, physical flow of human speech. When Koreans speak quickly and naturally, consonants and vowels blend, shift, and sometimes disappear entirely. To move beyond reading like a robot and start sounding natural, you need to understand the physics of Korean sounds and the rules that govern how they change in real-time conversation. The Three Faces of Korean Consonants In Chapter 1, you were introduced to the basic consonants: ㄱ, ㄴ, ㅁ, ㅅ, and ㅇ. However, Korean organizes its consonants into families based on how they are produced in the mouth. Take the "k/g" sound, for example. You can produce this sound in three distinct ways: 1. Plain: A light, relaxed burst of air. 2. Aspirated: A heavy burst of air accompanying the sound. 3. Tense: A tight, strained sound with no burst of air. Let’s break down how to physically produce each type. Plain, Aspirated, and Tense Consonants Plain consonants are the standard versions you learned in Chapter 1. They are pronounced with a light, gentle breath. ㄱ (g/k): Like the g in "guess." ㄷ (d/t): Like the d in "dog." ㅂ (b/p): Like the b in "book." Aspirated consonants add a heavy puff of air—what linguists call aspiration. You can test this by holding your hand in front of your mouth. If you feel a strong burst of air hit your palm, it is aspirated. To write these in Hangul, an extra horizontal stroke is added to the plain consonant. ㅋ (k): Like the k in "kite." ㅌ (t): Like the t in "top." ㅍ (p): Like the p in "pen." Tense consonants are the trickiest for beginners. There is no equivalent in English. To make a tense consonant, you tense the muscles in your throat and vocal cords, completely stopping the flow of air, before releasing the sound. It sounds tight, sharp, and almost stressed. 3. Sentence Structure and Politeness Levels

The Great Word Scramble: Understanding SOV Order Imagine you are back in elementary school, learning your very first English sentence. Your teacher holds up an apple and says, "I eat an apple." The order is burned into your brain: Subject (I) + Verb (eat) + Object (an apple). In English, the person doing the action comes first, the action itself comes second, and the thing receiving the action comes last. We call this SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) order. Korean, however, plays by a completely different set of rules. If you translate "I eat an apple" directly into Korean, the literal translation comes out as: "I an apple eat." This is the SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) sentence structure. In Korean, the verb always comes at the very end of the sentence. Not sometimes, not usually—always. Whether you are saying a simple greeting or describing a complex event, the action or state of being is the final piece of the puzzle. Let’s break down the components of this structure using terminology you will encounter throughout your Korean learning journey: Subject: The person, place, or thing doing the action. (e.g., I, she, the dog). Object: The person, place, or thing receiving the action. (e.g., an apple, a book, a ball). Verb: The action or state of being. (e.g., eat, read, is). If you want to say "Minji reads a book," the English order is: 1. Minji (Subject) 2. reads (Verb) 3. a book (Object) But in Korean, you scramble the order to: 1. Minji (Subject) 2. a book (Object) 3. reads (Verb) Why does Korean do this? The simplest way to think about it is that Korean saves the most important information—the action—for the very end. When listening to a Korean sentence, your brain is waiting for the verb to find out what actually happened. Did Minji read the book? Did she buy the book? Did she throw the book? You won't know until the final syllable drops. The "Head-Last" Rule This verb-at-the-end rule applies to more than just verbs. It applies to almost every part of a Korean sentence. In linguistics, Korean is considered a "head-final" language. For now, as a beginner, the main takeaway is this: whenever you construct a sentence in your head, push the verb to the back. If you want to say, "I go to school," the order is: "I + to school + go." If you want to say, "I drink coffee," the order is: "I + coffee + drink." You might be wondering, "How do I know which word is the subject and which is the object if they are scrambled?" That is an excellent question. 4. Essential Vocabulary and Number Systems

The Two Faces of Korean Numbers You step into a café in Seoul. The barista asks what you'd like. You order a coffee, and then comes the follow-up question: "하나요?" (hana-yo?). You might not recognize the word, but the barissa is holding up one finger and asking, "Just one?" You nod. A few moments later, the register displays 3,000. The barista says, "삼천 원이에요" (sam-cheon won-i-e-yo). Why did the barista use hana for "one" but sam for "three"? The answer lies in one of the most fascinating features of the Korean language: it doesn't have just one number system. It has two. Because of Korea's history and its geographical proximity to China, Korean vocabulary is split into two main categories. The first is Native Korean, the indigenous words that have been spoken on the peninsula for millennia. The second is Sino-Korean, words derived from Classical Chinese that were absorbed into Korean over centuries. This historical split affects almost every part of the language, but it is most obvious when dealing with numbers. To count objects, tell time, and discuss money, you need to master both systems. Fortunately, they follow predictable patterns. Building Your Core Vocabulary Before we dive into the math of Korean numbers, we need to establish a baseline of words to talk about the world around you. In previous chapters, you learned how to read Hangul and how to structure basic sentences. Now, we need things to actually read and structure. Memorizing these will give you the building blocks for the conversational scenarios you'll encounter later in the book. Note: In Korean, the dictionary form of a verb always ends in 다 (da). We will cover how to change these into actual spoken sentences in Chapter 5. For now, focus on memorizing the base form. Essential Nouns Nouns in Korean do not have gender (like in Spanish or French) and do not change form based on singular or plural. 5. Basic Verb Conjugation and Tenses

The Magic of Shape-Shifting Verbs Imagine you are sitting in a bustling café in Seoul. The barista asks what you would like to order. You want to say, "I drink coffee." A few minutes later, your friend asks what you did yesterday. You want to say, "I drank coffee." Later, your friend asks what you plan to do tomorrow. You want to say, "I will drink coffee." In English, to express these three different moments in time, you have to completely change the verb: drink becomes drank, and then you have to add an entirely new word, will, to make will drink. English verbs are notoriously irregular and require memorizing dozens of forms. Korean, on the other hand, is beautifully logical. Instead of memorizing a whole new word for the past or future, you simply take the verb’s core and attach a different "ending" to it—much like snapping different lenses onto a camera. Change the lens, and you change the time the action happens. In Chapter 3, we learned that Korean sentences often end with verbs, and that we default to the polite informal style (often ending in -yo) to show respect in everyday conversations. In this chapter, we will take the essential vocabulary you learned in Chapter 4 and bring those words to life by learning how to conjugate them into the past, present, and future tenses. The Dictionary Form and Finding the Stem Whenever you look up a Korean verb in a dictionary or vocabulary list, it will always appear in its dictionary form. This is the raw, unconjugated version of the word. In Korean, the dictionary form of every single verb ends in 다 (da). 가다 (gada) – to go 먹다 (meokda) – to eat 공부하다 (gongbuhada) – to study To start conjugating, you have to find the verb's stem. The stem is the heart of the verb; it holds the core meaning. Finding the stem is incredibly easy: you just drop the 다 (da) from the end of the dictionary form. 가다 (gada) ➔ Stem: 가 (ga) 먹다 (meokda) ➔ Stem: 먹 (meok) 공부하다 (gongbuhada) ➔ Stem: 공부하 (gongbuha) Now that you have isolated the stem, you are ready to attach new endings to indicate when the action happens. 6. Subject, Topic, and Object Particles

The Puzzle Piece Principle Imagine you are looking at a desk scattered with loose puzzle pieces. You pick up a piece painted bright red and shaped perfectly to lock into the center of the puzzle. In English, we usually rely on strict word order to tell us where that piece goes. If we say "The dog bites the man," the dog is doing the biting. If we flip it to "The man bites the dog," the man is suddenly the one doing the biting. Korean operates on a completely different principle. Instead of relying on where a word sits in the sentence, Korean attaches tiny, two-letter puzzle pieces directly to the ends of nouns. These puzzle pieces are called particles, and they tell the listener exactly what role that noun is playing in the sentence—regardless of where it is placed. Because Korean sentence structure follows a Subject-Object-Verb order (as we established in Chapter 3), the verb always comes at the very end. This means the listener doesn't know what is actually happening in the sentence until the final word. Particles are the road signs that keep the listener from getting lost before they reach that final verb. In this chapter, we will unlock the three most essential road signs in the Korean language: the subject particles, the topic particles, and the object particles. What Are Particles? Before we dive into the specific types, let's define our terms. A particle is a short grammatical word or suffix that attaches to a noun to show its grammatical function. In Korean, particles are always attached directly to the end of a noun without any spaces. Think of a particle as a name tag you stick onto a noun. If you stick the "subject" name tag on a noun, it means that noun is doing the action. If you stick the "object" name tag on a noun, it means the action is happening to it. To use particles correctly, you only need to remember one simple rule from Chapter 1 (The Korean Alphabet): Does the noun end in a final consonant (a batchim) or a vowel? Korean particles come in pairs. One version is used when the noun ends in a consonant, and the other is used when the noun ends in a vowel. This pairing exists purely to make the language flow smoothly when spoken, a concept tied directly to the pronunciation rules we covered in Chapter 2. 7. Forming Questions and Negative Statements

The Power of a Rising Voice You are sitting in a bustling café in Seoul. The waiter approaches your table, gestures at your empty coffee cup, and says: "커피 마셔요?" (Keopi masyeoyo?) Are they telling you to drink coffee, or are they asking if you want to drink coffee? If you remember our chapter on Sentence Structure and Politeness Levels, you know that Korean verbs change their endings depending on who you are speaking to. The standard polite ending—the haeyo-che—ends in -아요 (-ayo), -어요 (-eoyo), or -해요 (-haeyo). Here is the secret to the waiter’s sentence: the exact same words can be a statement or a question. "커피 마셔요" with a flat tone means "I drink coffee" or "He drinks coffee." But "커피 마셔요?" with a rising intonation at the end means "Do you drink coffee?" or "Will you drink coffee?" In Korean, you do not need to scramble the word order or add auxiliary verbs like "do," "does," or "did" to form a question. You simply use the exact same sentence structure you learned in previous chapters, change your tone of voice, and add a question mark to your writing. Raising Intonation to Form Informal Questions Let's briefly recall how Korean sentences are built. As we covered in the chapter on sentence structure, Korean is an SOV language: Subject-Object-Verb. The verb always sits at the very end of the sentence. Because the verb is the last thing out of your mouth, it is the natural place to shift your tone. Just like in English, where your voice goes up at the end of "You're going home?", Korean relies on a rising intonation to signal that a sentence is a question rather than a statement. Let’s look at some examples using the standard polite form (haeyo-che): Statement: 학교에 가요. (Hakgyo-e gayo.) = I go to school. / I am going to school. Question: 학교에 가요? (Hakgyo-e gayo?) = Do you go to school? / Are you going to school? Statement: 김치를 먹어요. (Gimchi-reul meogeoyo.) = I eat kimchi. / I am eating kimchi. Question: 김치를 먹어요? (Gimchi-reul meogeoyo?) = Do you eat kimchi? / Are you eating kimchi? Notice that the words and the particles (like the location particle -에 and the object particle -를) did not change at all. The only difference is the punctuation in writing and the upward inflection in speech. 8. Practical Conversations and Daily Scenarios

Meeting and Greeting: Politeness in Action You step off the train in Seoul, navigate the bustling corridors, and finally spot your friend's Korean colleague waiting near the exit. You know the alphabet, you understand how sentences are built, and you’ve memorized the vocabulary. Now, it is time to string those elements together in real time. In Korean, the most critical step of any interaction is setting the correct politeness level immediately. As covered in Sentence Structure and Politeness Levels, Korean uses specific verb endings to show respect. For beginners, the polite/informal level (해요체, haeyo-che) is your best friend. It is polite enough to use with strangers and acquaintances, but not overly stiff. Hello and Goodbye The standard greeting in Korean changes depending on the time of day. Good morning: 안녕히 주무셨어요? (Annyeonghi jumusyeosseoyo?) – Literally: Did you sleep peacefully? (Used mostly in the morning). Hello (standard): 안녕하세요? (Annyeonghaseyo?) – Used any time of day. Saying goodbye, however, depends on two things: who is leaving, and who is staying. If you are leaving and the other person is staying: 안녕히 계세요. (Annyeonghi gyeseyo.) – Literally: Stay peacefully. If the other person is leaving and you are staying: 안녕히 가세요. (Annyeonghi gaseyo.) – Literally: Go peacefully. If you are leaving a workplace, you generally use a specific phrase to say goodbye to those staying behind: 수고하세요. (Sugohaseyo.) – Literally: Work hard / Keep up the good work. The Apology and Thank You Pair Two phrases you will use constantly are "thank you" and "I'm sorry." Thank you: 감사합니다 (Gamsahamnida) or 고맙습니다 (Gomapseumnida). Both are highly polite and interchangeable. I am sorry / Excuse me: 죄송합니다 (Joesonghamnida) for formal situations, or 미안합니다 (Mianhamnida) for slightly less formal situations. If you are trying to get a waiter's attention in a restaurant or pushing through a crowded subway car, you can also use 실례합니다 (Sillyehamnida), which translates to "Excuse me." Introducing Yourself When meeting someone for the first time, a standard self-introduction follows a predictable script. You will rely heavily on the topic particle -은/는 (-eun/neun) and the verb -입니다 (-imnida), which means "to be." The Basic Formula 1. Greeting: 안녕하세요. (Annyeonghaseyo.) 2. Name: [Name]입니다. ([Name]-imnida.) Or, [Name]이라고 합니다 ([Name]-irago hamnida) which means "I am called [Name]." 3. Nationality: [Country] 사람입니다. ([Country] saram-imnida.) Literally: I am a [Country] person. 4. Occupation: [Occupation]입니다. ([Occupation]-imnida.) 5. Pleasantry: 만나서 반갑습니다. (Mannaseo bangapseumnida.) Literally: Because we meet, I am glad.
