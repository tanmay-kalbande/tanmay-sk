1. CPA Exam Structure, Eligibility, and the 18-Month Window

The New CPA Exam Landscape: Core and Discipline Imagine a candidate who scored a perfect 99 on FAR, AUD, and REG, only to lose all three passing scores because life got in the way before they could sit for the fourth section. This scenario happens more often than the AICPA would like to admit. Passing the CPA Exam on your first try isn’t just about mastering accounting principles; it requires a strategic understanding of the exam’s architecture, the bureaucracy of applying, and the unforgiving ticking clock of the passing window. The CPA Exam underwent a massive transformation with the CPA Evolution initiative, shifting from a strictly uniform four-part exam to a Core + Discipline model. Every candidate must pass three core sections that test universal accounting knowledge, followed by one discipline section that allows for specialization. The Four Sections at a Glance 1. Core 1: Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR) Tests financial reporting standards, state and local government accounting, and the preparation of financial statements. 2. Core 2: Auditing and Attestation (AUD) Covers the entire audit process, ethics, professional responsibilities, and the evaluation of internal controls. 3. Core 3: Taxation and Regulation (REG) Focuses on federal taxation, business law, and professional ethics. 4. Discipline Sections (Choose One) - Business Analysis and Reporting (BAR): Deepens FAR concepts, focusing on financial statement analysis, forecasting, and data analytics. - Information Systems and Controls (ISC): Deepens AUD concepts, focusing on IT governance, system design, and data management controls. - Tax Compliance and Planning (TCP): Deepens REG concepts, focusing on advanced tax compliance and entity tax planning. Format and Weighting Every section of the CPA Exam consists of two testlets of Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs) followed by Task-Based Simulations (SIMs). The BEC section (now replaced by the three disciplines under CPA Evolution) historically contained Written Communication tasks, but the current exam relies heavily on SIMs to test higher-order cognitive skills. Here is the standard format for all four sections: MCQs: 78 questions (split across two testlets). MCQs account for 50% of your total score. SIMs: 7 Task-Based Simulations. SIMs account for the remaining 50% of your score. Time Allowed: 4 hours per section. Note: The exact number of MCQs and SIMs is subject to minor adjustments by the AICPA, but the 50/50 scoring split between MCQs and SIMs remains the foundational structure. The CPA license is granted at the state level, meaning there is no single universal set of eligibility requirements. You must apply through a specific State Board of Accountancy. 2. Strategic Study Planning and Material Selection

Consider two CPA candidates, Sarah and David. Sarah, a senior accountant working 45 hours a week, purchases the most expensive review course on the market, driven by the assumption that a higher price tag guarantees a first-try pass. She carries a 400-page textbook on her commute, reads during lunch, and studies haphazardly whenever she finds a spare moment. David, working the same job with the same time constraints, spends half as much on a course tailored to his audio-visual learning style. He maps out a week-by-week calendar with specific daily time blocks, builds in buffer days, and schedules his first section based on his recent academic strengths. Six months later, David is celebrating passing his fourth section; Sarah is retaking her second, her expensive textbooks gathering dust. The difference between these two candidates wasn’t intellectual capacity or accounting knowledge. It was strategy. Passing the CPA exam on the first try requires treating your study process like a high-stakes project management endeavor. You need the right tools and a ruthlessly realistic execution plan. Selecting Your CPA Review Course Your CPA review course is your primary weapon. Because the exam is criterion-referenced—meaning you are judged against a fixed standard of knowledge rather than the performance of your peers—the quality and alignment of your study materials dictate your ceiling. There is no single "best" review course. There is only the best course for you. Intermediate candidates should evaluate providers across four primary dimensions: learning style, technology, budget, and structural alignment. Evaluating Learning Style and Course Delivery Most modern CPA review courses offer a blend of lectures, textbooks, Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs), and Task-Based Simulations (SIMs). However, the emphasis and delivery methods vary wildly. Visual and Auditory Learners: Look for courses with high-production-value video lectures, digital whiteboards, and animated graphics. Some candidates need an engaging, dynamic instructor to keep them focused through dense topics like lease accounting or pass-through taxation. Reading/Writing Learners: If you retain information best by reading and annotating, prioritize courses known for comprehensive, well-structured physical textbooks and detailed written explanations for incorrect answers. Tactile/Application Learners: If you learn by doing, seek out courses with robust digital platforms, unlimited practice drills, and highly detailed SIMs that mimic the actual exam interface. Technology and Adaptive Learning The best courses do not rely on static study plans. They use adaptive learning technology that tracks your performance in real-time. When you answer an MCQ incorrectly, the algorithm should automatically feed you more questions on that specific subtopic until your proficiency improves. Look for platforms that offer granular analytics—you need to know not just that you are weak in "REG," but that you are specifically weak in "Partnership Tax Basis Calculations." 3. Evidence-Based Study Techniques for CPA Candidates

The Illusion of Competence in CPA Preparation Consider a scenario familiar to almost every CPA candidate: You spend three hours reading through a textbook chapter on lease accounting. The explanations make sense. The journal entries seem logical as you read them. You nod along, feeling a steady build-up of confidence. You close the book, satisfied with a productive study session. Two days later, you sit down to tackle the corresponding Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs). You stare at a prompt asking for the initial measurement of a right-of-use asset for an operating lease under ASC 842. Your mind goes blank. The clarity you felt while reading has completely evaporated. This phenomenon is known as the illusion of competence. Reading and understanding are passive activities; they create recognition, not recall. The CPA exam does not test your ability to recognize a correct journal entry when you see it—it tests your ability to generate it from scratch under immense time pressure. To pass the exam on the first try, you must transition from passive consumption to evidence-based, active learning. Active Recall and Spaced Repetition: The Engines of Retention Two of the most rigorously validated concepts in cognitive science are active recall (retrieving information from memory) and spaced repetition (reviewing material at increasing intervals). When combined, they form the foundation of long-term memory retention, which is critical for a multi-part exam where knowledge must persist for 18 months. Applying Active Recall to Accounting Content For CPA candidates, active recall means closing the textbook and forcing your brain to retrieve the rules, formulas, and exceptions before attempting a practice problem. - The "Brain Dump" Before Practice: Before starting a block of MCQs on a specific topic (e.g., Basis of Property Contributions under IRC Sec. 351), take a blank sheet of paper and write down everything you remember about the rules. Include the required ownership percentage (80% control) and the tax basis formulas. Only after you have exhausted your recall should you open the book to check your accuracy. - Reverse-Engineering MCQs: Instead of just finding the right answer, explain why the other three options are incorrect. The incorrect options (distractors) are often based on common candidate mistakes. Understanding why a distractor is wrong builds a mental web of exceptions and rules, which is especially vital for AUD and REG. - The Feynman Technique for Complex Topics: When struggling with a dense topic like Consolidated Financial Statements or Deferred Tax Assets, attempt to explain it out loud as if teaching it to someone with no accounting background. 4. Conquering FAR: Financial Accounting and Reporting

The FAR Mountain: Scope and Strategy Of the three Core sections under the CPA Evolution model, Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR) is universally regarded as the most voluminous. The AICPA blueprint for FAR covers everything from basic accruals to complex hedge accounting, governmental funds, and business combinations. Passing FAR on your first attempt requires a strategic shift: you cannot treat every topic equally. As established in Strategic Study Planning and Material Selection, your study schedule must be dynamic. FAR rewards candidates who ruthlessly allocate time based on heavily tested areas. Instead of reading the textbook cover-to-cover, use Evidence-Based Study Techniques for CPA Candidates to engage actively with the material—specifically by practicing journal entry logic and working through Task-Based Simulations (SIMs) early and often. Revenue Recognition: The Five-Step Model ASC 606, Revenue from Contracts with Customers, is a cornerstone of the FAR exam. Because revenue recognition is foundational to financial statements, the examiners test it exhaustively. You must know the five-step model cold and be able to apply it to complex scenarios. 1. Identify the contract(s) with a customer. 2. Identify the performance obligations in the contract. 3. Determine the transaction price. 4. Allocate the transaction price to the performance obligations. 5. Recognize revenue when (or as) the entity satisfies a performance obligation. For intermediate learners, the challenge is rarely steps one or two; it is steps three, four, and five. The exam loves to test whether revenue is recognized at a point in time or over time. Over time recognition occurs if one of three criteria is met: the customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits; the entity creates or enhances an asset the customer controls; or the entity has no alternative use for the asset and has an enforceable right to payment for performance to date. Scenario: Software and Implementation Bundle Imagine a tech company sells a software license for $50,000 and a two-year implementation service for $30,000. The software can be used by the customer immediately on its own, without the implementation service. Here is the journal entry logic: Standalone Selling Prices: Software ($50,000), Implementation ($30,000). Total contract = $80,000. Performance Obligations: Two (the software and the implementation). Recognition: The software license is recognized at a point in time (upon delivery/access), assuming it functions independently. The implementation service is recognized over time as the service is performed. If the exam SIM asks you to calculate revenue after year one where the implementation is 50% complete, the recognized revenue is $50,000 (software) + $15,000 (50% of $30,000) = $65,000. Deferred Revenue (Liability): $15,000. 5. Conquering AUD: Auditing and Attestation

The Auditor's Mindset: Professional Skepticism and Judgment An auditor sits across from a CFO who is visibly stressed. The company needs a clean opinion to secure critical financing, and the CFO has a perfectly reasonable explanation for why inventory turnover dropped sharply in the final month of the fiscal year. The explanation makes sense on the surface. But the auditor notices a slight hesitation, a fraction of a second delay before the CFO answers the follow-up question about the physical count procedures. Passing the AUD section of the CPA exam requires you to adopt the persona of that auditor. You cannot approach exam questions as a rule-memorizing accounting student; you must approach them as a skeptical, independent professional evaluating the work of others. Where FAR asked you to calculate and report, AUD asks you to evaluate, assess risk, and judge the sufficiency and appropriateness of evidence. The foundation of this mindset is professional skepticism. This is not cynicism or an assumption that management is lying. It is an attitude that includes a questioning mind, a critical assessment of evidence, and a heightened alertness to conditions that may indicate possible misstatement. On the exam, you will frequently encounter answer choices that assume management is entirely honest or that the auditor can rely on management's verbal assertions without corroboration. These are always incorrect. Closely tied to skepticism is professional judgment. The AICPA defines this as the application of relevant training, knowledge, and experience to make informed decisions. AUD SIMs often place you in a scenario where you must evaluate documentation, identify missing steps, or determine the appropriate modification to an audit report. There is rarely a pure math calculation to fall back on; you must rely on the professional judgment framework to select the most appropriate course of action. The AICPA Code of Professional Conduct in Practice Ethics is not just a memorization exercise on the AUD exam; it is the framework that dictates every action an auditor takes. The AICPA Code of Professional Conduct establishes the rules for independence, integrity, and objectivity. To pass AUD on the first try, you must internalize how these rules apply to specific, nuanced scenarios. 6. Conquering REG: Taxation, Business Law, and Ethics

A CPA candidate sits for the REG section and encounters a simulation involving a partner who contributes appreciated property to a partnership in exchange for a 40% interest. The candidate freezes. They know the property was appreciated, and they know partnerships are pass-through entities, but they cannot remember if the contributing partner recognizes gain immediately or if the gain is deferred. They guess, cascading that error into the basis calculations for both the partner and the partnership. This scenario is the number one reason candidates fail REG. Unlike FAR, which tests your ability to apply a cohesive set of financial reporting standards, or AUD, which tests your logical understanding of audit risk, REG tests your ability to memorize and apply highly specific statutory rules, exceptions, and exceptions to those exceptions. To pass REG on the first try, you cannot rely on general business intuition. You must build a mental framework that categorizes federal taxation, business law, and ethics into distinct, rule-based silos. Mastering Individual and Entity Federal Taxation Federal taxation forms the bulk of the REG exam. The key to mastering this area is understanding the hierarchy of tax calculations: you must first determine what constitutes gross income, then apply the appropriate deductions (above or below the line), calculate the correct tax based on the entity type, and finally apply credits. Income Calculations and Entity Classifications The exam will frequently test your ability to distinguish between different entity types and how they are taxed. Sole Proprietorships and Single-Member LLCs: Treated as disregarded entities. Income and expenses are reported on Schedule C, flowing to the individual’s Form 1040. Partnerships and Multi-Member LLCs: Pass-through entities. The entity files an informational return (Form 1065), but the tax is paid by the partners via Schedule K-1. S Corporations: Also pass-through entities, but with strict ownership eligibility rules (max 100 shareholders, U.S. residents only, one class of stock). C Corporations: Separate taxable entities. The exam tests the concept of double taxation—once at the corporate level (Form 1120) and again at the shareholder level when dividends are distributed. When calculating income, remember that gross income is broadly defined as "all income from whatever source derived." The exam will test your knowledge of specific exclusions (e.g., municipal bond interest, life insurance proceeds, certain gifts and inheritances). Deductions: Above the Line vs. Below the Line A critical distinction in individual taxation is the classification of deductions. Above-the-line deductions (e.g., alimony paid for pre-2019 divorces, educator expenses, HSA contributions, half of self-employment tax) are subtracted from gross income to arrive at Adjusted Gross Income (AGI). 7. Conquering BEC: Business Environment and Concepts

The BEC Landscape: What Makes This Section Different A mid-sized manufacturing firm needs to decide whether to invest $2 million in automated assembly equipment. The CFO needs to know if the investment will pay off, how to finance it, what macroeconomic headwinds might arise, and how to document the internal controls surrounding the new system's financial reporting. This single scenario captures the essence of Business Environment and Concepts (BEC). Unlike FAR, which demands deep technical accounting mechanics, or AUD, which focuses heavily on attestation standards, BEC is the CPA exam’s "big picture" section. It tests your ability to view the business as an integrated whole—understanding how economic forces, financial management, IT infrastructure, and corporate governance intersect. Assuming you are using the evidence-based study techniques outlined earlier in this book, your approach to BEC should prioritize high-level application over rote memorization. BEC evaluates whether you can think like a business advisor. Notably, under the Core + Discipline model, BEC (Core 1) is the only exam section that includes a Written Communication task, making your ability to articulate business concepts just as important as your ability to calculate them. Corporate Governance, Internal Controls, and Enterprise Risk Management Because BEC takes a holistic view of business, it begins with the structures that keep a company running ethically and efficiently. You should already be familiar with the mechanics of internal controls from your studies in AUD; BEC approaches these concepts from the perspective of management design rather than auditor testing. Corporate Governance Structures Corporate governance is the system of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled. The exam focuses heavily on the roles of various participants in mitigating risk and ensuring shareholder value. Key players include: Board of Directors (BOD): Elected by shareholders to oversee management. The BOD relies on sub-committees to execute specific oversight functions. Audit Committee: A subset of the BOD responsible for overseeing financial reporting, internal controls, and the external audit. Must be composed of independent directors. Compensation Committee: Determines executive compensation, aiming to align management incentives with shareholder interests. Management: Responsible for designing, implementing, and maintaining internal controls and executing strategy. A frequent MCQ scenario involves identifying which committee handles a specific issue. For example, if a whistleblower reports a financial reporting fraud, the report should go to the Audit Committee, not the Compensation Committee or the CEO. Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) ERM is a comprehensive framework for managing risks across the entire organization. The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO) provides the primary ERM framework tested on the CPA exam. 8. Practice Exams, Adaptive Learning, and Weakness Targeting

Consider two CPA candidates preparing for FAR. Both score 72% on their first full-length practice exam. Candidate A panics, opens their textbook to page one, and spends three days re-reading government accounting and lease amendments. Candidate B spends exactly two hours dissecting their performance analytics, identifies that 65% of their missed points came specifically from pension plan roll-forwards and non-monetary exchanges, and spends those same three days hammering those isolated concepts with adaptive MCQ sets. On exam day, Candidate A fails with a 71. Candidate B passes with an 82. The difference between these candidates isn't raw intelligence or study hours—it is the strategic application of practice exams and performance data. By this point in your study journey, you have selected your materials, mastered evidence-based study techniques, and reviewed the core concepts of your specific exam sections. Now, as you enter the final stretch, the goal shifts from acquiring knowledge to proving it under testing conditions. The Progressive Practice Exam Strategy Taking a full-length practice exam cold is a waste of a valuable diagnostic tool. Just as you wouldn't run a marathon the week you start training, you shouldn't attempt a full, simulated CPA exam before you have built the necessary cognitive and physical endurance. A progressive practice exam strategy scales up in difficulty and length, conditioning you for the real event. Phase 1: Section-Specific Sprints (Weeks 1-4) Early in your study cycle for a specific section, your practice should be confined to section-specific sprints. These are 20- to 30-question sets focused strictly on the module you just finished studying. At this stage, you are assessing immediate recall and basic application. Because you are still building your foundational knowledge, your software's adaptive learning features should be turned off here; you want random or sequential questions to ensure you see every concept, not just the ones the algorithm thinks you need. Phase 2: Mixed Review and Mid-Length Simulations (Weeks 5-6) Once you have covered multiple modules, begin mixing question sets. This forces your brain to context-switch—jumping from a business law question to an individual tax question without warning, exactly as the real exam does. During this phase, introduce mid-length simulations. A mid-length simulation might consist of 30 MCQs and 2 Task-Based Simulations (SIMs) completed in a single sitting. This builds your initial focus endurance without completely draining you. Phase 3: The Full-Length Simulation (Weeks 7-8) In the final weeks leading up to your exam date, you must complete at least two full-length, heavily simulated practice exams. This is where you build true exam endurance. 9. Test-Taking Strategies: MCQs, SIMs, and Written Communication

The Mechanics of Scoring: Why Strategy Matters as Much as Knowledge You have spent months employing the Evidence-Based Study Techniques for CPA Candidates, mastering complex journal entries, tax rules, and audit procedures. You know the material. But knowing the material and passing the CPA exam are two distinct challenges. The exam is not just a test of accounting knowledge; it is a test of your ability to navigate a highly structured, time-constrained testing environment. A candidate who knows 80% of the material but employs poor test-taking strategy will often score lower than a candidate who knows 70% but excels at process of elimination, time allocation, and keyword targeting. The exam’s format—Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs), Task-Based Simulations (SIMs), and Written Communication tasks—requires specific, tactical approaches to maximize points. Mastering Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs) MCQs make up 50% of your score in the Core sections and the BAR and ISC disciplines. As outlined in the earlier sections on conquering FAR, AUD, REG, and BEC, the MCQ testlets adapt to your performance. If you do well on the first testlet, the second testlet becomes harder. Harder testlets are worth more points. To pass on the first try, you must capitalize on the "easy" points and aggressively farm partial credit in the difficult testlets. Strategic Time Management for MCQs You generally have just over a minute per MCQ. However, treating every question as equal is a fatal error. 1. The First Pass (Target: 30-40 seconds per question): Rapidly move through the testlet answering questions you immediately know. If you can solve it in your head or with a quick scratchpad calculation, do it. 2. The Flag and Bypass: If a question requires a lengthy calculation or you draw a blank, flag it and move on. Do not spend three minutes on a single MCQ when you could answer three others in that same timeframe. 3. The Second Pass (Target: 1.5 - 2 minutes per flagged question): Return to your flagged questions with the context of the rest of the testlet. Often, a concept tested in a later, easier question will trigger your memory for a harder, flagged question. Elimination Strategies and Educated Guessing The CPA exam is designed to punish half-reading. Distractors (incorrect answer choices) are meticulously crafted to trap candidates who miss a single word, such as "except," "not," or "always." Identify the "Throwaway" Option: In almost every difficult CPA exam MCQ, one answer choice is completely out of left field. It may be factually true in a different context but is irrelevant to the specific scenario. Cross it out mentally or on your scratchpad immediately. You have just improved your odds from 25% to 33%. 10. Exam Day Preparation and Mental Performance

The Final 48 Hours: Logistics and Physical Readiness You have spent hundreds of hours applying evidence-based study techniques, dissecting the nuances of FAR and AUD, and grinding through adaptive practice exams. But all of that academic preparation can be derailed by a single poor night of sleep, a missed exit on the highway, or an expired identification card. The final 48 hours before your CPA exam shift from intellectual preparation to physical and logistical readiness. NTS Verification and Prometric Logistics Your Notice to Schedule (NTS) is your golden ticket. Without it, you cannot sit for the exam. Forty-eight hours before your test, log into your NASBA account (or your state board’s portal) and verify that your NTS is valid and that the expiration date has not passed. Cross-reference the exam section ID on your NTS with your Prometric confirmation email. Ensure the date, time, and address match exactly. If you are testing at an unfamiliar Prometric center, drive there during a similar traffic time the day before. Note where to park, which building entrance to use, and how long the walk from the parking lot to the testing center takes. You want to arrive on test day with zero ambient stress about your surroundings. The Prometric Check-In Procedure Prometric check-in is highly standardized. Knowing the drill prevents pre-exam anxiety. Arrive 15 to 30 minutes early. If you arrive more than 30 minutes late, Prometric will deny you entry, and you will forfeit your exam fees. When you arrive, you will: 1. Present your NTS and one valid, unexpired form of government-issued identification. The name on your ID must exactly match the name on your NTS. 2. Have your photograph taken. 3. Have your palm scanned biometrically. 4. Empty your pockets and place all personal items in a provided locker. Managing Allowed and Prohibited Items Prometric strictly enforces what you can and cannot bring into the testing room. Prohibited items (must go in your locker): - Cell phones (ensure they are completely powered off, not just on silent) - Smartwatches and fitness trackers - Wallets, keys, and bags - Food and drink - Jackets with hoods or large pockets Allowed items: - Eyeglasses (these will be inspected by the test center administrator) - Earplugs (Prometric usually provides these, but you can bring your own foam earplugs if they are out of the wrapper) - A sweater or light jacket without pockets You will be given two sheets of laminated paper (or a small whiteboard) and two dry-erase markers. 11. Between Exams: Maintaining Momentum and Passing All Four

The Post-Exam Void: Decompression and Immediate Forward Planning You click "Submit," walk out of the testing center, and step into the sunlight. For the first time in weeks, you don't have a set of Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs) waiting for you at home. The immediate relief is overwhelming, but for many CPA candidates, it is rapidly followed by a strange, lingering anxiety. Did you do enough? Should you have spent more time on that final Task-Based Simulation (SIM)? The period between taking one exam and receiving your score—often a grueling wait of several weeks—is a psychological minefield. The biggest mistake candidates make during this window is falling into an all-or-nothing trap: either burning themselves out by studying for the next section the very next day, or completely shutting down and losing all academic momentum while waiting for the score release. To pass all four sections on the first try, you must treat the post-exam window with the same strategic rigor you applied to your study schedule. The 48-Hour Decompression Protocol Your brain and body have just endured a massive stress event. If you immediately pivot into heavy study sessions for a new section, you are highly likely to experience diminishing returns and early burnout. Implement a strict 48-Hour Decompression Protocol. During the two days following your exam: - Mandate a complete disconnect. Do not open your CPA review course. Do not read accounting forums. Do not try to look up the answers to the questions you remember. - Engage in physical recovery. Sleep in. Go for a walk. Exercise. Physical movement helps flush the stress hormones that have accumulated during your study and testing period. - Reclaim personal time. Spend time with family or friends, and catch up on the chores you neglected during your final push. Once the 48 hours have passed, it is time to pivot. Do not wait for your score release to begin studying for your next section. Waiting wastes precious weeks out of your 18-month window. Planning the Next Section By the time you sit for an exam, you should already know what you are taking next. If you are following the Core + Discipline model, you might be transitioning from a Core section to a Discipline section, or vice versa. 1. Evaluate your timeline. Look at your overall strategic study plan. How many weeks do you have until your target date for the next section? 2. Acknowledge the context shift. If you just took Core 2: Auditing and Attestation (AUD) and are moving to Core 3: Taxation and Regulation (REG), the mental shift from a judgment-based framework to a rule-based framework takes a few days.
