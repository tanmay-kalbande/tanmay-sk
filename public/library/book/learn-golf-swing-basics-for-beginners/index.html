What do you need to know before you step into the swing zone? 2. 2. Fundamentals of the Golf Swing

The Three Phases of a Complete Swing Imagine you are standing on the tee‑box with a fresh driver in hand, the fairway stretching out in front of you. You take a breath, step back, and—without thinking about the details—swing the club. The ball rockets off the tee, but it veers wildly to the left and lands in the rough. What happened? Most beginners discover that the problem isn’t the club or the ball; it’s the way the swing is put together. A golf swing is not a single motion, but a sequence of three connected phases: 1. Setup – the position you assume before the first movement. 2. Swing – everything that happens from the first take‑away to the moment the clubhead strikes the ball. 3. Finish – the follow‑through that ends the motion and reveals whether the swing was balanced. Each phase has a clear purpose, and mastering them together creates the power and accuracy needed to hit the ball where you intend. --- 1. Setup – Laying the Foundation The setup is the “ready” position. It is where you translate the goals of the shot (distance, direction, trajectory) into body alignment and club placement. 3. 3. Grip and Hand Position

A Grip That Sets the Tone Imagine you’re on the first tee of a crisp‑morning round. You step up, take a smooth swing, and watch the ball launch high—only to see it curve dramatically to the right, disappearing into the rough. You’ve just hit a classic “slice.” While many factors contribute to a slice, one of the most common culprits is an incorrect grip. The way you hold the club is the first physical decision your body makes in every swing, and it directly determines how the clubface will meet the ball. Mastering the grip is therefore the foundation for the swing basics you explored in Fundamentals of the Golf Swing. In the sections that follow, we’ll break down every aspect of the grip: the three classic grip styles, the subtle art of grip pressure, the exact placement of the left hand for right‑handed players, and how to spot and fix a faulty grip. By the end of this chapter you will be able to demonstrate each grip style, feel the difference between “too tight” and “just right,” and adjust your hands on the club with confidence. --- Understanding the Grip The grip is simply the way your hands wrap around the club’s shaft. Though it sounds elementary, the grip is a powerful lever that controls the clubface angle at impact, influences the swing path, and even affects the feel of the club throughout the swing. A consistent, well‑executed grip gives you a reliable reference point, allowing the body and arms to work together without the mind constantly guessing how the club is positioned. Why Grip Matters - Clubface Control: The orientation of your hands dictates the angle of the clubface at impact. A “strong” grip (hands turned more to the right for a right‑handed player) tends to close the clubface, while a “weak” grip opens it. - Swing Path Consistency: A stable grip reduces unwanted wrist hinging, helping you maintain the swing plane introduced in Chapter 2. - Feel & Feedback: A proper grip lets you sense the clubhead through the shaft, providing immediate feedback on miss‑hits and timing. --- The Three Common Grip Styles Most golfers adopt one of three grip styles: interlocking, overlapping (Vardon), or ten‑finger (baseball). 4. 4. Stance and Alignment

Why a Good Stance Is the Foundation of Every Good Shot Imagine you are standing on the tee‑box with a brand‑new driver, ready to launch your first ball of the round. You swing, make solid contact, but the ball drifts far to the right, missing the fairway entirely. The most common culprit? An unstable, mis‑aligned stance. Your stance is the only part of the swing you control from the moment you step up to the ball until you finish the swing. It determines balance, direction, and how efficiently you can turn your body. Mastering stance and alignment early prevents the habit‑forming errors that can haunt a beginner for years. --- Measuring the Right Shoulder Width for Each Club The distance between your shoulders—often called the stance width—should change with the length of the club you are using. A wider stance gives you a more stable base for longer clubs, while a narrower stance allows for better control with shorter clubs. | Club Type | Typical Club Length (in) | Recommended Shoulder Width | |-----------|--------------------------|----------------------------| | Driver / Fairway Wood | 43–45 | Feet shoulder‑width apart (≈ 21–24 in) | | Long Iron (3‑5) | 38–40 | Slightly wider than shoulder‑width (≈ 24–26 in) | | Mid‑Iron (6‑8) | 35–37 | Shoulder‑width (≈ 21–24 in) | | Short Iron / Wedge | 33–35 | Narrower than shoulder‑width (≈ 18–21 in) | How to measure it: 1. Place the club you intend to use across your shoulders, letting the grip rest on each shoulder. 2. Feel where the club’s shaft touches the side of your torso. 3. Adjust your feet until the distance between the outer edges of your shoes matches that width. Quick Check: “Club‑to‑Shoulder” Test 1. Hold the club horizontally across your back, gripping it as you would in a normal swing. 2. Without moving the club, step forward with your lead foot (left foot for right‑handed players) until the club rests comfortably on the inside of that foot’s heel. 3. Your other foot should naturally fall into the correct width. If the club feels too high on your shoulders, you are standing too narrow; if it presses against the neck, you are too wide. --- Aligning Your Body to the Target Once the stance width is set, the next step is to line up the three major “planes” of your body—feet, hips, and shoulders—parallel to the target line. 5. 5. Posture and Balance

Why Good Posture Is the Secret Weapon of Every Swing Imagine you are on the fairway, a crisp morning breeze ruffling the grass. You take your stance, grip the club, and swing. The ball rockets forward, but it lands short, veering off to the right. You feel a subtle “tipping” sensation during the swing, as if you were leaning too far forward or backward. Most beginners discover that the problem isn’t the club or the grip—it’s the way they hold their spine and balance their weight. A stable, repeatable swing starts with a solid posture. When the body is aligned correctly, the arms, shoulders, and hips can move freely, and the clubhead can deliver its maximum speed at impact. The following sections break down the three pillars of a reliable posture: spine tilt, weight distribution, and knee‑flex/hip‑hinge. By mastering each, you’ll create a firm platform that lets the swing “do the work” instead of the body fighting against it. --- 1. The Ideal Spine Tilt 1.1 What “Spine Tilt” Means Spine tilt (sometimes called spine angle) is the angle formed between the torso and the ground when you assume your address position. Think of it as the slope of a roof: a gentle forward lean that keeps your shoulders over the ball without collapsing into a hunch. 1.2 How Much Should You Tilt? For most amateur players, a tilt of about 20–25 degrees from vertical is a good starting point. This angle: Keeps the center of gravity (CG) slightly forward of the ball, helping the club approach the ball on a shallow, upward path. Allows the shoulders to rotate freely during the backswing and downswing. Prevents excessive strain on the lower back, which is a common source of injury for beginners. Tip: If you’re unsure of the angle, stand near a wall, place a plumb line (a string with a weight) on the wall, and compare the line to the slope of your spine. Adjust until the line matches the 20‑25° range. 1.3 Why Spine Tilt Affects Swing Mechanics 1. Clubhead Path – A proper tilt positions the clubhead behind the ball at the top of the swing, encouraging an upward strike that maximizes launch angle and distance. 2. Leverage – The forward tilt creates a natural “lever” between the hips and shoulders, allowing the torso to turn while the arms stay relatively stable. 3. Consistency – Repeating the same tilt each time gives the body a familiar reference point, reducing variability in swing length and direction. --- 2. 6. 6. The Backswing

The Three Pillars of the Backswing When you step up to the ball, the first half of the swing is often called the backswing. For a beginner, thinking of the backswing as three distinct “stations” makes it much easier to feel what the body and club should be doing. The three positions are: | Position | Where the club is | What the body is doing | Primary feel | |----------|-------------------|------------------------|--------------| | Takeaway | Club head moves straight back from the ball, staying low to the ground. | The arms and shoulders begin to turn as a unit; the hips stay almost still. | “The club is moving away like a pendulum, staying close to the ground.” | | Halfway | Club shaft is roughly parallel to the ground; the club head is near the end of the swing arc. | Shoulders have turned about 45°, hips have begun a very slight turn; the wrists start to hinge (cock). | “My shoulders are turned, but my hips feel anchored.” | | Top | Club is pointed upward, shaft is vertical or slightly past vertical; the club head is behind the hands. | Shoulders have completed ~90° of rotation, hips have remained relatively stable, wrists are fully cocked. | “I feel a stretch in my shoulders, and my wrists are locked.” | 1. The Takeaway 1. Start the club on a shallow path – Imagine the clubhead sliding along the ground, just a few inches above the turf. This keeps the club on a low‑to‑high arc, which is the most forgiving path for beginners. 2. Move the club with the hands and arms, not the wrists – At this early stage the wrists remain relatively flat; the motion is driven by the forearms and the shoulders turning together. 3. Maintain a neutral spine – Because you already established good posture in Chapter 5, keep that spine angle while the club moves back. A common mistake is “lifting the club” too early, which forces the club onto a steep, vertical path and makes it hard to return to the ball smoothly. 2. The Halfway Point When the club shaft is parallel to the ground, you have reached the halfway point. This is the sweet spot for two critical actions: - Shoulder turn – Your shoulders should have rotated roughly 45° from the target line. Think of drawing a line from your left ear (for a right‑handed player) to your right shoulder; that line should now point toward the target. - Wrist hinge (cocking) begins – The wrists start to bend backward, creating a 30°–45° angle between the club shaft and the forearms. 7. 7. The Downswing and Impact

From Backswing to Ball‑Contact: The Downswing Journey Imagine you’re standing on the fairway, the sun is just beginning to rise, and you have a clear view of the green ahead. You have set your grip, aligned your stance, and completed a smooth backswing. The moment the club reaches the top, a question flickers in your mind: “How do I get that club to meet the ball exactly where I want it, with the right speed and direction?” The answer lies in the downswing – the hidden engine that transforms stored energy into forward motion – and the precise moment of impact when the clubface meets the ball. Mastering this phase is the bridge between a good swing and a great one. --- 1. The Downswing Sequence: Hips → Torso → Arms → Club 1.1 Why a Sequence Matters Think of a whip: the motion starts at the handle, travels through the flexible body, and ends with a snap at the tip. In the golf swing, the hips are the handle, the torso the flexible body, the arms the middle section, and the club the tip. Initiating the downswing with the hips creates a natural “chain reaction” that maximizes speed while keeping the clubface square. 1.2 Step‑by‑Step Breakdown | Phase | What Happens | Feel / Cue | |-------|--------------|------------| | Hip Initiation | The hips rotate toward the target, beginning the weight shift. | “Turn your hips as if you’re starting to swing a baseball bat.” | | Torso Rotation | The shoulders follow the hips, opening up the upper body. | “Your chest should turn slightly after your hips.” | | Arm Drop | The elbows and wrists drop, bringing the club down on the inside of the swing arc. | “Feel the clubhead falling toward the ball, like a pendulum.” | | Club Release | The club accelerates, the hands unhinge (release), and the clubface squares up. | “The club should feel like it’s ‘catching’ the ball at impact.” | Key Point: Each segment must start after the previous one has begun. Rushing the arms before the hips turn leads to “casting,” which reduces power and often produces an open clubface. 1.3 Common Beginner Missteps - Early Arm Pull: Starting the arm swing before the hips rotate creates a “push‑pull” motion that stalls the club. - Hip Over‑rotation: Turning the hips too far can cause the torso to lag, resulting in loss of balance. - Flat Swing Plane: Failing to drop the arms on the correct path leads to a slice or hook. --- 2. 8. 8. Follow‑Through and Finish

Why the Finish Is the Secret Sauce of a Consistent Swing Imagine you’re on the driving range, club in hand, after weeks of practicing your grip, stance, and backswing. You finally feel the clubhead connect solidly with the ball and watch it launch down the fairway. Yet, after a few dozen swings, you notice a pattern: the ball often lands short of the target, or it veers off‑line despite a clean impact. What you’re missing isn’t the “big” parts of the swing you’ve already mastered; it’s the follow‑through—the motion that happens after the ball is struck. A full, balanced finish tells your body that the swing was executed correctly, reinforces the tempo you just generated, and protects you from the stresses that cause injury. In short, the finish is the bridge between a good swing and a great, repeatable swing. Pro tip: Think of the swing as a story. The opening (address), the middle (backswing and downswing), and the ending (follow‑through). A story that ends abruptly feels unsatisfying—so does a swing that stops short of the finish. --- The Three Pillars of a Complete Finish A complete finish can be broken down into three observable components. Mastering each one gives you a clear, physical cue that you have swung through the ball correctly. 1. Hip Rotation What it is: The turning of your hips toward the target after impact, ending with the hips facing roughly 30–45° left of the target line for a right‑handed golfer (the opposite for left‑handed players). Why it matters: - Drives the body’s kinetic chain forward, allowing the club to release naturally. - Helps keep the swing on plane, preventing “over‑the‑top” or “early release” faults. Beginner cue: After you hit the ball, feel the weight shift from your back foot to your front foot while the hips “open” toward the target. Imagine you are turning to watch the flight of the ball. Visual check: Stand sideways to the target. In a complete finish, your hips should be pointing about a half‑step left of the target line (for right‑handed players), and the left hip should be lower than the right. 2. Shoulder Alignment What it is: The position of the shoulders at the end of the swing, ending roughly parallel to the target line but slightly “open” (the left shoulder down and the right shoulder up for a right‑handed swing). Why it matters: - Indicates that the club has followed the correct swing arc. - Shows that the upper body has rotated in sync with the hips, a sign of good timing.
