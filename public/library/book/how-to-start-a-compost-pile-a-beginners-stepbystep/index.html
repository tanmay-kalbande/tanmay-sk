1. Understanding Composting: What It Is and Why It Matters

What Happens When You Throw Food Scraps "Away"? Imagine this: every week, you fill a trash bag with banana peels, coffee grounds, eggshells, and wilted lettuce. You tie it up, carry it outside, and toss it into a bin that’s whisked away by a garbage truck. That trash ends up in a landfill, where it sits under layers of plastic and other waste. Now, picture this instead: you take those same scraps, add a few dried leaves or shredded paper, and pile them in a corner of your yard. Over time, the scraps shrink, darken, and crumble into a rich, earthy material that smells like a forest floor. That transformation is composting—and it’s happening in millions of backyards, gardens, and communities around the world. So, why does this matter? Because the first scenario—where food scraps go to a landfill—is part of a system that contributes to pollution and wasted resources. The second scenario? It’s a small but powerful act of turning waste into a gift for the earth. This chapter will help you understand what composting really is, how it differs from regular trash disposal, and why it’s a habit worth starting. --- Composting in Plain Language Let’s start with a simple definition: Composting is the natural process of recycling organic materials—like food scraps and yard waste—into a nutrient-rich soil amendment through decomposition. In other words, it’s letting nature do the work of breaking down your kitchen and garden leftovers into something that helps plants grow. It doesn’t require fancy equipment or a science degree. All it needs is the right mix of ingredients, a little air, and time. Why "Organic Materials"? The word organic here doesn’t mean “certified organic” or “pesticide-free.” It just means materials that were once alive or came from living things. Think of: - Fruit and vegetable peels - Coffee grounds and tea bags (without plastic) - Eggshells - Grass clippings and leaves - Small twigs or wood chips These are the building blocks of compost. When you pile them up and let them rot over time, they transform into something new. --- Composting vs. Waste Disposal: What’s the Difference? At first glance, both composting and landfilling deal with “waste.” But the two systems couldn’t be more different in outcome—and in impact on the planet. | Waste Disposal (Landfilling) | Composting | |-------------------------------|----------------| | Food scraps are collected and buried in a landfill. | Food scraps are collected and allowed to decompose in controlled conditions. | | Without oxygen, food rots anaerobically (without air), producing methane, a potent greenhouse gas. | With proper aeration, food breaks down aerobically (with air), releasing carbon dioxide (a less harmful gas) and heat. | | Takes up space in landfills, …

2. The Science Behind Compost: How Decomposition Works

How Nature Turns Waste into Wealth Imagine walking into your garden on a crisp autumn morning. The leaves have just fallen, and instead of bagging them up for the landfill, you shred them and pile them with your vegetable scraps and coffee grounds. Weeks later, you’re left with a dark, crumbly material that smells earthy and rich—something that bears little resemblance to the original scraps. What happened in that pile? How did nature transform your kitchen waste into a substance that nourishes your plants? The answer lies in a complex but elegant process called decomposition, driven by invisible armies of organisms and shaped by the conditions you create. This chapter breaks down the science behind how organic matter breaks down into compost. You don’t need a biology degree—just an understanding of a few key players, the right environment, and what happens at each stage. By the end, you’ll know why your compost pile heats up, why it needs air, and how to set the stage for a successful transformation. --- Microbes at Work: The Tiny Architects of Compost At the heart of composting are microorganisms—tiny living things so small you need a microscope to see them. These include: - Bacteria – The most abundant decomposers. Some types break down sugars and starches quickly, while others handle tougher materials like cellulose from plant stems. - Fungi – Including molds and yeasts. They specialize in breaking down woody materials and help decompose tough plant fibers. - Actinomycetes – A group of bacteria-like organisms that give compost its earthy smell. They break down complex compounds like lignin, found in wood. - Protozoa and nematodes – Microscopic predators that eat bacteria and fungi, recycling nutrients in the process. These microorganisms are always present in soil and on organic materials. When you create a compost pile, you’re essentially feeding and housing them in ideal conditions. As they eat, they reproduce—and the more they eat, the more compost is made. Scenario: You add a banana peel to your compost. Within hours, bacteria begin breaking down its sugars. Soon, fungi join in to decompose the peel’s fibers. As they multiply, they generate heat—just like how your body warms up when digesting food. Microbes need energy and nutrients. Organic waste provides both. The carbon in plant material acts as an energy source, while nitrogen from greens like grass clippings fuels microbial growth. This carbon-to-nitrogen balance (often called the C:N ratio) is crucial. Too much carbon slows decomposition; too much nitrogen can create foul odors and leach nutrients. --- The Breath of Life: Oxygen and Aerobic Decomposition Composting happens in two main ways: aerobic (with oxygen) and anaerobic (without oxygen). 3. Composting Materials: What to Add and What to Avoid

The Right Ingredients: Building Your Compost Recipe Picture this: You’ve just set up your first compost bin in the backyard, full of enthusiasm. You’ve got a pile of kitchen scraps—banana peels, coffee grounds, eggshells—and a few dry leaves you raked up last fall. You chuck everything in, close the lid, and wait. A week later, you check the pile. Instead of the rich, earthy smell of healthy decomposition, you’re hit with a sour, rotten odor. Flies buzz around the top. You’re not sure what went wrong, but clearly, something doesn’t belong in a compost pile. This is exactly why knowing what to add—and what to avoid—is so important. Composting isn’t just about tossing organic material into a pile and hoping for the best. It’s more like baking a cake: you need the right ingredients in the right proportions, or the result will be disappointing, smelly, or even unsafe. The wrong materials can turn your compost pile into a breeding ground for pests, a source of foul odors, or a haven for harmful pathogens. In this chapter, we’ll break down exactly which materials fuel healthy compost and which ones sabotage the process. You’ll learn how to balance your greens and browns, why some seemingly harmless items should never go into your pile, and how a simple 1:3 ratio can make the difference between composting success and failure. By the end, you’ll have the confidence to build a compost pile that turns waste into nutrient-rich soil—safely, efficiently, and without unpleasant surprises. --- What Are "Greens" and "Browns"? Composting relies on two main types of organic materials: greens and browns. These terms aren’t about color—they describe the role each material plays in decomposition. - Greens are materials rich in nitrogen, which provides energy for the microorganisms breaking down your compost pile. They tend to be moist and fresh, like kitchen scraps or fresh grass clippings. - Browns are high in carbon, which gives the microorganisms structure and bulk. They’re typically dry, woody, or fibrous, like dried leaves, straw, or shredded paper. When greens and browns are balanced correctly, microorganisms thrive, decomposition speeds up, and your compost stays aerated and odor-free. Too many greens without enough browns, and your pile turns slimy and smelly. Too many browns without enough greens, and the pile slows to a crawl—like a campfire struggling to stay lit. 5. Setting Up Your Compost Pile: Location and Structure

The "Where" and "How" of Your Compost Site Imagine you’ve spent the last week collecting all your vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, and dried leaves. You have a bucket full of "browns" and "greens" ready to go. You walk out to your backyard and realize you haven't actually decided where the pile should live. You toss it in a random corner, only to find three weeks later that the pile is a soggy, smelly mess because it’s sitting in a low spot where rainwater pools, or it’s completely dried out because it’s in the baking afternoon sun. The success of your composting journey depends heavily on the foundation. While the microbial life does the hard work of decomposition, you are the "site manager." By choosing the right location and building a structure that promotes airflow, you create an environment where bacteria and fungi can thrive without you having to fight against nature. Choosing the Ideal Location You don't need a massive estate to start composting, but you do need a strategic spot. The goal is to balance convenience for you with the biological needs of the microorganisms. 1. The "Convenience Zone" (Proximity to the Kitchen) The most common reason people stop composting is that the pile is too far away. If you have to walk to the far edge of a large property every time you peel a carrot, you are more likely to throw those scraps in the trash. Why it matters: High accessibility ensures a consistent supply of organic matter. The Sweet Spot: Place your pile within a short, easy walking distance from your kitchen door, but far enough away that it doesn't interfere with your primary outdoor living space or walkways. 2. The "Goldilocks" Light Zone (Partial Shade) While the microorganisms in your pile generate their own heat through the decomposition process, extreme external temperatures can disrupt the balance. Full Sun: A pile in direct, scorching sun can dry out too quickly. As we learned in previous chapters, moisture is essential for microbial life; if the pile becomes a desert, decomposition stops. Deep Shade: A pile in a permanently damp, dark area may stay too wet, leading to anaerobic conditions (where oxygen is depleted), which causes the foul smells associated with landfills. The Sweet Spot: A location with partial shade—perhaps under the canopy of a deciduous tree—is ideal. This protects the pile from drying out in the summer while allowing some warmth to penetrate. 3. The "Drainage High Ground" (Well-Draining Soil) Water is a requirement, but standing water is an enemy. You want your compost to be damp, like a wrung-out sponge, not submerged in a puddle. 6. Building Your First Compost Pile: Step-by-Step Instructions

From Pile to Gold: The Assembly Process Imagine you have two piles of organic matter in your backyard. The first is a random heap of grass clippings and kitchen scraps thrown together in a mound. The second is a carefully structured "lasagna" of alternating layers—twigs, leaves, food scraps, and soil. Six months later, the first pile is a slumped, smelly mass that hasn't changed much. The second pile has shrunk by half, is steaming with heat, and has transformed into a rich, dark, earthy substance. The difference isn't the materials; it's the architecture. By building your pile in specific layers, you are essentially building a "house" for the microorganisms we discussed in Chapter 2, ensuring they have the perfect balance of air, water, and food to work at peak efficiency. The Four Essential Layers To build a successful compost pile, you need to organize your materials into four distinct layers. Each layer serves a specific biological purpose. 1. The Base: Coarse Aeration Layer The bottom of your pile is the most critical area for aeration (the process of circulating air through the pile). Without oxygen at the bottom, the pile becomes anaerobic, leading to the production of methane and foul odors. Materials: Small branches, twigs, corn stalks, or coarse brush. Purpose: This creates a "plenum" or a gap at the bottom that allows air to be sucked up into the pile from underneath, preventing the materials from compacting into a dense, airless mat. 2. The Brown Layer: Carbon-Rich Bulk Once your foundation is set, you need your "Browns." As established in Chapter 3, these provide the carbon necessary for microbial energy. Materials: Dried leaves, shredded cardboard, straw, or sawdust. Purpose: Browns provide structure. They create pockets of air throughout the pile and prevent the "Greens" from matting together. 3. The Green Layer: Nitrogen-Rich Fuel This is where the "engine" of your compost pile lives. The nitrogen in these materials fuels the rapid growth of bacteria and fungi. Materials: Vegetable scraps, fruit peels, fresh grass clippings, or coffee grounds. Purpose: Greens provide the essential nutrients that microorganisms need to multiply and break down the tougher carbon structures of the brown layer. 4. The Starter Layer: Microbial Inoculant While microorganisms are present in the air and on your scraps, adding a "starter" layer jump-starts the decomposition process. Materials: A few shovelfuls of finished compost, healthy garden soil, or a handful of forest loam. Purpose: This introduces a concentrated population of microorganisms, such as bacteria and fungi, directly into the heart of the pile. --- Step-by-Step Assembly Instructions Now that you have your materials gathered and your location chosen (as detailed in Chapter 5), it is time to assemble. 7. Maintaining Your Compost Pile: Monitoring and Troubleshooting

The "Set It and Forget It" Myth Imagine you’ve just finished building your first pile. You’ve balanced your C:N ratio, layered your greens and browns, and walked away, expecting nature to take its course. Two weeks later, you return to find your pile smells like rotten eggs, or perhaps it looks exactly as it did the day you built it—no change, no heat, just a static heap of waste. Many beginners believe composting is a "set it and forget it" process. While microorganisms do the heavy lifting, they are living creatures with specific needs. If they run out of oxygen or water, they either stop working or switch to a different, smellier way of breaking things down. Maintaining your compost is essentially "farming" these microorganisms—providing them with the right environment so they can efficiently turn your organic matter into nutrient-rich soil. Mastering Moisture: The Squeeze Test Water is the medium through which microorganisms move and breathe. If the pile is too dry, the bacteria and fungi become dormant (inactive), and decomposition grinds to a halt. If it is too wet, the water fills the air pockets in the pile, drowning the aerobic bacteria and allowing anaerobic bacteria (which don't need oxygen) to take over, creating foul odors. Since you cannot use a traditional moisture meter without spending money on specialized equipment, the most reliable method is the Squeeze Test. How to Perform the Squeeze Test 1. Reach into the center of your pile (use a glove or a small hand trowel). 2. Grab a handful of the material. 3. Squeeze the handful firmly in your fist. Reading the Results Too Dry: The material feels dry to the touch and doesn't hold its shape when you open your hand. It may crumble away immediately. Too Wet: Water streams out of your fist, or the material feels like a saturated sponge. Just Right: The material feels like a wrung-out sponge. It should be damp to the touch, and when you squeeze it, only one or two drops of water should come out. The material should hold its shape for a moment after you open your hand. If your pile is too dry, sprinkle it with water while turning it. If it is too wet, add more "browns" (carbon-rich materials like dried leaves or shredded cardboard) to soak up the excess moisture and create air pockets. 8. When Is Your Compost Ready? Harvesting and Using Finished Compost

The Moment of Truth: Is It Done? Imagine you’ve spent the last few months tending to your pile—turning it, monitoring the moisture, and balancing your materials. You look at the heap and wonder: Can I put this in my garden today, or will it harm my plants? Many beginners make the mistake of using compost too early. When organic matter is still in the middle of the decomposition process, the microorganisms are actively working to break down the materials. If you bury "unfinished" compost in your garden, those microbes will continue their work underground. In doing so, they can actually "steal" nitrogen from the surrounding soil to finish their meal, leaving your plants stunted and yellowed. Knowing exactly when your compost has transitioned from a pile of waste to a nutrient-rich soil amendment is the final hurdle in the composting process. Identifying Finished Compost Finished compost is the end product of the biological process where bacteria, fungi, and other microbial life have fully broken down the organic matter into a stable form called humus. Because every pile is different based on the materials used and the weather, there is no single "date" on a calendar when compost is done. Instead, you must rely on your senses. Visual Characteristics The most obvious sign of finished compost is that you can no longer recognize the original ingredients. Color: Finished compost should be dark brown or almost black. This deep color is a result of the concentrated organic matter and nutrients. Consistency: It should look like rich, crumbly soil. If you still see large chunks of recognizable food scraps, whole eggshells, or intact pieces of cardboard, the pile is either unfinished or needs more time for the microbes to reach those specific areas. Uniformity: The material should look consistent throughout. If you dig deep into the center and find a pocket of wet, smelly leaves while the top looks like soil, the pile needs more turning to ensure everything decomposes evenly. Textural and Olfactory Characteristics How the compost feels and smells provides the most reliable clues about its stability. The Smell Test: Finished compost should have a pleasant, earthy aroma—similar to the smell of a forest floor after a rainstorm. If it smells like ammonia, sulfur (rotten eggs), or sour vinegar, the decomposition process is still active or the pile has become anaerobic (lacking oxygen). The Texture Test: When you squeeze a handful of finished compost, it should feel moist but crumbly. It should not feel slimy or "sticky." 9. Troubleshooting Advanced Composting Problems

When Your Compost Hits a Wall Imagine this: You’ve followed the steps, balanced your greens and browns, and set up your pile with care. You check on it after a week, expecting to feel that satisfying warmth of microbial activity, but the pile is cold. Or perhaps you walk into your backyard and are hit by a pungent smell that reminds you more of a swamp than a garden. For many beginners, this is the moment they feel like they’ve "failed" at composting. However, a compost pile is a living ecosystem. Like any living thing, it can get sick, sluggish, or out of balance. These issues aren't signs of failure; they are signals from your microorganisms telling you exactly what they need to get back to work. The Cold Pile: Why Your Compost Isn't Heating Up In earlier chapters, we discussed how microorganisms generate heat as they break down organic matter. This heat is a sign that the decomposition process is accelerating. When a pile remains cold, it means the microbial life is either dormant or working too slowly to raise the temperature. Diagnosing the Cause If your pile is cold, it is usually due to one of three missing ingredients: Nitrogen, Moisture, or Oxygen. 1. Lack of Nitrogen (Too Many "Browns"): If your C:N ratio is too high—meaning you have a mountain of dried leaves or straw but very few food scraps or fresh grass clippings—the bacteria don't have enough "fuel" to multiply and generate heat. 2. Too Dry: Microorganisms need a thin film of water to move and breathe. If the pile is bone-dry, the bacteria go dormant. 3. Lack of Oxygen: If the pile is too compacted or drenched in water, air cannot reach the center. This suffocates the aerobic bacteria (the ones that create heat) and slows the process to a crawl. 4. Insufficient Mass: Heat is generated in the core of the pile. If your pile is too small (for example, less than 3 feet cubed), the heat escapes into the air faster than the bacteria can produce it. How to Fix a Cold Pile Add "Green" Materials: Mix in fresh grass clippings, coffee grounds, or a layer of food scraps to boost the nitrogen levels. Hydrate: Water the pile slowly while turning it. Aim for the consistency of a wrung-out sponge. Aerate: Use a pitchfork or compost turner to flip the materials. This introduces fresh oxygen to the center. Bulk it Up: If your pile is tiny, add more organic matter. If you don't have enough waste yet, consider merging two smaller piles into one larger one. 10. Composting in Small Spaces: Urban and Apartment Solutions

The "No Yard" Dilemma Imagine you’ve just finished a meal of roasted vegetables and a side of fruit. You remember everything you’ve learned about how organic matter breaks down and the environmental damage caused by waste disposal (landfilling). You want to do the right thing, but you look around your fifth-floor apartment. You have no backyard, no balcony, and your only "outdoor" space is a windowsill with a single succulent. For a long time, the conventional wisdom was that composting required a large plot of land and a heavy-duty pitchfork. But the science of decomposition doesn't require an acre of land—it only requires the right conditions for microbial life to thrive. Whether you live in a studio apartment or a townhouse with a tiny patio, you can still divert waste from the landfill and create nutrient-rich soil. Compact Composting Methods for Small Spaces When space is limited, the goal is to maximize efficiency while minimizing the physical footprint. Traditional piles are replaced by contained systems that manage moisture and airflow more aggressively. 1. Vermicomposting (Worm Bins) Vermicomposting uses specific types of earthworms—typically Red Wigglers (Eisenia fetida)—to accelerate the breakdown of organic matter. Unlike garden worms that tunnel deep into the earth, Red Wigglers stay near the surface and thrive in dense, organic environments. How it works: You provide a ventilated bin with a bedding of shredded cardboard or coconut coir. The worms eat your food scraps and excrete "castings," which are essentially concentrated nutrients for plants. Best for: People who have a small amount of under-sink or closet space and want a high-quality fertilizer alternative. Pros: Extremely fast decomposition; produces some of the richest compost available. Cons: Requires maintaining a living colony of animals; some people find the idea of worms in the house off-putting. 2. Bokashi Binning Bokashi is not composting in the traditional sense; it is a process of fermentation. While traditional composting relies on aerobic (oxygen-breathing) bacteria, Bokashi uses an anaerobic (oxygen-free) process. How it works: Food scraps are placed in an airtight bucket and sprinkled with "Bokashi bran"—wheat bran inoculated with specific microorganisms. The lid is sealed tight, and the food "pickles" rather than rots. Best for: Urban dwellers who want to compost everything, including dairy and meat, which are usually avoided in other small-space methods. Pros: No smell (if done correctly); takes up very little space; handles a wider variety of food scraps. 11. Composting with Pets and Livestock: Safety and Best Practices

The "Brown" and the "Green": The Animal Waste Dilemma Imagine you’ve just set up your first compost pile following the steps in Chapter 6. You have your browns and greens balanced, your moisture is right, and your microbial life is thriving. Then, you look at the bags of bedding from your rabbit hutch or the piles of manure from your neighbor's horse and think, "This is organic matter—it belongs in the pile!" While it is true that animal waste is rich in nutrients, you cannot simply toss every type of pet waste into a standard backyard bin. Doing so can turn a helpful garden resource into a health hazard. The difference lies in the diet of the animal and the types of pathogens—disease-causing microorganisms—that their waste carries. Why Some Pet Waste Requires Special Handling In previous chapters, we discussed how bacteria and fungi break down organic matter. However, not all bacteria are beneficial. Some animal wastes contain pathogens, such as E. coli, Salmonella, and various parasites (like roundworms), which can survive in a cold or "passive" compost pile for months or even years. The primary reason pet waste requires special handling is the distinction between herbivores (animals that eat plants) and omnivores/carnivores (animals that eat meat or a mix of plants and meat). Animals that eat meat consume organisms that carry more complex and dangerous pathogens. When these animals eliminate waste, those pathogens enter the compost. If the compost does not reach a specific, high temperature, these pathogens survive. If you then use that compost on your lettuce or tomatoes, you risk transferring those pathogens directly into your food. Safe vs. Unsafe Animal Waste To keep your garden and family safe, you must categorize animal waste into "safe for general composting" and "unsafe for general composting." Safe Types of Animal Waste These materials come from herbivores and are generally safe for composting, provided they are managed correctly to reach high temperatures. 1. Chicken Manure: Extremely high in nitrogen (a "green" material), making it a powerful fertilizer alternative. 2. Rabbit Manure: Often called "cold manure" because it doesn't need to be composted as intensely as others, though doing so is still recommended for safety. 3. Horse Manure: A classic composting staple that provides excellent bulk and nutrients to the soil. 12. Seasonal Composting Tips: Year-Round Maintenance

The Rhythm of the Pile: Why Seasons Matter Imagine it is the first week of January. You walk outside to add your morning coffee grounds to the compost pile, only to find the entire mass frozen solid, like a giant block of organic ice. Or, picture a blistering August afternoon where your pile has become so dry it feels like sawdust, and no matter how much you turn it, it refuses to break down. If you feel discouraged by these shifts, remember this: your compost pile is a living ecosystem. Just as the plants in your garden go dormant in the winter and grow aggressively in the summer, the microbial life—the bacteria and fungi responsible for decomposition—reacts to the temperature and moisture of the environment. While the basic principles of composting remain the same year-round, the management of your pile must shift with the weather. To keep your decomposition process moving efficiently, you need to adapt your strategy to the seasons. Winter: Managing the Big Chill The most important thing to understand about winter composting is that decomposition slows down. Bacteria are temperature-sensitive; when the air turns freezing, these microorganisms either go dormant or work at a fraction of their normal speed. In some climates, the center of a large pile may stay warm, but in others, the process may stop entirely until spring. Protecting Your Pile from Freezing You cannot force a frozen pile to decompose, but you can prevent it from losing all its heat and protect the microbial life for when spring arrives. Insulate the Pile: Think of this as putting a winter coat on your compost. You can cover your pile with a layer of straw, dried leaves, or even a burlap sack. This traps the internal heat generated by the bacteria and prevents wind from stripping away warmth. Reduce Turning: In the summer, turning the pile introduces oxygen to speed up the process. In the winter, frequent turning lets precious heat escape. Limit your turning to once a month, or stop entirely if the pile is frozen. Bulk Up on "Browns": Adding a thick layer of carbon-rich materials (browns) to the top of the pile acts as a natural thermal blanket. 13. Composting Myths Debunked: Separating Facts from Fiction

Myth 1: Compost Piles Always Smell Bad You’re not alone if the mere thought of a compost pile conjures images of a rotting, stinky mess. Many people avoid composting altogether because they assume it will create a foul odor that lingers in their backyard or apartment. But here’s the truth: a well-maintained compost pile should not smell bad. If it does, that’s usually a sign that something is wrong—not with composting itself, but with how the pile is being managed. Composting is a controlled process of decomposition, driven by microorganisms like bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes. These tiny helpers break down organic matter into simpler compounds. When the pile has the right balance of ingredients and conditions, the process produces mostly carbon dioxide and water vapor—both odorless gases. Any unpleasant smell usually signals an imbalance: too much moisture, not enough air, or an excess of nitrogen-rich materials (often called greens). What Causes Bad Smells? - Anaerobic conditions: When oxygen is absent, decomposition slows, and certain bacteria produce methane and hydrogen sulfide—gases that smell like rotten eggs or sewage. - Too much moisture: A soggy pile suffocates oxygen-loving microbes and encourages anaerobic bacteria. - Too many greens: Fresh grass clippings, food scraps, or manure can create a nitrogen overload, leading to rapid heating and odor. How to Prevent Odors A healthy compost pile is like a well-run kitchen: it needs the right ingredients in the right proportions, good airflow, and regular attention. - Balance greens and browns: Aim for a mix of nitrogen-rich greens (e.g., fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds) and carbon-rich browns (e.g., dry leaves, shredded paper, straw). A good rule of thumb is a 2:1 or 3:1 ratio of browns to greens by volume. - Keep it aerated: Turn your pile every 1–2 weeks using a pitchfork or compost aerator. This introduces oxygen and prevents compaction. - Maintain moisture: The pile should feel like a damp sponge—not dripping wet, not bone dry. If it’s too wet, add more browns. If too dry, sprinkle water and turn it. - Chop materials: Smaller pieces break down faster and allow better airflow. She adds dry leaves and stirs the pile with a stick. Within days, the odor fades as the pile returns to a balanced state. Odor isn’t a sign that composting is failing—it’s a signal to adjust your approach. Compost tea is a liquid extract made by steeping well-aged compost in water, allowing the beneficial microorganisms—bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and even beneficial nematodes—to multiply and disperse into the water.
