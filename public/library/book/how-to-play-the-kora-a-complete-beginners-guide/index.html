1. Introduction to the Kora: History and Overview

The Kora: Where Ancient Strings Meet Timeless Stories Imagine walking through the bustling markets of Dakar or the quiet villages along the Gambia River, where the air hums with the sound of griots—West African oral historians—whose voices carry the weight of centuries. Now picture their hands dancing over a curved wooden instrument, its gourd body resonating with deep, melodic tones. This is the kora: a bridge between past and present, a tool for storytelling, celebration, and spiritual connection. For centuries, it has been more than an instrument; it has been a companion to history itself. If you're picking up a kora for the first time, you're not just learning to play notes—you're joining a lineage of musicians who have used this instrument to preserve memory, negotiate power, and express the soul of a people. But before your fingers touch the strings, it helps to understand what you're holding: where it comes from, why it matters, and how it’s built. This chapter lays the foundation. You’ll learn what the kora is, where it came from, how it’s made, and why it sounds the way it does. No prior knowledge is needed—just curiosity and a willingness to step into a tradition that’s as deep as the rivers it was born beside. --- The Kora in West African Tradition: More Than an Instrument The kora is a spiked harp-lute—a type of stringed instrument with a distinctive curved neck that passes through a large gourd resonator. It belongs to a family of instruments found across West Africa, but the kora is unique for its size, tuning system, and deep cultural role. Unlike many instruments, the kora isn’t just played; it’s embodied—its sound is inseparable from the oral traditions it accompanies. In the Mandinka, Wolof, Fula, and other West African cultures, the kora is sacred. It’s played by griots—hereditary musicians, historians, and praise-singers who act as living archives. A griot doesn’t just play music; they recite genealogies, settle disputes, and narrate the deeds of kings and commoners alike. The kora is their voice. When a griot plays, they’re not just making sound—they’re invoking memory, honor, and identity. Real Scenario: During a village ceremony in southern Senegal, a griot named Lamine arrives under a baobab tree. Elders gather around a fire. Lamine’s kora, wrapped in leather and aged by decades of use, is placed gently on his lap. He begins to play—not a song, but a rhythm that calls the ancestors. Within minutes, the crowd knows: this isn’t just music. It’s a summoning. A remembering. This is the power of the kora: it doesn’t just play songs. It tells stories. This chapter will guide you through selecting a kora that fits your hands, your budget, and your goals. --- Understanding Kora Sizes: Finding the Right Fit The kora isn’t a one-size-fits-all instrument. Kora makers craft them in different sizes to accommodate players of varying heights, hand sizes, and playing styles. Choosing the wrong size can lead to discomfort or difficulty in learning proper technique. Standard Kora Sizes Most beginner koras fall into three main size categories: - Small (Youth) Kora: Typically 12–16 strings, shorter neck, and a smaller calabash gourd. Best for: - Children or players with smaller hands - Travel or casual playing - Those who want a lighter instrument Limitations: May not provide the full tonal range of a larger kora, and advanced techniques can feel cramped. - Medium (Standard) Kora: The most common size, with 21 strings (the standard tuning configuration). Ideal for: - Teenagers and adults with average hand sizes - Learners aiming for traditional or contemporary playing - Most griot traditions Advantages: Balanced sound, comfortable grip, and versatility for both melody and bass lines. - Large (Jali) Kora: 25–30 strings, longer neck, and a deeper, resonant tone. Suited for: - Tall players or those with larger hands - Advanced players seeking a broader tonal range - Specific traditional repertoires requiring extended bass notes Challenges: Heavier to hold, more expensive, and less portable. After months of struggling with the weight and hand position, he switched to a medium 21-string kora—his progress improved immediately. A little regular care can prevent most surprises, keep the tone bright, and extend the life of the beautiful wooden‑and‑gourd instrument you’ve come to love. --- Safe Tuning and Re‑tuning Tuning is the first step before any practice session. Even beginners can tune safely with a systematic approach. 1. Gather the right tools - Electronic tuner (chromatic or kora‑specific). - Fine‑tuning wrench (the small hex key that came with the instrument). - Soft cloth to protect the bridge while you turn the tuning pegs. 2. Understand the tuning layout The kora has 21 strings arranged in two parallel rows. Each string is attached to a tuning peg on the neck (the mango or mahogany spike). The standard West African tuning follows a heptatonic scale, but for beginners the most common pattern is: | Row | String number (from left) | Pitch (relative) | |-----|---------------------------|------------------| | Upper | 1‑11 | G‑A‑B‑C‑D‑E‑F‑G‑A‑B‑C | | Lower | 1‑10 | G‑F‑E‑D‑C‑B‑A‑G‑F‑E | (Refer back to the “Choosing Your First Kora” chapter for a visual diagram.) 3. Step‑by‑step tuning routine 1. Loosen all strings slightly – turn each peg counter‑clockwise a half turn. This reduces tension and protects the wood. 2. Start with the central string (usually the 11th upper string). Pluck and adjust until the tuner reads the correct pitch. 3. Move outward symmetrically – tune the 10th, then the 12th, then the 9th, then the 13th, etc. This keeps the neck balanced and avoids warping. 4. Check for “slippage” – after the first pass, re‑pluck each string. If a peg has moved, tighten it a little more. 5. Final sweep – run a quick scan from low to high to confirm all pitches are stable. 4. Tips for avoiding damage - Never force a peg: If a string refuses to reach pitch, stop turning. Over‑tightening can strip the wood or break the peg. - Temperature matters: Tune in a room that is between 18‑24 °C (65‑75 °F). Extreme cold makes wood brittle; heat can loosen glue. - Use gradual adjustments: Turn each peg no more than a quarter turn at a time, then re‑check the pitch. --- Cleaning the Gourd Resonator The calabash gourd amplifies the kora’s sound. 11. Kora Notation and Tab Reading Basics

A Real‑World Moment: When the Video Stops Making Sense You’re watching a master‑class video of Balla Faseke Kouyaté playing a classic Mandinka lullaby. And where does the dash belong?” If you’ve ever felt that moment of confusion, you’re not alone. The ability to read kora tablature (often simply called “tab”) turns a puzzling string of symbols into a clear roadmap for your fingers. This chapter demystifies the layout of kora tab, teaches you to read and play simple exercises, introduces the few musical symbols you’ll encounter in sheet music, and shows you how to write your own melodies using tab. By the end, you’ll be able to sit down with a piece of paper (or a screen) and know exactly what to do—no guesswork required. --- 1. The Building Blocks of Kora Tablature 1.1 Why Tab Looks Different from Guitar or Ukulele The kora has 21 strings arranged in two parallel rows: the lower (bass) row is plucked with the left hand thumb (p) and the upper (treble) row with the left hand index finger (i). Because the instrument’s layout is unique, the tab system was created specifically for it. Unlike guitar tab, which shows six horizontal lines for six strings, kora tab uses a single line of numbers that reference the string numbers directly. 1.2 String Numbers: The Core Map | String | Position on the Kora | Typical Pitch (when in standard tuning) | |--------|----------------------|---------------------------------------------| | 1 | Uppermost (closest to the player’s left hand) | High G (G₆) | | 2‑10 | Upper row (index finger) | G₆ → B₅ → D₅ → … | | 11‑21 | Lower row (thumb) | C₄ → D₄ → E₄ → … | - Numbers 1‑10 refer to the upper row (index finger). - Numbers 11‑21 refer to the lower row (thumb). When you see a “5” in a tab line, you know you must press the fifth string of the upper row (the fifth string from the top) and pluck it with the index finger. A “15” tells you to press the fifteenth string—the fifth string of the lower row (because 11 is the first lower string)—and pluck it with the thumb. One of them lifts a kora and, after a brief warm‑up, launches into a spontaneous melody that weaves through the fading light. No sheet music, no rehearsed arrangement—just the instant creation of a line that feels both ancient and fresh. That moment captures the heart of improvisation: the art of making music in the moment, using the language of the instrument. In the next pages you will learn the building blocks that let you join that riverside circle, or any jam session, with confidence. --- 1. The Scale Foundations of the Kora 1.1 Pentatonic vs. Hexatonic Traditional West African music most often relies on pentatonic (five‑note) or hexatonic (six‑note) scales. These scales give the kora its characteristic “open” sound because the intervals avoid the half‑steps that dominate Western diatonic scales. | Scale type | Number of notes | Typical interval pattern (in semitones) | |-----------|----------------|----------------------------------------| | Pentatonic (major‑type) | 5 | 2 – 2 – 3 – 2 – 3 | | Pentatonic (minor‑type) | 5 | 3 – 2 – 2 – 3 – 2 | | Hexatonic (Mande) | 6 | 2 – 2 – 2 – 2 – 2 – 2 (often called a whole‑tone collection) | Why it matters: A pentatonic scale naturally reduces dissonance, so any melody you create within it will sound harmonious, even to ears accustomed to Western harmony. 1.2 Finding the Scale on Your Kora Your instrument is already tuned (see Understanding Kora Tuning: Theory and Practice). Most beginner kora sets are tuned to a C major pentatonic layout: - C (root) – 1st string, left hand thumb - D – 2nd string, left hand index - E – 3rd string, left hand middle - G – 4th string, left hand ring - A – 5th string, left hand pinky If your tuning differs, simply map the same interval pattern onto the notes you have. For a hexatonic tuning, the layout often adds an F between E and G, giving a stepwise walk up the scale. 1.3 Visualizing the Scale on the Kora Neck Because the kora’s strings are arranged in two parallel rows, the scale can be “read” like a ladder: - Ascending: pluck the next higher string with the right‑hand thumb (or index) while the left hand presses the appropriate note. - Descending: reverse the direction, using the opposite right‑hand finger.
