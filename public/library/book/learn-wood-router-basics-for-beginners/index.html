1. Module 1: Router Fundamentals & Safety

A Real‑World Moment: The First Edge You’ve just finished sanding a piece of pine that will become the coffee table you’ve been dreaming about. The surface is smooth, the joints are tight, and the only thing left is that elegant “rounded‑edge” profile you saw in a woodworking magazine. You pull out a hand‑held router from the garage, plug it in, and—without a second thought—press the spinning bit against the wood. The machine roars, the bit bites, and a flash of wood shavings erupts. In the split‑second that follows, the router jerks, the bit snaps, and a splinter of wood flies toward your hand. That moment—sharp, sudden, and unsettling—is the perfect illustration of why understanding router fundamentals and safety is the first step toward confident, successful woodworking. Before you carve a decorative groove or shape a complex contour, you must know exactly what a router is, how its parts work together, and how to protect yourself and your workspace. --- 1. What Is a Wood Router? A router is a powered hand tool designed to remove material from wood (or other materials) using a rotating cutting tool called a router bit. By moving the router over a workpiece—or by moving the workpiece under a stationary router—you can create grooves, dados, decorative edges, and three‑dimensional shapes. The tool’s speed, precision, and versatility make it a staple in both hobbyist and professional workshops. Two primary designs dominate the market: | Design | Typical Use | Key Characteristic | |--------|-------------|--------------------| | Hand‑held router | Quick edge work, shallow cuts, routing on the spot | Fixed base, the motor and collet are in a single housing you hold directly | | Plunge router | Precise depth control, mortising, blind cuts, template work | Adjustable depth‑setting mechanism that lets the bit “plunge” into the material | Both types share many components, but each also has unique parts that affect how you set up and operate the tool. --- 2. Major Parts of Hand‑Held and Plunge Routers Below is a breakdown of every major component you’ll encounter on a typical router. Knowing each part’s name and purpose will help you assemble, adjust, and troubleshoot the tool safely. 2.1 Common Components (Hand‑Held & Plunge) - Motor – The heart of the router. Most routers use an electric universal motor that spins at high RPM (revolutions per minute). The motor’s speed determines how fast the bit cuts. - Speed Control (Variable Speed Switch) – A dial or electronic switch that lets you adjust the motor’s RPM. 2. Module 2: Router Types & Power Sources

A Real‑World Dilemma: The DIY Picture‑Frame Project Emma has just finished sanding the pieces for a 12‑inch picture frame. She wants to add a decorative edge and a shallow groove for the glass. She heads to the local hardware store, where two routers catch her eye: a compact hand‑held router on a corded power‑supply and a slightly larger plunge router that runs on a lithium‑ion battery. Which one should she buy? The answer depends on three things: the router’s physical type, the power source, and the performance specifications (voltage and amperage). This chapter unpacks those factors, giving you the tools to pick the right router for any beginner project. --- 1. Hand‑Held vs. Plunge Routers 1.1 What Sets Them Apart | Feature | Hand‑Held Router | Plunge Router | |---------|------------------|---------------| | Base (or Sole Plate) | Fixed, flat sole that sits on the workpiece. | Removable base with a plunge mechanism (spring‑loaded or lever‑controlled) that allows the bit to be lowered into the material. | | Depth‑Adjustment | Simple knob or lever; depth is set before cutting. | Depth can be set and then changed while the router is running, thanks to the plunge action. | | Typical Size & Weight | Lighter, more compact; easier to maneuver in tight spots. | Slightly heavier due to the plunge assembly; feels more solid when routing larger pieces. | | Primary Use Cases | Edge profiling, trimming, shallow dadoes, quick‑cut jobs. | Mortising, creating deep grooves, template routing, any job that benefits from starting the bit above the workpiece and then plunging down. | 1.2 When to Reach for a Hand‑Held Router You need speed and flexibility. Hand‑held routers excel when you’re: - Rounding over edges on a short board or panel. - Cleaning up a joint before glue‑up. - Cutting a shallow dado (≤ 1/4 in) across a narrow strip. Because the base is fixed, the tool feels like a large, powerful drill. You can quickly flip it over and work on the opposite side of a board without re‑setting depth. 1.3 When a Plunge Router Is the Better Choice You need control over depth and entry. Plunge routers shine for: - Deep mortises (e.g., drawer bottoms) where the bit must start above the wood surface. - Template work – the ability to start the bit inside the template without damaging it. - Repeatedly adjusting depth on the fly, such as when routing a series of progressively deeper grooves. The plunge mechanism gives you a clean entry point, reducing tear‑out and producing smoother walls. 1.4 Quick Decision Checklist 1. Is the cut shallow (< 1/4 in) and on an edge? → Hand‑held. 2. 3. Module 3: Router Bits Basics

A First Cut: Turning a Plain Edge into a Polished Profile Imagine you have just sanded the top of a kitchen island and the flat, unfinished edge looks like a “road‑kill” rectangle. You picture a smooth, rounded edge that invites hands to glide along it, but you’re not sure how to get from raw wood to that finished profile without a lot of hand‑carving. The answer lies in selecting the right router bit, installing it correctly, and letting the router do the heavy lifting. This chapter walks you through the anatomy of a router bit, the most common profiles you’ll reach for, how shank size and collet compatibility affect your setup, and the step‑by‑step process for safe installation and removal. By the end, you’ll be able to match any basic edge‑shaping need to the appropriate bit and confidently mount it on your router. 1. Router Bit Anatomy – What Each Part Does Even though you saw a router bit for the first time in Module 1, it helps to break the piece down into its functional parts. Knowing the anatomy makes it easier to spot wear, choose the right bit, and install it without damage. | Part | Description | Why It Matters | |------|-------------|----------------| | Shank | The cylindrical section that slides into the router’s collet. It is usually either ¼ in or ½ in in diameter. | Determines which collet size the bit fits; a mismatch can cause the bit to slip or the motor to strain. | | Cutting Edge(s) | The sharpened teeth or profile that remove material. They may be on one side (single‑flute) or both sides (double‑flute). | The shape of the edge defines the cut profile (straight, round‑over, etc.). | | Bearing (optional) | A metal or plastic ring positioned near the shank that rides on the workpiece. | Guides the bit for clean, repeatable cuts such as rabbets or dados; also protects the workpiece from the shank. | | Tip (or Point) | The very end of the cutting portion. Some bits have a pointed tip, others a flat tip. | A pointed tip helps the bit start cleanly in the wood; a flat tip is safer for certain materials. | | Length (overall) | Measured from the tip to the end of the shank. | Longer bits can reach deeper cuts but may be less rigid; shorter bits are stiffer and better for precision. | Tip: When you first hold a new bit, run your finger lightly along the shank. A Real‑World Situation: The First Edge on a New Shelf You’ve just ripped a ¾‑in. pine board to the width of a future bookshelf. The next step is to cut a clean, straight‑edge profile along the front face so the shelf will sit flush against the wall. The router will be your primary tool for this job, but before you fire it up you need to make three critical decisions: 1. How fast should the router spin? 2. How deep will the cut be? 3. What guide will keep the cut perfectly straight? Getting these settings right the first time saves material, time, and frustration. The following sections walk you through each decision, using the shelf‑edge scenario as a running example. --- 1. Selecting the Right RPM for Your Bit and Cut The speed at which the router’s motor turns—measured in revolutions per minute (RPM)—must match the material of the router bit and the type of cut you intend to make. Too fast, and the bit can overheat, lose its edge, or even burn the wood. Too slow, and you’ll produce chatter, tear‑out, or a rough surface. 1.1 Why RPM Matters - Heat generation: Higher speeds create more friction; heat can dull or damage bits, especially those made from high‑speed steel (HSS). - Chip evacuation: Faster speeds help clear wood shavings, reducing the chance of the bit binding. - Surface finish: The right speed yields a smoother cut with fewer passes. 1.2 Bit Materials and Their Typical RPM Ranges | Bit Material | Recommended RPM Range | Typical Uses | |--------------|-----------------------|--------------| | High‑Speed Steel (HSS) | 12,000 – 18,000 | General‑purpose routing in softwoods, occasional hardwood work | | Carbide‑Tipped | 8,000 – 14,000 | Heavy‑duty cuts, hardwoods, laminates, plastics | | Solid Carbide | 6,000 – 12,000 | Precision work, fine detail, high‑temperature applications | \RPM ranges are guidelines; consult the bit manufacturer’s data sheet when available. 1.3 Matching RPM to Cut Type | Cut Type | Recommended Speed | Reason | |----------|-------------------|--------| | Light, finishing passes (e.g., a shallow decorative edge) | Upper end of the bit’s RPM range | Faster speed reduces burn‑on and produces a clean finish. | | Heavy material removal (e.g., a ¼‑in. dado) | Lower end of the bit’s RPM range | Slower speed gives the bit more torque and prevents overload. | | Fine detail or intricate patterns | Mid‑range | Balances smoothness with control, avoiding vibration. | 1.4 Practical Example: Setting RPM for a ½‑in. 5. Module 5: Basic Cutting Techniques

From Rough Edge to Finished Detail: Why the Right Cut Matters Imagine you are building a simple coffee table for a friend’s living room. The tabletop is a beautiful walnut board, but the raw edges are sharp, the underside has a series of recessed shelves, and the front will need a neat joint to attach a lower apron. With just a hand‑held router and a few basic bits, you can turn those raw edges into elegant round‑overs, cut clean dadoes for the shelves, and create a precise rabbet for the apron—all in a single afternoon. The difference between a “just‑made” piece and a “crafted” piece often lies in mastering these fundamental routing operations. Below is a step‑by‑step guide that walks you through the three core cutting techniques every beginner should master: edge profiling, dado & groove cutting, and rabbet joints. Along the way we’ll reinforce the importance of a steady feed rate and how to keep the wood from scorching. --- 1. Edge Profiling: Adding Shape to Straight Edges Edge profiling is the process of shaping the perimeter of a board. The most common profiles for beginners are the round‑over and the chamfer. Both are created by guiding the router’s base along the edge while the bit removes material to the desired shape. 1.1 Choosing the Right Bit | Profile | Typical Bit Shape | Recommended Size for Beginners | |---------|-------------------|--------------------------------| | Round‑over | Concave, semi‑circular | 1/4"–1/2" radius | | Chamfer | Straight‑cut bevel | 1/8"–1/4" bevel angle (usually 45°) | These bits were introduced in Module 3: Router Bits Basics. Ensure the bit is securely seated in the collet and the collet nut is tightened per the manufacturer’s torque recommendation. 1 Set‑up the Router for Edge Work 1. Mount the router on a stable workbench or use a router table if you have one. For edge profiling, a hand‑held router is often more convenient because you can follow the board’s edge directly. 2. Install an edge guide (optional but highly recommended for beginners). The edge guide slides in the router’s base and rides along the board’s side, keeping the cut parallel to the edge. 3. Adjust the cutting depth using the depth‑adjustment lever/knob. For a 1/4" round‑over, set the bit so that the deepest point of the cut is 1/4" below the board’s surface. 4. Select an appropriate speed on the variable speed switch. 6. Module 6: Intermediate Techniques & Templates

Why Templates Transform Your Router Work Imagine you need ten identical wooden medallions for a set of coasters, each with a perfect 2‑inch circle and a delicate leaf motif inside. Hand‑routing each piece freehand would be a guessing game, and the results would vary. By attaching a template guide—a reusable pattern that the router follows—you turn a potentially inconsistent task into a repeatable, precise operation. Templates are the bridge between artistic intent and mechanical consistency. They let you: Duplicate shapes (letters, logos, decorative cut‑outs) fast and accurately. Control the router’s path without relying on visual judgment alone. Reduce material waste by minimizing errors and tear‑out. In the sections that follow you’ll learn how to select, attach, and use a template, how to route interior cutouts, how bearing‑guided bits keep patterns identical, and how to handle tight radii while keeping the wood clean. --- Selecting and Preparing a Template 1. Template Materials | Material | Pros | Cons | |----------|------|------| | Hardboard / MDF (¼‑½ in thick) | Stable, inexpensive, easy to cut with a jigsaw or router bit | Can chip; not ideal for very fine detail | | Plywood (thin, 3‑mm) | Strong, resists warping, holds fine details | Slightly heavier; may split if not supported | | Acrylic / Lexan (plastic) | Smooth surface, no grain, easy to clean | Can melt if router runs too hot; more expensive | | Thin metal (e.g., aluminum sheet) | Extremely durable, perfect for high‑volume production | Requires special cutting tools; may be overkill for hobby work | For most beginner projects, a ¼‑in hardboard template offers the best balance of cost and performance. 2. Cutting the Template 1. Mark the shape on the template material using a fine‑point pencil or a permanent marker. 2. Secure the material to a sacrificial board with clamps; this prevents movement while you cut. 3. Cut the outline with a fine‑tooth jigsaw or a small router bit (e.g., a ¼‑in straight bit). Keep the cut slightly oversized (≈0.02 in) to allow a snug fit with the router’s bearing. Tip: If you have a router with a plunge mechanism (see Module 1), you can use it to cut the template’s interior details, ensuring perfect alignment with the final piece. 3. Adding Reference Marks - Mounting holes: Drill two or three ¼‑in holes near the edges of the template. These will align with the router’s bearing (the small metal ball that rides along the template’s edge). - Direction arrows: Indicate the intended feed direction—most routers cut best when moving against the grain of the wood. --- Attaching the Template Guide to the Router 1. 7. Module 7: Router Jigs and Accessories

Why Jigs Matter: A Real‑World Example Imagine you are building a set of three‑drawer kitchen cabinets. The drawer fronts need a clean, tight dovetail joint on each side, the drawer bottoms must be mortised into the sides, and every edge has to be perfectly square before you apply the finish. Doing each joint by hand would be slow, inconsistent, and tiring. By adding a few router jigs and a router table, the same hand‑held router that you learned to control in Modules 1‑5 becomes a versatile, repeatable workstation. In the next few pages you’ll see how a simple dovetail jig, a mortising jig, and an edge guide turn a single router into a production‑grade tool, and you’ll learn to set up a router table and use sanding/trim attachments safely. Finally, you’ll build a homemade jig that lets you cut identical rebates for a series of picture frames—proof that you can create your own accessories whenever a commercial one isn’t available. --- Common Router Jigs Jigs are purpose‑built fixtures that hold the workpiece (or the router) in a precise relationship to the cutting bit. They eliminate guesswork, increase repeatability, and dramatically reduce the chance of a mistake. Below are the three most frequently encountered jigs for a beginner who is transitioning from basic cuts to more complex joinery. Dovetail Jig A dovetail jig guides the router along a pre‑set angle to produce the interlocking “tails” and “pins” of a dovetail joint. Key components Template board – a flat piece of MDF or plywood with the desired dovetail pattern milled or cut into it. Rail system – two parallel rails that attach to the router base, allowing the router to slide smoothly while staying locked to the template. Clamps – keep the workpiece firmly against the template. When to use it Drawer or box construction where strength and aesthetics matter. Projects that require multiple identical dovetails (e.g., a set of cabinets). Tips for beginners Start with a medium‑size dovetail (e.g., 1/8‑in. pin, 1/4‑in. tail) before moving to finer pitches. Use a straight‑bit (e.g., 1/4‑in. straight bit) for the pins and a chisel‑shaped bit for the tails, as recommended in Module 3. Mortising (or Mortise) Jig A mortising jig enables you to cut a clean rectangular pocket—called a mortise—into a workpiece, typically to receive a tenon. Key components Guide plate – a flat plate that slides on a set of adjustable stops to control the width of the mortise. Depth stop – a collar or block that limits how deep the router can plunge, using the depth‑adjustment lever you mastered in Module 4. 8. Module 8: Maintenance & Troubleshooting

A Router in Distress: A Real‑World Snapshot You’re midway through a decorative edge on a pine tabletop. The router hums, the bit glides smoothly, and the dust plume is a thin, steady ribbon—until, without warning, the motor sputters, the speed drops, and a cloud of fine wood dust erupts from the vent. You shut the machine off, wipe away the dust, and wonder: What went wrong, and how can I prevent it from happening again? This chapter equips you with the habits and know‑how to keep your router and its bits performing like new, and to diagnose the most common hiccups before they ruin a project. --- 1. The Why of Regular Maintenance A router is a precision‑engineered tool. Even a small amount of dust or a dull bit can change the way the collet, base, and plunge mechanism interact with the workpiece, leading to: Reduced cutting quality – tear‑out, chatter, or uneven edges. Increased wear – premature bearing failure, overheating motor, or stripped collet threads. Safety hazards – loss of control, kick‑back, or electric shock. Treating maintenance as a short, routine “check‑up” after every 5–10 hours of use (or after each project) pays off in smoother cuts, longer tool life, and fewer interruptions. --- 2. Cleaning the Router: Dust‑Free is Best‑Free Dust is the silent enemy of any power tool. It settles in vents, lubricated bearings, and moving parts, acting like sandpaper on metal surfaces. 2.1 Gather Your Cleaning Kit | Item | Why It’s Needed | |------|-----------------| | Soft‑bristled brush (e.g., paintbrush) | Dislodges dust from vents and crevices without scratching metal | | Compressed‑air canister (or a low‑pressure air compressor) | Blows out dust from hard‑to‑reach spots | | Lint‑free cloth (micro‑fiber) | Wipes away residual particles and oil | | Mild soap solution (water + a drop of dish soap) | Removes sticky residue from the base and collet | | Vacuum with a narrow nozzle attachment | Collects loose debris without scattering it | Tip: Always unplug the router before cleaning. A powered tool can start unexpectedly if the trigger is engaged. 2.2 Step‑by‑Step Cleaning Procedure 1. Detach the collet and bit (refer to Module 4 for removal). Place the bit in a separate container to avoid damage. 2. Brush the vents on the housing and base plate. Use short strokes to pull dust outward. 3. Apply compressed air to the vent openings and the interior of the collet nut. Hold the can upright and spray in short bursts to avoid condensation. 4.
