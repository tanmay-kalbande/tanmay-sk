1. 1. Introduction to First Aid and CPR

A Moment That Could Change Everything Imagine you are walking through a busy city park on a sunny Saturday afternoon. A jogger collapses nearby, clutching his chest. A few strangers gather, eyes wide, phones out, but no one steps forward. You feel a surge of adrenaline and a sudden question: What should you do? Your response—whether you call for help, begin chest compressions, or simply stay frozen—could be the difference between life and death. This split‑second decision is at the heart of first aid and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Understanding what these interventions are, why they matter, and what responsibilities accompany them is the first step toward becoming a confident, capable responder. --- Defining First Aid First aid is the immediate, temporary care given to a person who is injured or suddenly ill, with the aim of: 1. Preserving life – stopping the condition from worsening. 2. Preventing further injury – protecting the victim from additional harm. 3. Promoting recovery – preparing the person for professional medical treatment. First aid is not a substitute for professional medical care; it is the bridge that buys critical time until emergency services arrive. The practice includes simple actions such as: - Controlling bleeding with direct pressure. - Positioning an unconscious person to keep the airway open. - Treating minor burns with cool water. Because emergencies can happen anywhere—at home, work, school, or public spaces—first aid skills are valuable for anyone, regardless of age, occupation, or fitness level. Core Elements of First Aid | Element | What It Means | Typical Action | |---------|---------------|----------------| | Safety | Ensure the environment is safe for both rescuer and victim. | Look for traffic, fire, or hazardous substances. | | Assessment | Quickly evaluate the victim’s condition. | Check responsiveness, breathing, and bleeding. | | Help | Activate emergency medical services (EMS). | Call 911 (or local emergency number) and give clear details. | | Intervention | Provide appropriate first‑aid measures. | Apply a bandage, perform CPR, or place the person in recovery position. | | Monitoring | Keep watching the victim until help arrives. | Re‑check breathing, comfort the person, and hand over information to EMS. | --- Defining Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR) Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is a lifesaving technique used when a person’s heart stops beating effectively (cardiac arrest). The goal is to maintain blood flow to the brain and other vital organs until the heart can be restarted, either spontaneously or with advanced medical equipment. CPR consists of two fundamental actions: - Chest compressions – rhythmic pressing on the sternum to manually pump blood. - Ventilations (breaths) – delivering air into the lungs to oxygenate the blood. 2. 2. Safety and Scene Assessment

A Real‑World Wake‑Up Call Imagine you’re walking your dog when a screeching car brakes hard a few metres ahead. The driver, pale and shaking, is trying to climb out of the vehicle while a small flame licks the rear bumper. A passerby shouts, “Help!”—but the traffic is still moving, the fire is growing, and a faint smell of gasoline hangs in the air. Before you rush to the driver, you must decide: Is the scene safe for you to enter? Your split‑second decision will determine whether you can help at all. This chapter shows how to scan a scene, spot hidden dangers, and perform a rapid primary assessment of the victim—using the DRSABCD protocol—as the very first step in any first‑aid response. --- Why Scene Safety Comes First The moment you step into a hazardous environment, you become a second victim if the danger is not controlled. Injuring yourself not only harms you but also removes a valuable rescuer from the situation. The principle of “Safety first” underpins every action you will take later—whether it is opening an airway, delivering chest compressions, or applying a bandage. Bottom line: If the scene is unsafe, you cannot safely provide aid. --- The DRSABCD Overview The DRSABCD acronym is the backbone of a systematic response: | Letter | Meaning | Quick reminder | |--------|---------|----------------| | D | Danger – Identify and control hazards. | | R | Response – Check the victim’s level of consciousness. | | S | Send for help – Call emergency services early. | | A | Airway – Ensure a clear airway. | | B | Breathing – Look for normal breathing. | | C | CPR – Begin chest compressions if needed. | | D | Defibrillation – Use an AED if available. | Only after the D (danger) step is completed can you safely move on to R (response) and the rest of the chain. This chapter focuses on the first two letters—Danger and Response—plus the rapid primary assessment that follows. --- Recognizing Common Environmental Hazards Even a brief glance can reveal a range of threats. Below are the most frequent hazards you may encounter in everyday settings. 1. Traffic‑Related Hazards - Moving vehicles – Cars, bicycles, motorcycles, or trucks that may not have seen the incident. - Blind spots – Intersections, curves, or parked cars that hide oncoming traffic. - Road surface – Slippery, uneven, or debris‑covered surfaces that can cause a fall. 3. 3. Adult CPR – Hands‑Only and Full Technique

A Real‑World Moment Imagine you’re in a busy supermarket aisle. A middle‑aged man suddenly clutches his chest, collapses onto the floor, and remains unresponsive. Shoppers gasp, a few dial 911, but the seconds feel like minutes. In that brief window, your actions can mean the difference between life and death. This is the exact situation where adult CPR—whether hands‑only or with rescue breaths—becomes the critical link in the Chain of Survival. --- 1. Confirming Cardiac Arrest Before you start compressions, make sure the person is truly in cardiac arrest. 1. Check responsiveness – Gently tap the shoulder and shout, “Are you okay?” 2. Look for normal breathing – If the victim is not breathing or is only gasping, treat it as cardiac arrest. 3. Call for help – If you haven’t already, shout for someone to call emergency services (or call yourself if you’re alone). These steps echo the scene‑assessment principles covered in Safety and Scene Assessment: protect yourself, secure the area, and summon professional help before you intervene. --- 2. Hands‑Only CPR Hands‑only CPR is the simplest, most universally applicable method for adult victims when you are untrained, uncomfortable with mouth‑to‑mouth contact, or when the situation is chaotic. When to Use Hands‑Only CPR - You have no formal CPR training or have not refreshed your skills in the past two years. - The victim is an adult who collapsed suddenly from a presumed cardiac cause (e.g., heart attack, arrhythmia). - Immediate rescue breaths are not feasible (e.g., crowded environment, language barrier, concern about disease transmission). Stack the other hand on top, interlocking the fingers. | | 3 | Set compression depth | Aim for 2‑2.4 inches (5‑6 cm). Use the weight of your upper body to achieve depth; avoid “pumping” with just the arms. | | 4 | Set compression rate | Deliver 100‑120 compressions per minute. 4. 4. CPR for Children and Infants

When Every Second Counts: A Playground Emergency A 4‑year‑old girl collapses while playing tag. She is silent, not moving, and her skin looks pale. A nearby adult rushes over, checks for responsiveness, and realizes the child is not breathing. The adult knows the steps from Safety and Scene Assessment – they call for help, protect themselves, and begin CPR. In the next minutes, the correct depth of chest compressions and the right compression‑to‑ventilation rhythm could be the difference between a full recovery and lasting brain injury. This scenario illustrates why CPR for children (ages 1‑8) and infants (under 1 year) is not simply a scaled‑down version of adult CPR. Their bodies are smaller, their ribs are more flexible, and their heart rates are naturally faster. The techniques you will learn in this chapter adapt the adult method to these anatomical and physiological differences, giving you the confidence to act safely and effectively. --- Age Groups and Why They Matter | Age Group | Approximate Size | Key Anatomical Feature | Typical Heart Rate (beats/min) | |-----------|------------------|------------------------|-------------------------------| | Infant | < 1 year (≈ 5 kg, 55 cm) | Very soft sternum, pliable ribs | 120‑160 | | Child | 1‑8 years (≈ 10‑30 kg, 80‑130 cm) | More rigid chest wall than infant, but still less than adult | 80‑120 | Understanding these differences guides two critical decisions: 1. How much force to use – Too much pressure can fracture ribs or damage internal organs. 2. How to position the hands – Hand placement and size affect the quality of compressions. --- Quick Assessment Before Chest Compressions 1. Check responsiveness – Tap gently and shout “Are you okay?” 2. Call for help – If you are alone, shout for a bystander and activate emergency services (e.g., “Call 911”). 3. Open the airway – Use the Head‑Tilt, Chin‑Lift method unless a neck injury is suspected (then use Head‑Tilt, Chin‑Lift with C‑spine precautions). 4. Look, listen, feel for breathing – Do this for no more than 10 seconds. 5. If the child or infant is not breathing or only gasping, begin CPR immediately. These steps echo the early recognition and early CPR components of the Chain of Survival introduced earlier; the only addition here is the emphasis on age‑appropriate technique once you start compressions. --- Chest Compressions: Depth, Hand Technique, and Rate 1. 5. 5. Managing Airway Obstructions (Choking)

A Life‑Saving Moment in a Family Kitchen Emily, a 32‑year‑old mother, was preparing dinner when her 4‑year‑old son, Max, grabbed a piece of hot dog and began to eat it quickly. Within seconds Max clutched his throat, his eyes widened, and he let out a high‑pitched “I can’t… breathe!” He fell to the floor, coughing violently but unable to speak. Emily’s heart raced – she knew she had to act fast, but she wasn’t sure whether Max was choking partially or completely. This scenario illustrates the split‑second decisions you must make when a person’s airway is blocked. The good news is that, with the right knowledge, you can recognize the type of obstruction, choose the correct technique, and restore breathing before the situation becomes life‑threatening. --- Recognizing Airway Obstructions Partial vs. Complete Obstruction | Partial (Partial Airway Obstruction) | Complete (Total Airway Obstruction) | |---|---| | Signs | Signs | | • Coughing, gagging, or choking sounds | • No cough, no speech, no cry | | • Ability to make some noise (e.g., “I’m choking”) | • Silent struggle; may make a high‑pitched “gurgling” sound (called a stridor) | | • Can still breathe, though with difficulty | • Inability to breathe or speak; looks panicked | | What to Do | What to Do | | • Encourage the victim to keep coughing | • Immediate first‑aid intervention (back blows, chest thrusts, or abdominal thrusts) | | • Do not perform abdominal thrusts unless coughing stops | • Do not wait for coughing to start; act right away | Key point: A person who can cough or speak is still moving air past the obstruction and should be encouraged to keep coughing. When the airway is totally blocked, you must intervene quickly. Quick Assessment Checklist 1. Ask, “Are you choking?” (If the person can answer, it is likely a partial obstruction.) 2. Listen for the sound of a cough or a high‑pitched noise. 3. Observe the throat area – a visible object may be seen. 4. Check for breathing – look for chest rise, listen for breath sounds, feel for air movement. If the person cannot speak, cough, or breathe, treat it as a complete obstruction. --- The Heimlich Maneuver (Abdominal Thrusts) The Heimlich maneuver is the most effective method for relieving a complete airway obstruction in conscious adults and children over one year old. It uses a rapid upward thrust to increase pressure in the airway, forcing the foreign object out. 6. 6. Controlling Bleeding and Basic Wound Care

A Split‑Second Decision Imagine you’re helping a friend set up a backyard barbecue. As she slices a watermelon, the knife slips and a deep gash opens on her forearm. Bright red blood spurts, and within seconds the pool of blood is growing. Your heart races—what do you do? The answer isn’t “call an ambulance and wait.” In those critical first minutes you must stop the bleeding, protect the wound, and prevent infection. This chapter walks you through exactly how, from recognizing the type of bleed to applying the right pressure technique and caring for the wound afterward. --- Understanding the Types of Bleeding Blood can escape from three different kinds of vessels, each with its own appearance and urgency. | Vessel | Typical Appearance | Flow Rate | Typical Location | |--------|-------------------|-----------|------------------| | Capillary | Small, oozing or dripping red or dark blood | Slow | Superficial skin, minor cuts | | Venous | Steady dark red flow that may spurts when the vein is disturbed | Moderate | Larger cuts, especially on limbs | | Arterial | Bright red, pulsatile spurting that matches the heartbeat | Rapid | Deep lacerations, puncture wounds near major arteries (e.g., wrist, thigh) | Why it matters: - Arterial bleeding can lead to life‑threatening blood loss in seconds. - Venous bleeding is slower but still needs prompt control. - Capillary bleeding is usually manageable with simple pressure. When you first see a wound, pause a moment to identify the bleed type. This guides the level of intervention you’ll need. --- Immediate Actions: “What Should You Do?” The question “What should you do?” appears throughout first‑aid training. After you have completed the Safety and Scene Assessment (Chapter 2) and ensured the environment is safe, the next steps are: 1. Call for help – dial emergency services (e.g., 911) and give a concise location and description. 2. Expose the wound – gently remove clothing or bandages that might be concealing the injury. 3. Control the bleeding – start with the least invasive method that can stop the flow. Below are the three core techniques, listed from most common to most extreme. 1. Direct Pressure Direct pressure is the first line of defense for any bleed. How to apply: 1. Use a clean cloth (gauze, a washcloth, or even a clean shirt) and place it over the wound. 2. Press firmly with the heel of your hand, using your body weight if needed. 3. Maintain pressure for at least 5 minutes without checking to see if the blood has stopped. 4. 7. 7. Burns and Thermal Injuries

A Hot Kitchen Accident You’re preparing dinner and a pot of boiling water spills onto a child’s forearm. The skin sizzles, tears form, and the child screams. In the split‑second you have to decide what to do, your first priority is the same as in any emergency: preserve life, prevent further injury, and promote recovery. The steps you take now—how you assess the burn, how quickly you cool it, and what you cover it with—will determine how much tissue survives. --- 1. Understanding Burn Severity Burns are injuries caused by heat, chemicals, electricity, or radiation. For first‑aid purposes we focus on thermal burns (heat, flame, hot liquids, steam). The severity of a burn is judged by two factors: | Factor | What It Means | How to Evaluate | |--------|---------------|-----------------| | Degree (Depth) | How deep the damage goes into the skin | Look at color, pain, and texture | | Surface Area | How much of the body is affected | Use the “Rule of Nines” or “Palmar Method” | 1.1 Degrees of Burns | Degree | Depth of Skin Involved | Appearance | Pain | Typical Causes | |--------|------------------------|------------|------|----------------| | First‑degree (superficial) | Epidermis only (outermost layer) | Red, dry, no blisters | Burns sting, like a sunburn | Light flash flame, brief contact | | Second‑degree (partial‑thickness) | Epidermis + part of dermis (middle layer) | Red with blisters, moist surface | Very painful | Hot water, steam, prolonged contact | | Third‑degree (full‑thickness) | Epidermis, dermis, and possibly underlying tissue | White, leathery, or charred; no blisters | May be painless (nerve endings destroyed) | Direct flame, contact with hot metal, chemical burns | First‑degree burns heal on their own in 5‑7 days. Second‑degree burns can heal in 2‑3 weeks if small, but larger areas may need medical care. Third‑degree burns always require professional treatment. 1.2 Estimating Burn Surface Area Rule of Nines (Adults) The body is divided into sections that represent 9 % (or multiples) of total body surface area (TBSA): - Head & neck: 9 % - Each arm: 9 % - Each leg: 18 % (front + back) - Front of torso: 18 % - Back of torso: 18 % - Perineum/genitals: 1 % Add the percentages for the burned regions. For example, a burn covering the anterior (front) thigh (9 %) and the front of the torso (18 %) equals 27 % TBSA. Palmar Method (Children & Small Areas) The palmar surface of the child’s hand (including fingers) ≈ 1 % of their TBSA. 8. 8. Common Medical Emergencies

A Real‑World Flashpoint It’s a Saturday afternoon at a bustling community fair. While you’re waiting in line for a pretzel, a nearby vendor suddenly clutches his chest, his face turning pale. A friend nearby shouts, “Call 911!” You step forward, heart racing, wondering what to do. In the next few minutes, the same crowd could also be faced with a person slumped on a bench after a sugar‑filled snack, a child wheezing after a bee sting, or a neighbor struggling to speak after a sudden facial droop. Knowing how to spot the warning signs and act quickly can mean the difference between a full recovery and a life‑threatening outcome. Below are the four most common medical emergencies you are likely to encounter in everyday settings, together with clear, step‑by‑step first‑aid protocols that build on the safety and scene‑assessment principles you already mastered in Chapter 2. --- 1. Recognizing the Emergency 1.1 Myocardial Infarction (Heart Attack) Definition: A myocardial infarction (MI) occurs when blood flow to a portion of the heart muscle is blocked, usually by a blood clot, causing damage to heart tissue. Key symptoms (remember “CHOP”) - Chest pain or pressure, often described as a heavy, squeezing sensation - Heavy sweating (diaphoresis) - Out‑of‑breath, especially with mild exertion - Palpitations (rapid or irregular heartbeat) Additional clues: nausea, light‑headedness, pain radiating to the left arm, jaw, or back. 1.2 Stroke Definition: A stroke happens when blood flow to the brain is interrupted (ischemic) or a blood vessel bursts (hemorrhagic), leading to rapid loss of brain function. Key symptoms (FAST) - Face drooping – one side of the face looks uneven when smiling - Arm weakness – the person cannot raise one arm or it drifts downward - Speech difficulty – slurred or strange‑sounding speech, trouble understanding - Time to call emergency services – note the exact time symptoms began Other signs: sudden vision changes, severe headache, loss of balance or coordination. 1.3 Hypoglycemia (Low Blood Sugar) Definition: Hypoglycemia occurs when blood glucose falls below normal levels, often in people with diabetes who have taken too much insulin or missed a meal. Key symptoms - Shakiness, trembling, or tremor - Sweating, pale skin - Hunger, nausea - Confusion, irritability, or sudden mood changes - Slurred speech, vision changes, or loss of consciousness (if severe) 1.4 Anaphylaxis (Severe Allergic Reaction) Definition: Anaphylaxis is a rapid, systemic allergic reaction that can cause airway swelling, a sudden drop in blood pressure, and potentially death if untreated. 9. 9. Building an Emergency Kit and Maintaining Skills

A Real‑World Snap‑Decision You’re in the kitchen preparing dinner when the timer blares, a pot of boiling water slips from the counter, and a child runs into the room, grabbing a hot pan. Within seconds you’ve got a burn, a cut, and a cry for help. Your mind races through the steps you learned in Safety and Scene Assessment: check the environment, call for help, protect yourself, then begin first‑aid interventions. But where is the first‑aid kit? Is the bandage you need even in the drawer? Do you have the right supplies to manage a burn while also performing CPR for Children and Infants if the child collapses? Having a well‑stocked, purpose‑built kit and a plan for keeping your skills sharp can turn a chaotic moment into a controlled response. This chapter walks you through exactly that—what to pack, how to adapt the kit for different settings, and how to make practice a habit. --- 1. Core Items for a Basic First‑Aid Kit A first‑aid kit is a collection of supplies that lets you provide basic first‑aid until professional help arrives. Below is a concise list of items that cover the most common emergencies discussed in earlier chapters (bleeding, burns, choking, etc.).
