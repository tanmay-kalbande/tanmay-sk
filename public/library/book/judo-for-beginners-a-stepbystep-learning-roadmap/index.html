1. Introduction to Judo

The Moment of Maximum Efficiency Imagine two people facing off. One is a heavyweight, towering and muscular, possessing raw strength that could easily overpower most opponents. The other is smaller, leaner, and seemingly disadvantaged in a test of force. The larger opponent lunges forward with a powerful, aggressive push. Instead of pushing back—which would be a battle of strength the smaller person is likely to lose—the smaller practitioner steps slightly to the side and pulls the larger opponent in the direction they were already moving. With a precise pivot and a subtle shift in balance, the smaller person uses the giant's own momentum to send them sailing through the air, landing flat on their back. This is the essence of Judo. It is not a battle of muscles, but a battle of physics, timing, and psychology. The word Judo literally translates to "The Gentle Way" (Ju meaning gentle or yielding, and Do meaning way or path). However, "gentle" does not mean weak; it means flexible. It is the art of using an opponent's strength against them, ensuring that the person who wins is not necessarily the strongest, but the one who understands the principles of balance and momentum. The Origins of Judo To understand Judo, one must understand the world from which it emerged. Judo did not appear in a vacuum; it was a deliberate evolution of ancient Japanese combat systems. From Jujutsu to Judo For centuries, the samurai class practiced Jujutsu, a broad term for various grappling arts used on the battlefield. Jujutsu was designed for survival; its goal was to neutralize an enemy—often an armored opponent—using joint locks, throws, and strikes. Because samurai often fought with weapons, Jujutsu focused on how to fight if you were disarmed or if the fight moved to the ground. By the late 19th century, Japan was undergoing a massive cultural shift. The era of the samurai was ending, and the traditional martial arts were fading into obscurity. A young man named Kano Jigoro sought to preserve these ancient techniques but felt they were too violent and lacked a cohesive educational purpose. The Vision of Kano Jigoro Kano Jigoro was a scholar and an educator. He studied several styles of Jujutsu, but he noticed a problem: many of the techniques were dangerous to practice, and the training was often haphazard. He believed that a martial art should be more than just a method of fighting; it should be a tool for personal development and social contribution. In 1882, Kano founded the Kodokan (The Place for Studying the Way). 2. Basic Judo Etiquette and Safety

The Invisible Contract of the Dojo Imagine walking into a room where two people are locked in a fierce struggle, throwing each other to the ground with significant force. To an outsider, it looks like a fight. But to the practitioners, it is a conversation. The only reason they can push their bodies to the limit without causing permanent harm is because they have entered into an invisible contract of trust. This contract is not written in a handbook; it is expressed through Etiquette (known in Japanese as Reigi). In Judo, etiquette is not about mindless tradition or "acting formal" for the sake of it. It is a practical safety mechanism. When you bow to your partner, you aren't just following a rule—you are signaling, "I trust you with my safety, and I promise to protect yours." This is the living application of Jita Kyoei (Mutual Welfare). If you ignore the etiquette, you break the trust, and the training becomes dangerous. The Art of the Bow (Rei) The bow is the most fundamental gesture in the Dojo. It marks the beginning and end of every interaction, creating a psychological boundary between the chaos of the outside world and the focused environment of the training hall. The Standing Bow (Ritsurei) The standing bow is used when greeting instructors, partners, or entering the training area. 1. Posture: Stand with your heels together and toes slightly apart (forming a small "V" shape). Your arms should be straight at your sides, palms facing your thighs. 2. The Movement: Bend forward from the waist, keeping your back straight. Do not simply drop your head; move your entire torso as one unit. 3. The Angle: Formal Bow (approx. 30–45 degrees): Used for the Sensei (teacher) or during official ceremonies. Informal Bow (approx. 15 degrees): Used between training partners before and after a drill. 4. The Gaze: Your eyes should follow the movement of your head, looking slightly downward, but not staring directly at the floor at your feet. 5. The Return: Return to the standing position smoothly and with control. The Kneeling Bow (Zarei) The kneeling bow is more formal and is typically performed at the start and end of a class while sitting in Seiza (the formal kneeling position). 1. The Position (Seiza): Kneel on the mat with your big toes overlapping or touching, and your weight resting on your heels. Your backs of the feet should be flat on the mat. 2. 3. Fundamental Stances and Footwork

The Foundation of Balance Imagine two people facing each other. One is standing with their feet glued together, leaning slightly backward. The other is standing with feet shoulder-width apart, knees softly bent, weight distributed evenly. If you gave both of them a sudden, firm push on the shoulder, who would fall first? The answer is obvious. In Judo, your feet are your anchor. Without a stable base, the most powerful throw in the world becomes impossible to execute, and the simplest push becomes a dangerous fall. While the flashy throws (Nage Waza) are what people see in the Olympics, those movements are merely the "roof" of the house. Stances and footwork are the foundation. If the foundation is shaky, the house collapses. To embody Seiryoku Zenyo (Maximum Efficiency), you must first master how to stand and move so that you waste no energy fighting your own lack of balance. Understanding Shizentai (The Natural Stance) Before learning specialized fighting stances, you must master Shizentai, the Japanese term for "natural posture." Shizentai is not a "pose"; it is a state of readiness. It is the neutral position from which all other movements begin. If you are too tense, you are slow; if you are too relaxed, you are easily pushed. How to Achieve Shizentai To find your natural stance, follow these alignment cues from the ground up: 1. The Feet: Place your feet roughly shoulder-width apart. Your toes should point forward or be turned out slightly (no more than 15 degrees). 2. The Weight: Distribute your weight evenly between both feet. Avoid leaning too far onto your heels (which makes you easy to push backward) or your toes (which makes you easy to pull forward). 3. The Knees: Keep a "soft" bend in the knees. Never lock your joints. Locked knees act like brittle sticks; soft knees act like shock absorbers, allowing you to react instantly to an opponent's movement. 4. The Hips: Keep your pelvis neutral. Avoid arching your back excessively or slouching. Your hips should feel "heavy," as if they are sinking slightly toward the mat. 5. The Upper Body: Keep your shoulders relaxed and down, away from your ears. Your spine should be straight, maintaining the posture principles introduced in your first lessons. The Purpose of Neutrality The goal of Shizentai is to hide your intentions. If you stand in a rigid, aggressive stance, your opponent knows you are about to attack. By maintaining a natural, relaxed posture, you keep your opponent guessing, making it easier to achieve Kuzushi (off-balancing) when you finally decide to move. 4. Breaking Falls (Ukemi)

The Art of Falling Imagine you are standing in the dojo, and for the first time, a partner applies a technique that knocks you off your feet. For a split second, gravity takes over. Your instinct—the one developed over a lifetime of walking—is to stiffen your muscles and reach out your arms to "catch" yourself. In Judo, following that instinct is the fastest way to get injured. If you land with a locked elbow or a stiff wrist, the entire force of your body weight is concentrated into a tiny joint. This often results in sprains, fractures, or dislocations. To practice Judo safely, you must unlearn the instinct to resist the fall and instead learn how to "flow" with it. This is the essence of Ukemi, the Japanese term for "the art of receiving" or "breakfalling." Ukemi is not just a safety measure; it is a fundamental skill. You cannot learn to throw (Nage Waza) until you know how to be thrown. By mastering Ukemi, you remove the fear of falling, which allows you to relax your body and apply the principles of Seiryoku Zenyo (Maximum Efficiency) even when you are the one hitting the mat. The Physics of a Safe Fall Before practicing specific movements, you must understand the two primary goals of every breakfall: surface area expansion and energy dissipation. 1. Surface Area Expansion: If you land on a single point (like a palm or an elbow), the pressure is immense. If you land on a wide area (like the flat of your back and arm), the force is spread across a larger portion of your body, reducing the impact on any single point. 2. Energy Dissipation: Instead of hitting the mat and stopping abruptly (a "thud"), you want to turn the downward energy into a rolling or sliding motion. By converting linear energy into rotational energy, you "bleed off" the force of the fall. Throughout this chapter, remember the golden rule of Ukemi: Protect your head. Your head should never touch the mat. This is achieved by tucking your chin toward your chest, creating a protective pocket of space. 5. Grips and Hand Positions

The Steering Wheel of Judo Imagine you are trying to move a heavy piece of furniture across a room. If you push it with your palms flat against the surface, your hands will likely slip, and you will exhaust yourself quickly. But if you can get a firm hold of a handle or a sturdy edge, you can direct that furniture with precision, using far less effort. In Judo, your opponent's uniform—the Judogi—is that handle. Everything in Judo begins with the grip. You cannot achieve Kuzushi (off-balancing) if you cannot control your opponent. Your hands are not merely tools for holding on; they are the steering wheel and the brakes of the fight. If your grip is weak or poorly positioned, you are a passenger in the match. If your grip is strong and intentional, you are the driver. Understanding Kumikata The Japanese term for gripping is Kumikata. This refers to the art of taking, maintaining, and fighting for grips. For a beginner, Kumikata is often the most frustrating part of training. You will find your fingers slipping, your knuckles rubbing against heavy fabric, and your opponent constantly stripping your hands away. However, mastering the basics of Kumikata allows you to apply the principle of Seiryoku Zenyo (Maximum Efficiency); instead of using raw muscle to pull an opponent, you use the leverage provided by the fabric of the Gi to move their entire body mass. The Primary Grips While there are dozens of specialized grips used by advanced practitioners, almost every technique in Judo is built upon two fundamental points of contact: the sleeve and the lapel. The Sleeve Grip (Sode) The sleeve grip is primarily used to control the opponent's shoulder and arm. By controlling the arm, you limit their ability to posture up or strike back. How to execute the sleeve grip: 1. The Reach: Reach across with your hand to the opponent's sleeve, usually just above the elbow. 2. The Grip: Do not grab the fabric with your whole palm. Instead, use a "C-clamp" shape. Your thumb should be on the inside of the arm, and your fingers should wrap firmly around the outside. 3. The Tension: Grip firmly, but avoid "death-gripping" (squeezing so hard that your forearm muscles lock up). A locked forearm is slow and rigid; a firm but flexible grip is fast and responsive. Why the elbow? Grabbing the wrist gives you control over the hand, but grabbing above the elbow gives you control over the entire shoulder girdle, which is essential for creating the rotation needed for throws. 6. Basic Throwing Techniques (Nage Waza)

The Magic of the Pivot: Why Power Isn't the Secret Imagine two people facing each other. One is a heavyweight powerhouse, leaning into their opponent with all their strength. The other is a smaller practitioner who doesn't push back. Instead, the smaller person simply steps slightly to the side and pulls the heavyweight's sleeve in the direction they are already leaning. In a split second, the heavyweight is airborne, rotating through the air, and landing flat on their back. To an outsider, it looks like a feat of strength. To a Judoka, it is a simple application of physics. Throwing in Judo, known as Nage Waza, is not about muscularly hoisting a human being into the air. If you try to "muscle" a throw, you will fatigue quickly and likely fail against a strong opponent. Instead, Nage Waza is the art of removing a person's balance and using your own body as a fulcrum—a pivot point—to let gravity do the heavy lifting. The Three Pillars of a Successful Throw Every single throw in Judo, from the simplest trip to the most complex sacrifice throw, consists of three distinct phases. If you skip one, the throw will either fail or require excessive strength to complete. 1. Kuzushi (Off-balancing) You have already been introduced to the concept of Kuzushi, but now we apply it as the "engine" of the throw. Kuzushi is the act of breaking the opponent's center of gravity. Think of a person standing upright as a stable pyramid. To tip a pyramid, you must pull or push the top point outside the base. In Judo, we achieve this through: Pulling: Using your grips to lead the opponent's weight onto their toes or heels. Pushing: Driving the opponent's weight in a direction they aren't expecting. Rotation: Turning the opponent so they cannot easily find their footing. The Golden Rule of Kuzushi: You cannot throw someone who is balanced. If you find yourself struggling to lift your partner, stop and check your Kuzushi. Are they leaning? Is their weight shifted? If not, the throw isn't ready. 2. Tsukuri (Entry and Positioning) Once the opponent is off-balance, you must move your body into the correct position to execute the throw. This is called Tsukuri. Tsukuri is the "setup." It involves your footwork and the placement of your hips. The goal is to place your center of gravity lower than your opponent's and to position your body so that you become the axis around which they will rotate. If Kuzushi is the "pull," Tsukuri is the "pivot." 3. Kake (The Execution) Kake is the final phase: the actual delivery of the throw. This is where the momentum created by the Kuzushi and the …

7. Groundwork (Newaza)

The Transition to the Floor Imagine you have just executed a perfect Nage Waza (throwing technique). Your opponent has hit the mat, and the referee hasn't called Ippon. In a real-world scenario or a competitive match, the fight doesn't end the moment the back hits the ground; it simply changes dimensions. This is where Newaza (groundwork) begins. While throwing is about explosive power and timing, Newaza is often a game of patience, leverage, and suffocating control. If throwing is a sprint, groundwork is a chess match played with the entire body. In Judo, the goal of Newaza is generally one of three things: 1. Osaekomi (Pinning): Holding the opponent on their back for a specific duration. 2. Shime-waza (Strangling/Choking): Applying pressure to the neck to force a submission. 3. Kansetsu-waza (Joint Locks): Applying pressure to a joint (primarily the elbow) to force a submission. For a beginner, the priority is always Control. You cannot safely apply a submission if your opponent is free to move, and you cannot maintain a pin if you do not understand the physics of the floor. Principles of Ground Control Before learning specific moves, you must understand the "laws" of the ground. Controlling an opponent on the mat differs fundamentally from standing because you no longer have the benefit of your feet for balance. Instead, you use three primary tools: 1. Weight Distribution In standing Judo, you move your center of gravity to create Kuzushi. In Newaza, you use your weight to "kill" the opponent's movement. This is often called "heavy pressure." Instead of resting your weight on your own joints, you distribute it across the opponent's chest, hips, or shoulders. If you leave a gap of air between your body and theirs, you have given them a space to move, shrimp, or bridge. 2. Point of Contact The more points of contact you have with your opponent, the more control you possess. If you only hold one arm, they can rotate their body. If you hold an arm, control their hip with your leg, and press your chest against theirs, they are effectively immobilized. 3. The Concept of "The Box" Think of your opponent's body as a series of boxes: the head/shoulder box, the hip box, and the leg box. To control a person, you must "pin" at least two of these boxes. For example, if you control the shoulders but leave the hips free, the opponent can simply roll away from you. Fundamental Ground Positions Before you can pin someone, you need to know where you are. 8. Randori (Free Practice)

From Drill to Dance: What is Randori? Imagine you have spent weeks practicing a single throw. You know exactly where to place your feet, how to rotate your hips, and where to guide your partner. In a controlled drill, your partner (the uke) is cooperating—they are moving exactly where you want them to move. It feels seamless. Then, for the first time, you step into Randori. You reach for a grip, but your partner pulls their arm away. You attempt to step in for a throw, but they shift their weight, leaving you stumbling forward. Suddenly, the "perfect" technique you practiced in drills feels impossible to execute. This is the most critical transition in a beginner's Judo journey. Randori is the Japanese term for "free practice." While drills teach you the mechanics of a technique, Randori teaches you the application. It is the bridge between knowing how a move works in theory and knowing how to make it work against a resisting human being. The Philosophy and Objectives of Free Practice In earlier chapters, we discussed Seiryoku Zenyo (Maximum Efficiency). In drills, efficiency is easy because there is no resistance. In Randori, you are forced to actually apply this principle. If you try to force a throw through raw strength, you will tire out in two minutes. To succeed in Randori, you must stop "fighting" your partner and start "listening" to their movement. The primary objectives of Randori for a beginner are: Developing Reactivity: Learning to respond to an opponent's movement in real-time rather than following a pre-set script. Timing and Distance: Understanding the precise moment a partner is off-balance (Kuzushi) and the exact distance needed to execute a throw. Integration: Learning how to transition smoothly from standing throws (Nage Waza) into groundwork (Newaza) without a pause in action. Stress Testing: Discovering which of your techniques are reliable and which ones require more refinement in your basic drills. The Rules of Engagement: Safety and Etiquette Because Randori involves resisting an opponent, the risk of injury increases. To keep the environment safe, the Dojo follows strict guidelines. The "Partner First" Mindset Randori is not a fight to win; it is a shared study session. If you "win" every exchange by using overwhelming strength, you aren't practicing Judo—you are wrestling. Conversely, if you are too passive, your partner cannot learn. The goal is mutual growth (Jita Kyoei). Safety Constraints for Beginners As a beginner, you must adhere to these safety boundaries: 1. No "Death Grips": Avoid gripping the fabric of the Gi so tightly that your partner cannot move or breathe. 2. Controlled Execution: Even in free practice, you should not "slam" your partner. 9. Competition Rules and Strategies

The Moment of the Match Imagine you are standing on the edge of the competition mat. Your heart is racing, your judogi is tied tight, and you are facing an opponent you’ve never met. The referee calls "Hajime!" (Begin!). Suddenly, the relaxed atmosphere of Randori vanishes. Every grip you fight for, every step you take, and every attempt at Kuzushi now carries a specific value. In a practice session, you might let a partner throw you just to help them learn. In a competition, the goal shifts: you are seeking a decisive victory while avoiding the same fate. To do this, you need to understand not just how to throw, but how the game is scored and how to navigate the strategic "chess match" of a Judo bout. The Scoring System Judo competitions are designed to reward efficiency and control. The ultimate goal is to achieve an Ippon, which ends the match immediately. If an Ippon is not achieved, the match is decided by lower scores or a decision by the referee. Ippon (The Full Point) An Ippon is the highest score in Judo. It represents a "perfect" execution of a technique. Think of it as a knockout in boxing; once it happens, the match is over. You can achieve Ippon in three primary ways: 1. The Throw: To score Ippon with a throw, the opponent must be thrown onto their back with force, speed, and control. If the throw lacks any of these three elements, it may only be a Waza-ari. 2. The Pin (Osaekomi): If you hold your opponent on their back in a controlled position (as learned in Newaza) for 20 seconds, you score Ippon. 3. Submission: If you force your opponent to submit via a choke or an armlock, it is an automatic Ippon. Waza-ari (The Half Point) A Waza-ari is awarded when a technique is successful but lacks one of the elements required for Ippon. For example: A throw where the opponent lands on their side rather than their back. A throw that has control and speed, but lacks enough force. A pin (Osaekomi) that lasts at least 10 seconds but less than 20 seconds. Crucially: Two Waza-ari equal one Ippon. If you score two Waza-ari, you win the match immediately (Waza-ari-awasete-ippon). The Hierarchy of Victory If the clock runs out and neither player has an Ippon, the winner is the person with the most Waza-ari. If the scores are tied, the match may go into "Golden Score" (overtime), where the first person to score any point—or the first person to receive a penalty—determines the winner. 10. Conditioning and Flexibility for Judo

The "Gas Tank" and the Rubber Band Imagine you are in the middle of Randori. You’ve successfully executed a throw, but the transition to Newaza (groundwork) has left you breathless. Your opponent is fighting for a grip, and while you know exactly which technique to use, your arms feel like lead and your hips feel stiff. Suddenly, a move that felt effortless during a slow-motion drill becomes impossible because your body cannot keep up with your mind. In Judo, technique is the engine, but conditioning is the fuel. You can have the most precise Kuzushi (off-balancing) in the world, but if you lack the explosive strength to finish the throw or the flexibility to avoid an injury during a fall, your technical knowledge is limited by your physical capacity. Conditioning for Judo is not about becoming a bodybuilder or a marathon runner. It is about developing functional fitness: the ability to apply strength, balance, and endurance in the specific, chaotic movements of a grappling match. The Pillars of Judo Conditioning To build a body capable of handling the rigors of the Dojo, you need to focus on four primary physical pillars. 1. Functional Strength Judo requires a mix of "pulling" strength (to control your opponent's gi) and "core" stability (to maintain your balance while being pushed). Pulling Strength: Since most Grips and Hand Positions involve pulling the opponent toward you or breaking their posture, you need strong lats, biceps, and grip strength. Core Stability: Your core is the bridge between your legs and your arms. Without a stable core, the power from your legs cannot reach your hands, making your throws feel weak. Leg Power: Explosive power in the legs is what allows you to drive into a throw or pivot quickly during Fundamental Stances and Footwork. 2. Flexibility and Mobility There is a critical difference between flexibility and mobility. Flexibility is the ability of a muscle to stretch (passive). Mobility is the ability to move a joint through its full range of motion under control (active). In Judo, mobility is king. You need: Hip Mobility: To enter deep into throws without losing balance. Shoulder Mobility: To maintain strong grips and absorb the impact of Ukemi (breaking falls). Spinal Flexibility: To twist and rotate during groundwork without straining your lower back. 3. Cardiovascular Endurance Judo is an interval-based sport. You may have moments of low intensity followed by ten seconds of maximum exertion. This requires two types of endurance: Aerobic Capacity: The ability to keep moving over a long period (stamina). 11. Advanced Techniques and Variations

The Wall: When Basic Throws Stop Working Imagine you are in a Randori session. You’ve spent weeks perfecting your basic hip throws. You see an opening, you execute your Kuzushi (off-balancing), and you step in for the throw. But this time, your partner is experienced. The moment they feel you stepping in, they shift their weight, stiffen their posture, or step back just an inch. Suddenly, your "perfect" technique fails. You are left off-balance, staring at the mat, while your partner smiles. This is the "Wall" every beginner hits. In the beginning, you learn the "textbook" version of a throw—the version where the opponent stands exactly where they are supposed to. But real humans don't stand still. To move past this wall, you must stop thinking of Judo as a series of isolated moves and start seeing it as a conversation. Advanced Judo isn't necessarily about more complex movements; it is about variations (adjusting a move to fit the situation) and combinations (chaining moves together to bypass a defense). Mastering the Sweeping Hip Throw: Harai Goshi Until now, you have likely focused on throws where you pull the opponent over your hip. Harai Goshi (Sweeping Hip Throw) takes this a step further by adding a dynamic leg sweep to the rotation. While a standard hip throw relies primarily on the pivot and pull, Harai Goshi uses the leg to physically remove the opponent's base, making it much harder for them to resist. The Mechanics of Harai Goshi To execute Harai Goshi, follow these steps: 1. The Entry: Use the Grips and Hand Positions you've practiced to pull your opponent forward and slightly to the side. You want their weight on the balls of their feet. 2. The Pivot: Rotate your body, stepping across the opponent's midline. Your hip should be positioned slightly in front of theirs. 3. The Sweep: As you rotate and pull the opponent over your hip, you don't just let them fall. You swing your outer leg in a powerful, outward arc, "sweeping" the back of the opponent's thigh. 4. The Finish: Use your upper body to guide the opponent's momentum downward, ensuring they land flat on their back for an Ippon. Why Harai Goshi is "Advanced" Harai Goshi requires a higher level of coordination than basic throws because you are managing two different movements simultaneously: the rotation of your torso and the sweeping motion of your leg. If your timing is off, you will either miss the sweep or lose your own balance. 12. Mental Training and Focus

The Invisible Opponent: The Battle Within Imagine you are standing on the mat for your first competitive match or a high-intensity session of Randori. Your heart is hammering against your ribs, your palms are sweating, and your mind is racing. You know the Basic Throwing Techniques and you’ve practiced your Ukemi, but suddenly, your muscles feel stiff, and your thoughts are scattered. You find yourself gripping too tightly, forgetting to use your partner's momentum, and reacting with panic rather than precision. In this moment, your primary opponent isn't the person standing across from you—it is your own nervous system. Judo is often described as "physical chess." While the physical skills (the Waza) are essential, the mental framework that governs how you apply those skills is what separates a frantic struggle from a controlled execution. Mental training is the process of teaching your brain to remain calm, observant, and decisive under pressure. Managing the Stress Response When we enter a high-pressure situation, our bodies trigger the "fight-or-flight" response. This is a biological survival mechanism that floods the body with adrenaline. For a beginner, this often manifests as "tunnel vision" (losing track of the overall situation) or "muscle bracing" (tensing up), which directly contradicts the principle of Seiryoku Zenyo (Maximum Efficiency). The Trap of Over-Tension When you are nervous, your natural instinct is to grip your opponent as hard as possible and stiffen your posture. However, a stiff body is a predictable body. If you are tense, you cannot react fluidly to your opponent's movements, and you become easier to throw. To combat this, you must practice conscious relaxation. This doesn't mean being limp; it means maintaining "relaxed alertness." You want your muscles to be supple enough to move instantly, but focused enough to execute a technique. Breathing as an Anchor The fastest way to communicate to your brain that you are safe and in control is through your breath. When we panic, we take shallow breaths from the chest, which signals the brain to stay in a state of alarm. The Box Breathing Technique: This is a simple tool used by athletes and high-stress professionals to reset their nervous system: 1. Inhale slowly through your nose for 4 seconds. 2. Hold the breath for 4 seconds. 3. Exhale slowly through your mouth for 4 seconds. 4. Hold the empty lungs for 4 seconds. Practice this during the warm-up or in the brief moments between rounds of Randori. By controlling the breath, you lower your heart rate and clear the mental fog, allowing you to return to the precision required for an Ippon.
