1. Introduction to Human Resources

What Is Human Resources? Meet Alex. Alex recently applied for a marketing coordinator position at a mid-sized tech company. After hitting "submit" on the application portal, Alex received an automated email confirming receipt. A week later, a human being named Sarah reached out to schedule a phone interview. When Alex arrived at the office for the final round, Sarah greeted him at the front desk, guided him through the necessary paperwork, and explained the company's benefits package when an offer was eventually made. Sarah did not work in marketing, and she did not design the software the company sold. Sarah worked in Human Resources. Without her, the company would not have been able to find, hire, integrate, or retain Alex. At its most basic level, Human Resources (HR) is the department within an organization that manages its human capital—the people. While other departments manage money (Finance), products (Operations), or sales (Marketing), HR manages the employee lifecycle from the moment a job is posted to the day an employee retires or moves on. The Primary Role of HR in an Organization Historically, HR was referred to as the "personnel department." Decades ago, this department primarily handled administrative tasks: processing payroll, filing employee records, and ensuring the company followed basic labor laws. While these tasks are still part of the modern HR landscape, the field has evolved dramatically. Today, the primary role of HR is to act as a strategic bridge between the organization's business goals and its people. If a company wants to double its revenue in the next year, it needs to double its workforce—or train its current workforce to be twice as productive. HR is the department that figures out how to acquire that talent, develop the necessary skills, and keep employees motivated enough to achieve the goal. The functions of modern HR generally fall into three main categories: 1. Administrative and Operational: The day-to-day management of the workforce. This includes processing payroll, managing employee benefits (like health insurance and retirement plans), maintaining employee records, and ensuring the physical workspace is safe and functional. 2. Employee Relations: Managing the relationship between the employer and the employee. This involves resolving workplace conflicts, investigating harassment complaints, ensuring fair treatment, and fostering a positive company culture. 3. Strategic: Looking ahead to ensure the company is prepared for future challenges. This includes workforce planning (predicting how many people the company will need in five years), designing training programs to upskill current staff, and developing compensation structures that keep the company competitive in the job market. 2. Education and Entry-Level Certifications

The Educational Foundation: Which Degrees Matter Most? Meet Alex and Jordan. Both are applying for the same HR Coordinator position. Alex has a bachelor’s degree in Communications and spent the last year running social media for a campus organization. Jordan has a bachelor’s degree in Human Resource Management, but no formal work experience. When the hiring manager reviews the resumes, who gets the interview? The answer might surprise you: it could easily be either of them. In the modern HR job market, a specific degree is rarely a strict requirement for entry-level roles, but your educational background shapes how quickly you grasp core concepts and how competitive you are against other candidates. Let’s look at the most common degrees that feed into HR careers and how they position you in the job market. The Direct Route: Human Resources Management A degree in Human Resources Management (sometimes called Human Resource Administration) is the most direct path into the profession. These programs are specifically designed to teach the fundamentals of the HR lifecycle. If you pursue this degree, your coursework will typically cover: Employment law and regulations Compensation and benefits structures Organizational behavior and workplace psychology Talent acquisition and training fundamentals Why it helps: As an HR Assistant or HR Coordinator, you will be expected to understand basic HR terminology from day one. A specialized degree gives you a built-in vocabulary and a framework for understanding why certain policies exist, reducing the learning curve in your first job. The Closely Related Fields: Business and Psychology Because HR is a business function focused on human behavior, degrees in Business and Psychology are highly valued by hiring managers. Business Administration or Management: A general business degree teaches you how an organization operates as a whole. Because HR must align with broader company goals (a concept known as Strategic HR), understanding finance, operations, and management principles makes you a more business-minded HR professional. Many business programs also allow you to concentrate or minor in HR. Psychology or Sociology: Degrees in these fields focus on human behavior, motivation, and group dynamics. These are incredibly useful skills for roles leaning heavily toward Employee Relations, Training and Development, or Recruiting. A psychology major understands how to assess candidate behavior during interviews or how to design training modules that actually engage adult learners. The Wildcards: Liberal Arts and Beyond Remember Alex from our opening scenario? Degrees in Communications, English, Public Relations, or even History are surprisingly common in HR. Why? Because entry-level HR is highly administrative and communication-heavy. 3. Core HR Concepts and Terminology

Picture an HR Coordinator sitting at their desk on a Tuesday morning. Within the span of two hours, they field a phone call from a manager asking if they can legally fire an employee without warning, receive an email from a new hire asking how to enroll in the health plan, and log into a software system to review the 150 resumes submitted for an open junior accountant role. To an outside observer, these tasks might seem completely unrelated. But to an HR professional, they are all part of a single, interconnected system. They represent the employee lifecycle, the legal framework of employment, and the daily vocabulary of the trade. In previous chapters, we explored what Human Resources is and the various roles you can pursue, from HR Generalist to Recruiting Coordinator. But before you can step into any of those roles, you need to speak the language. Every industry has its jargon, and HR is no exception. Mastering this vocabulary is about understanding the mechanics of how businesses manage their people. The Employee Lifecycle The employee lifecycle is the chronological journey of a worker within an organization. As an HR professional, your daily tasks will almost always map back to one of these stages. Understanding this lifecycle helps you see the "big picture" of how your role—whether you are focused on recruiting, benefits, or employee relations—fits into the broader organization. 1. Recruitment and Selection This is the phase where a company identifies a need for talent and brings someone in to fill it. If you are stepping into a Recruiting Coordinator role, this will be your primary focus. During this phase, you will frequently encounter the term ATS, which stands for Applicant Tracking System. An ATS is a software application used by HR departments to manage the end-to-end hiring process. Instead of sorting through hundreds of emails with attached resumes, an HR professional uses an ATS to post jobs to multiple boards, collect applications, filter candidates based on keywords, and move applicants through interview stages. Popular ATS platforms include Workday, Greenhouse, and Lever. When you hear someone say, "I updated the candidate's profile in the ATS," they simply mean they logged a note or changed the applicant's status in the hiring software. 2. Onboarding Once a candidate accepts a job offer, they transition into the onboarding phase. While often confused with orientation (which is typically a one-day event focused on filling out paperwork and touring the office), onboarding is a comprehensive, multi-week process designed to integrate the new employee into the company’s culture, tools, and workflows. 4. Gaining Relevant Experience

The Experience Paradox You’ve almost certainly encountered it before, whether you were looking for your very first job or trying to pivot into a new field: every job posting asks for experience, but to get experience, someone has to hire you. In Human Resources, this paradox can feel especially frustrating. Because HR professionals handle sensitive employee data, enforce company policies, and manage complex interpersonal issues, hiring managers are often risk-averse. They want proof that you can handle these responsibilities before they hand you the keys. So, how do you prove you can do the job without already having the job? The answer lies in understanding that "HR experience" does not exclusively mean having the job title of an HR Assistant or HR Coordinator. Experience is built by demonstrating HR competencies—the actual skills and tasks that make HR function. If you can show that you have resolved conflicts, organized complex data, or trained a coworker, you already have the foundation of HR experience. This chapter breaks down how to build practical HR experience by mining your past for transferable skills, strategically taking on HR-adjacent tasks in your current role, and finding internships or volunteer opportunities to bridge the gap. Identifying Your Transferable Skills When reviewing your resume, an HR hiring manager is looking for evidence that you can perform the core duties of the role. If you are coming from a customer service, administrative, or retail background, you have likely been performing tasks that are structurally identical to HR functions. The key is learning how to translate your previous experience into HR language. Let’s look at how skills from common non-HR roles map directly to the HR field. From Customer Service to Employee Relations Employee Relations is a core pillar of HR, focusing on maintaining positive employer-employee relationships and resolving workplace issues. Customer service is, fundamentally, employee relations with a different audience. Instead of managing relationships with external customers, you will be managing relationships with internal employees. If you have worked in customer service, you already possess highly refined skills in: De-escalation: Calming down frustrated individuals and guiding them toward a solution. In HR, this is used daily when handling employee grievances or mediating conflicts between coworkers. Active Listening: Ensuring someone feels heard before offering a solution. This is critical when conducting exit interviews or investigating workplace complaints. Navigating Policy: Explaining company policies (like return policies or warranty terms) to customers. In HR, you will explain employee handbooks, benefits policies, and compliance guidelines to staff. 5. Crafting an HR-Specific Resume and Cover Letter

The First Filter: Making It Past the Bots Imagine a busy HR Director with a stack of 300 applications for a single open HR Coordinator position. They do not have time to read every word of every resume. Instead, they spend about six seconds skimming each one for specific skills and keywords. Now, imagine that before the human even sees the stack, a software program has already scanned all 300 applications and automatically thrown 200 of them in the digital trash. This software is called an Applicant Tracking System (ATS). An ATS is a tool used by employers to collect, sort, and filter job applications based on specific keywords and formatting. If your resume is not built to pass through this system, your application will never reach human eyes, no matter how qualified you are. To land a job in Human Resources, your application materials must do two things simultaneously: they must speak the algorithmic language of the ATS, and they must compellingly demonstrate your human-centric HR skills to the hiring manager. In previous chapters, we explored HR concepts, education, and how to gain relevant experience. Now, we will translate those elements into a polished, targeted resume and cover letter. Structuring Your HR Resume A resume is a strategic marketing document, not an exhaustive autobiography. For entry-level HR professionals, the goal is to structure the document so that core HR competencies—like communication, confidentiality, and organization—are impossible to miss. A standard, ATS-friendly HR resume should include the following sections in this order: 1. Contact Information and Professional Summary Keep your contact information clean and professional. Include your name, phone number, email address, and a link to your LinkedIn profile. Avoid including unnecessary personal details like a photo or your full home address. Below your contact information, include a Professional Summary. This is a 3-to-4 sentence paragraph at the top of your resume that highlights your background, key skills, and career goals. Scenario: Sarah has spent three years working as an Administrative Assistant. Through this role, she managed employee files, helped onboard new hires, and completed an HR certification. She is now applying for an HR Assistant role. Certified Professional in Human Resources (PHR) with a proven ability to handle sensitive information with strict confidentiality. Seeking to leverage strong organizational and communication skills as an HR Assistant to support seamless employee lifecycle operations." 2. Core Competencies (Skills Section) Immediately following your summary, include a "Core Competencies" or "Key Skills" section. This is a bulleted list of six to ten skills. 6. Building a Professional HR Network

The Hidden HR Job Market Imagine two candidates applying for the same HR Coordinator position. Both have similar educational backgrounds, both recently earned their entry-level certifications, and both have crafted strong, HR-specific resumes. The first candidate applies exclusively through online job boards, submitting hundreds of applications into what often feels like a black hole. The second candidate applies to far fewer openings, but has spent the last three months having coffee with HR professionals, attending local industry meetings, and engaging with HR groups online. When an unexpected opening pops up, the second candidate gets a direct message from an HR Manager saying, "We have a sudden need for a coordinator. We haven't posted the job publicly yet, but based on our conversations, I think you'd be a great fit. Can we chat?" This scenario illustrates the hidden job market—the large percentage of job openings that are never advertised to the general public. In Human Resources, the hidden job market is particularly active. Because HR professionals are the very people who manage company hiring, they often rely on their own professional circles to fill vacancies within their departments. It is faster, cheaper, and safer to hire someone who comes recommended by a trusted peer. Networking can feel intimidating, especially if you are transitioning into HR from another field and feel like you don't know anyone yet. However, networking in HR is simply the process of building relationships with peers who share your professional interests. It is not about asking strangers for a job; it is about learning from others, sharing your own goals, and positioning yourself to be remembered when opportunities arise. Leveraging Professional HR Associations One of the fastest ways to plug into the HR community is by engaging with professional associations. A professional association is an organization dedicated to supporting a specific profession by offering resources, education, and networking opportunities to its members. The Role of SHRM In the United States, the most prominent HR association is the Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM). SHRM is a global organization that provides education, certification, and advocacy for HR professionals at every stage of their careers. As someone learning how to get into HR, SHRM offers several distinct advantages: Local Chapters: SHRM is divided into local chapters that hold regular in-person and virtual meetings. Attending a local chapter meeting puts you in the same room as HR Generalists, Specialists, and HR Managers from businesses in your immediate area. Student Memberships: If you are currently a student, SHRM offers heavily discounted student memberships, giving you access to the same resources as working professionals at a fraction of the cost. 7. Navigating the HR Job Market

Where to Actually Find HR Jobs You have built a foundation in HR concepts, earned relevant certifications, crafted a targeted resume, and started growing your professional network. Now comes the part that trips up many aspiring HR professionals: figuring out where to look for that crucial first job. If your strategy is to type "HR jobs" into a single search engine and scroll, you will quickly drown in a sea of listings—many of which are too senior, too vague, or outright scams. To find an entry-level HR position, you need to know where to look and how to filter out the noise. The Major Job Boards: Casting a Wide Net General job boards are the most common starting point. They aggregate (collect) thousands of postings from companies of all sizes. While they are highly competitive because of their massive user base, they are essential for getting a lay of the land. LinkedIn: More than just a job board, LinkedIn allows you to apply for jobs directly using your profile. Because you already started building your network there, you can often see if you have mutual connections at a company hiring for an HR role. Indeed: As one of the largest job search engines, Indeed pulls postings from company websites and allows employers to post directly. It is an excellent place to gauge the volume of HR openings in your local area. Glassdoor: Best known for company reviews and salary data, Glassdoor also features a robust job board. It is particularly useful because you can read the job posting and immediately look at what current and former employees say about the company culture. A tip for general boards: Set up automated job alerts. Instead of checking Indeed every day, you can create a search for "HR Assistant" within a 10-mile radius and have the site email you new postings daily. This saves time and ensures you are among the first to apply. Niche HR Websites: Targeting the Right Employers General boards are broad. To find employers who are specifically invested in hiring quality HR professionals, you should turn to niche websites. These are platforms dedicated entirely to human resources. SHRM’s HR Jobs: Operated by the Society for Human Resource Management, this board is highly trusted. Employers who post here are actively looking for candidates who understand HR. As a beginner, this is a prime place to look for entry-level roles. Hire Heroes: Another dedicated HR job board that features roles ranging from entry-level to executive. Association websites: If you belong to a local HR chapter or a specialized group, check their websites. 8. Acing the HR Interview

You sit across the desk from the hiring manager. The interview has been going well. You’ve successfully discussed your HR Assistant certification, walked through your carefully tailored resume, and demonstrated a solid grasp of core HR terminology. Then, the hiring manager leans forward, folds their hands, and asks: "Tell me about a time you had to deal with a conflict between two coworkers." If you are like most people, your mind suddenly goes blank, or you launch into a rambling story that loses its point halfway through. In human resources, behavioral questions like this are the true test. Because HR professionals deal with people, policies, and sensitive information daily, employers need to know exactly how you have handled these situations in the past. They don't just want to know what you know; they want to know how you act. The STAR Method: Your Blueprint for Behavioral Answers When interviewers ask behavioral questions—questions that usually start with "Tell me about a time..." or "Give me an example of..."—they are looking for a structured story. The most effective way to deliver this is by using the STAR method. STAR is an acronym that stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. It provides a framework to ensure your answer is comprehensive, focused, and easy for the interviewer to follow. Situation: Set the scene. Briefly describe the context, the who, what, where, and when. Keep this concise—do not get bogged down in unnecessary backstory. Task: Explain your specific responsibility or the challenge you needed to overcome. What was the goal? What was the problem? Action: This is the most important part. Describe the specific steps you took to address the task. Use "I" statements rather than "we" statements so the interviewer understands your individual contribution. Result: Share the outcome. What happened because of your actions? Whenever possible, quantify the result (e.g., "reduced processing time by 15%" or "retained both employees"). Then, briefly state what you learned. Applying STAR to an HR Scenario Let’s look at how the STAR method works in practice. Suppose the interviewer asks: "Tell me about a time you had to enforce a policy that was unpopular with employees." 9. Evaluating Offers and Starting Your HR Career

You have navigated the HR job market, submitted tailored applications, and successfully aced your interviews. The hiring manager shakes your hand and says, "We'd love to have you on the team." A few days later, an email arrives with the subject line: "Offer Letter Attached." After months of hard work, it is incredibly tempting to immediately sign the document and celebrate. However, as an aspiring HR professional, this is the moment where your new career truly begins. Evaluating an offer and transitioning into your new role requires the same analytical and interpersonal skills you will soon be applying every day at work. Decoding the Job Offer A formal job offer is more than just a salary number; it is a comprehensive package that outlines how an organization will compensate and support you. Before you can evaluate an offer, you need to understand the individual components that make up a standard compensation package—the total combination of pay and benefits an employer provides to an employee in exchange for their work. Base Salary Your base salary is the initial, guaranteed amount of money you will earn per year, before any taxes or deductions are taken out. When evaluating an offer, compare this number to the market rate for your specific area and the entry-level role in question. A Recruiter or HR Generalist in a major metropolitan area will likely have a different base salary baseline than someone in a rural setting. Benefits Benefits are non-wage compensations provided to employees. While they don't show up directly in your paycheck, they have significant financial value. Common benefits include: Health Insurance: Look at premiums (what you pay monthly), deductibles (what you pay before insurance kicks in), and co-pays. An employer covering 100% of your health premiums is a massive financial benefit compared to one where you pay $300 a month out of pocket. Retirement Plans: Many employers offer a 401(k) or similar retirement savings plan. Pay close attention to the employer match—this is when a company contributes a certain amount to your retirement fund based on how much you contribute. For example, a 3% match means if you put in 3% of your salary, the company will also put in 3%. This is essentially free money. Additional Perks: Look for life insurance, disability insurance, tuition reimbursement, or wellness stipends. Paid Time Off (PTO) Paid Time Off (PTO) is the time you are allowed to take away from work while still receiving your regular pay. PTO structures vary wildly by company: Traditional: Separate banks for vacation, sick leave, and personal days. Banked PTO: One single pool of days you can use for any reason.
