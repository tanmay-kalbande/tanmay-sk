1. Advanced Diatonic Harmony

The "Color" of the Seventh Imagine you are playing a standard C Major to F Major progression (I - IV). It sounds clean, stable, and functional—but it also sounds like a nursery rhyme. Now, imagine adding a B to that C chord (making it a Cmaj7) and an E to that F chord (making it an Fmaj7). Suddenly, the atmosphere shifts. The sound is no longer just "stable"; it is lush, sophisticated, and evocative. This is the power of the 7th. While triads provide the structural foundation of a piece, 7th chords provide the emotional texture. In the world of professional piano playing, triads are the skeleton, but 7th chords are the flesh and blood. To move from an intermediate to an advanced harmonic vocabulary, you must stop thinking of chords as three-note blocks and start seeing them as four-note colors. Constructing the Diatonic 7ths In a major key, every note of the scale can serve as a root. When we stack thirds on top of these roots, we naturally create four distinct types of 7th chords. Because these chords are built exclusively from notes within the parent scale, they are called diatonic. The Major 7th (Maj7) The Major 7th chord consists of a Root, Major 3rd, Perfect 5th, and Major 7th. Character: Ethereal, jazzy, stable but "yearning." Diatonic Occurrence: Found on the I and IV degrees of the major scale. Example (Key of C): Cmaj7 (C - E - G - B) and Fmaj7 (F - A - C - E). The Dominant 7th (7) The Dominant 7th consists of a Root, Major 3rd, Perfect 5th, and Minor 7th (flat 7). Character: Tense, driving, demanding resolution. Diatonic Occurrence: Found on the V degree. Example (Key of C): G7 (G - B - D - F). The Minor 7th (m7) The Minor 7th consists of a Root, Minor 3rd, Perfect 5th, and Minor 7th. Character: Mellow, moody, sophisticated. Diatonic Occurrence: Found on the ii, iii, and vi degrees. Example (Key of C): Dm7 (D - F - A - C), Em7 (E - G - B - D), and Am7 (A - C - E - G). The Half-Diminished 7th (m7♭5) The Half-Diminished chord consists of a Root, Minor 3rd, Diminished 5th (flat 5), and Minor 7th. Character: Unstable, dark, leaning heavily toward resolution. Diatonic Occurrence: Found on the vii° degree. Example (Key of C): Bm7♭5 (B - D - F - A). --- The Functional Map of 7th Chords Understanding the construction is the "how," but understanding function is the "why." 2. Chord Inversions and Voice Leading

The "Jump" Problem: Why Inversions Matter Imagine you are playing a standard ii - V - I progression in C Major. You play a iim7 (D-F-A-C) in root position. To move to the V7 (G-B-D-F), your hand leaps down a fourth. Then, to resolve to the Imaj7 (C-E-G-B), you leap back up. To the listener, the harmony is correct, but the performance sounds "blocky" and mechanical. Your hand is traveling unnecessary distances, creating gaps in the sound and increasing the risk of missing a note during fast tempos. The secret to professional-sounding piano playing isn't knowing which chords to play—you've already mastered that in Advanced Diatonic Harmony—but knowing where to place the notes of those chords. This is the essence of Voice Leading: the art of moving each individual note (voice) of a chord to the nearest available note in the next chord. Mastering 7th Chord Inversions To eliminate the "jump," we must move beyond root position. An inversion occurs when any note other than the root is the lowest note of the chord. For 7th chords, we have three primary inversions. The Anatomy of 7th Chord Inversions Using a V7 (G-B-D-F) as our model: 1. Root Position: Root is in the bass (G). 2. 1st Inversion: 3rd is in the bass (B). The voicing becomes B-D-F-G. 3. 2nd Inversion: 5th is in the bass (D). The voicing becomes D-F-G-B. 4. 3rd Inversion: 7th is in the bass (F). The voicing becomes F-G-B-D. Application Across 7th Types While the root is the anchor, the "character" of the chord is maintained regardless of the inversion. Apply this logic to all types introduced previously: Major 7th (Imaj7): Focus on the 1st inversion (3rd in bass) to create a softer, less "final" tonic sound. Dominant 7th (V7): The 3rd inversion (7th in bass) is particularly powerful for creating strong pull toward the tonic. Minor 7th (iim7, vim7): 2nd inversions are often used as "connectors" when moving toward a dominant chord. Half-Diminished (iiø7): Because of the unstable tritone, 1st and 2nd inversions help smooth the transition into the V7. The Principle of Common Tones The most efficient way to transition between chords is to identify Common Tones—notes that exist in both the current chord and the target chord. The Golden Rule of Voice Leading: If a note is shared between two chords, do not move the finger playing that note. Keep it stationary. Example: iim7 $\rightarrow$ V7 in C Major iim7: D - F - A - C V7: G - B - D - F Common Tones: D and F. 3. Extended Harmony: 9ths, 11ths, and 13ths

The "Color" Gap: Why 7ths Aren't Enough Imagine you are painting a landscape. You have your primary colors (the triads) and your secondary blends (the 7th chords). You can create a functional image—the viewer knows it’s a mountain and a lake—but the image feels "flat." It lacks the atmospheric haze of a morning fog or the shimmering gold of a sunset. In music, 7th chords provide the structural foundation of harmony, but extensions—the 9th, 11th, and 13th—provide the atmosphere. When you hear a professional jazz pianist or a sophisticated neo-soul track, they aren't just playing a V7 or a Imaj7. They are adding "color tones" that soften the edges of the harmony or heighten the tension before a resolution. Extensions are simply the notes of the scale that exist above the 7th. By stacking thirds beyond the octave, we move from basic functional harmony into the realm of emotional nuance. Anatomy of the Extensions To build an extended chord, you must first have a complete 7th chord. Extensions are additive; you cannot have a 13th chord without the 7th, 9th, and 11th being implied or present. The 9th (The Sophisticate) The 9th is the second note of the scale, transposed up an octave. Construction: Root $\rightarrow$ 3rd $\rightarrow$ 5th $\rightarrow$ 7th $\rightarrow$ 9th. Emotional Quality: Open, airy, and polished. On a Imaj7, it adds a sense of luxury. On a V7, it adds a "yearning" quality that pushes the resolution forward. Example (Key of C): For a Cmaj9, you play C-E-G-B-D. The 11th (The Ambiguous) The 11th is the fourth note of the scale, transposed up an octave. Construction: Root $\rightarrow$ 3rd $\rightarrow$ 5th $\rightarrow$ 7th $\rightarrow$ 9th $\rightarrow$ 11th. Emotional Quality: Complex and often unstable. In major chords, the natural 11th clashes with the 3rd (creating a minor 9th interval), which is why it is most commonly used in m11 chords or as a "sharp 11" (11) in dominant chords. Example (Key of C): For a Dm11 (the iim7 in C), you play D-F-A-C-E-G. The 13th (The Full Spectrum) The 13th is the sixth note of the scale, transposed up an octave. Construction: Root $\rightarrow$ 3rd $\rightarrow$ 5th $\rightarrow$ 7th $\rightarrow$ 9th $\rightarrow$ 11th $\rightarrow$ 13th. Emotional Quality: Rich, lush, and "complete." It often feels like the final piece of the puzzle, providing a wide sonic palette. Example (Key of C): For a G13 (the V7 in C), you play G-B-D-F-A-C-E. --- Additive vs. Altered Extensions Not all extensions are created equal. Some follow the diatonic scale (Additive), while others are intentionally shifted to create more "gravity" or tension (Altered). 4. Secondary Dominants and Related II-Vs

The "Wrong" Chord That Sounds Right Imagine you are playing a standard I - IV - I progression in C Major: Cmaj7 $\rightarrow$ Fmaj7 $\rightarrow$ Cmaj7. It’s stable, diatonic, and perhaps a bit predictable. Now, try inserting a C7 right before the Fmaj7. Suddenly, the C7 feels like it has a "mission." It creates a magnetic pull that forces the ear to crave the Fmaj7. Even though C7 isn't a diatonic chord in C Major (which requires a Cmaj7), it sounds perfectly natural. Why? Because for a brief moment, you’ve tricked the listener’s ear into believing that Fmaj7 is the temporary "home" (the tonic), and the C7 is the Dominant (V7) that leads into it. This is the essence of the Secondary Dominant. It is a harmonic "detour" that allows you to target any diatonic chord other than the tonic with increased intensity and direction. Anatomy of the Secondary Dominant A Secondary Dominant is any dominant 7th chord that is not the primary dominant (V7) of the key, but resolves to a diatonic chord. To construct one, you simply identify the target chord (the chord you want to move toward) and play the Dominant 7th chord located a perfect fifth above it. The Construction Formula 1. Pick a target: Choose any diatonic chord (ii, iii, IV, V, vi, or vii°). 2. Find its V: Go up a perfect fifth (or down a perfect fourth) from the root of that target. 3. Apply the Dominant 7th quality: Build a V7 chord on that root. Example (Key of C): Target: Fmaj7 (the IV chord). Calculation: A perfect fifth above F is C. Result: C7. Notation: This is written as V7/IV (read as "Five-seven of Four"). Common Secondary Dominants in C Major While you can target any chord, some are more common in popular music and jazz than others: V7/ii (A7 $\rightarrow$ Dm7): Adds a sophisticated, slightly melancholic lift before hitting the pre-dominant. V7/V (D7 $\rightarrow$ G7): The most common secondary dominant. It creates a "dominant of the dominant" chain, intensifying the eventual resolution to the tonic. V7/vi (E7 $\rightarrow$ Am7): Creates a strong pull toward the relative minor, often used in emotional bridges or verses. Expanding the Pull: The Related II If a secondary dominant is a "shortcut" to a target chord, the Related II is the scenic route. In Chapter 1, we established the ii - V - I as the strongest cadence in tonal music. The same logic applies to secondary dominants. Since every V7 has a corresponding iim7, we can precede any secondary dominant with its own "related" ii chord to create a Secondary ii-V. 5. Modal Interchange and Borrowed Chords

The "Emotional Pivot" Effect Imagine you are playing a standard ii - V - I progression in C Major: Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7. It sounds clean, resolved, and predictable. Now, imagine replacing that final Cmaj7 with an Fm6 or Fm7 before returning to the tonic. Suddenly, the mood shifts from "bright and settled" to "nostalgic, yearning, or bittersweet." That shift didn't happen because you changed keys—you didn't modulate. You simply "borrowed" a chord from a different version of the same key. This is the essence of Modal Interchange. Modal interchange allows you to step outside the boundaries of Advanced Diatonic Harmony without losing the gravitational pull of your home key. By pulling chords from the Parallel Minor (the minor scale starting on the same root), you introduce colors that are unavailable in the standard major scale, providing a sophisticated emotional palette used extensively in film scoring, jazz, and neo-soul. --- Parallel Minor Borrowing The most common source for modal interchange in a major key is its parallel minor. If you are in C Major, your parallel minor is C Natural Minor (C Aeolian). While the diatonic chords of C Major are built from the C Major scale, the borrowed chords are built from the C Natural Minor scale. The "magic" happens because these two scales share the same tonic (C), but differ in their 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths. 6. Tritone Substitutions and Dominant Logic

The "Hidden" Shared Core of Dominants Imagine you are playing a standard ii-V-I in C Major: Dm7 $\rightarrow$ G7 $\rightarrow$ Cmaj7. The transition from G7 to Cmaj7 is the bedrock of Western harmony. But what if you replaced that G7 with a Db7? Dm7 $\rightarrow$ Db7 $\rightarrow$ Cmaj7 To a beginner, this looks like a mistake—a random chromatic chord sliding into the tonic. To an intermediate player, this is a Tritone Substitution. The result is a sophisticated, "darker" jazz sound where the bass descends chromatically (D $\rightarrow$ Db $\rightarrow$ C) rather than jumping a perfect fifth (G $\rightarrow$ C). The reason this works isn't magic; it is mathematical. The tritone substitution is not a "replacement" of the chord's function, but a replacement of the chord's shell. The Anatomy of the Tritone Every Dominant 7th chord contains a tritone (an augmented fourth/diminished fifth) between its 3rd and 7th. This interval is the "engine" of the chord; it creates the instability that demands resolution to the tonic. In a G7 chord (G, B, D, F): The 3rd is B. The 7th is F. The interval B to F is a tritone. Now, look at a Db7 chord (Db, F, Ab, Cb/B): The 3rd is F. The 7th is Cb (enharmonically B). The interval F to B is the exact same tritone. Because both G7 and Db7 share the same guide tones (B and F), they share the same harmonic "gravity." Your ear hears the tritone and expects it to resolve to the tonic. It doesn't matter which root is supporting that tritone; the resolution remains the same. Executing the bII7 to I Resolution In functional harmony, we refer to the tritone substitute as the bII7 (the lowered second degree of the scale). While the V7 provides a strong, leaping resolution, the bII7 provides a smooth, sliding resolution. Voice Leading Logic The power of the tritone substitution lies in Chord Inversions and Voice Leading. When moving from V7 to I, the bass leaps a fifth. When moving from bII7 to I, the bass moves by a half-step. The Chromatic Descent: 1. Root Motion: The root of the substitute chord (Db) resolves down a half-step to the root of the tonic (C). 2. Internal Motion: The 3rd of the Db7 (F) resolves down to the 5th of the Cmaj7 (E). The 7th of the Db7 (B) stays as the major 7th of the Cmaj7 (B). Piano Execution: The "Side-Slip" To execute this on the piano, avoid jumping your whole hand. 7. Advanced Progression Patterns

The Logic of Momentum: Moving Beyond the ii-V-I Imagine you are composing a piece and you reach the final cadence. You’ve built the tension perfectly, but as you land on the Imaj7, the music stops dead. It feels like a door slamming shut. Now, imagine instead that the V7 doesn't resolve to the tonic, but slides into a surprising chord that keeps the listener leaning forward, forcing the harmony to travel through three other keys before finally coming home. Up to this point, you have mastered the "bricks" of harmony: Secondary Dominants, Modal Interchange, and Tritone Substitutions. However, a house is not just a pile of bricks; it is the architectural plan that determines how a listener moves through a space. Advanced progression patterns are the blueprints. They allow you to move away from static "block" harmony and toward fluid, cinematic movement. The Cycle of Fourths (The Circle of Fifths Progression) While the Circle of Fifths is often taught as a way to find key signatures, in practice, it is the most powerful engine for harmonic momentum. In a progression, we typically move in fourths (the inverse of fifths), as this creates the strongest sense of resolution. The Sequential Chain A "Cycle Progression" occurs when a series of chords follow the same intervalic relationship. Instead of a simple ii-V-I, you create a chain where the resolution of one chord becomes the starting point for the next. The most common application is the Extended Cycle, often found in jazz standards and baroque music. In the key of C Major, a full cycle looks like this: | vi7 | ii7 | V7 | Imaj7 | (Standard) | iii7 | vi7 | ii7 | V7 | Imaj7 | (Extended) The logic here is a sequence of falling fifths (or rising fourths): iii (Em7) resolves to vi (Am7) vi (Am7) resolves to ii (Dm7) ii (Dm7) resolves to V (G7) V (G7) resolves to I (Cmaj7) Application: Creating "The Long Way Home" To implement this, stop thinking of chords as individual destinations and start thinking of them as a conveyor belt. If you find your progression feels too "jumpy," apply the Cycle of Fourths to smooth out the transitions. Scenario: Enhancing a Pop Ballad If your bridge is currently | Fmaj7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |, it feels predictable. To add sophistication, expand it into a cycle: | Am7 (vi) | Dm7 (ii) | G7 (V) | Cmaj7 (I) | By adding the vi, you create a longer arc of tension that makes the final arrival at the tonic feel earned rather than inevitable. 8. Rhythmic Voicing and Comping

Rhythmic Comping: The Missing Pulse in Your Chords Picture this: you’re playing a simple ii-V-I progression in F major—Gm7 | C7 | Fmaj7—but the bandleader leans in and says, “Can you make it swing a little more?” You know the chords, you know the voicings, but the groove feels static, predictable. The rhythm isn’t carrying the harmony forward—it’s just sitting there. That’s the difference between playing chords and comping. Comping isn’t just about which notes you play; it’s about when you play them. A well-placed syncopation can turn a bland chord progression into a driving rhythm section. A ghost note can imply motion without cluttering the harmony. And when your left hand locks in with your right, you’re no longer just a pianist—you’re a groove machine. This chapter isn’t about learning new chords. It’s about making the chords you already know feel alive. We’ll focus on rhythm as the primary tool for expression, turning chord voicings into rhythmic statements that support the soloist, lock in with the drummer, and define the style. --- Syncopation and "Push" Rhythms: The Engine of Groove Syncopation isn’t just for jazz—it’s the secret sauce in pop, R&B, gospel, and even some rock. But in jazz and its related styles, it’s non-negotiable. The trick is to displace the expected beat just enough to create tension and release. The "Push" Concept A "push" rhythm anticipates the next beat by a 16th or 8th note, creating a forward-driving feel. Think of it like leaning into the next step before your foot lands. Example in 4/4: - Straight: Play a chord on beat 1. - Pushed: Play the chord on the "& of 1" (the "and" between beats 1 and 2). - Effect: Instantly makes the harmony feel more urgent. Try this in a ii-V-I in C: The G7 pushed on the "& of 1" creates a forward motion toward the Cmaj7. Syncopated Chord Stabs Instead of playing full chords on every beat, isolate the rhythm by: - Playing stabs (short, percussive chord hits) on off-beats. - Leaving space for the harmony to breathe. Pattern: "Off-Beat 2 & 4" The Gm7 lands on beat 1, C7 on beat 3. The rests on 2 and 4 let the bass and drums breathe while the chords still drive the groove. Jazz Standard Application: Take the opening of "Autumn Leaves" in G minor: Try syncopating the C7 and Fm7: - Gm7 on beat 1 - C7 on "& of 2" - Fm7 on "& of 3" - Bbmaj7 on beat 4 This creates a rolling, walking-bass-like feel even without the left hand moving. --- Ghost Notes and Grace Notes: The Subtle Art of Implication Ghost notes and grace notes aren’t just ornaments—they’re …
