1. Introduction to Press Releases: What They Are and Why They Matter

What Is a Press Release? Imagine you’re the founder of a small organic chocolate company. After months of hard work, you’ve perfected a new dark chocolate bar sweetened only with coconut sugar. It’s not just delicious—it’s the first of its kind to earn a “climate-positive” certification, meaning it actively removes more carbon from the atmosphere than it produces. You’re bursting with pride. Now, how do you share this milestone with the world? You could post about it on Instagram. You could write a long blog post for your website. Or you could send a carefully crafted press release to news outlets, food bloggers, and industry publications. A press release isn’t just another social media update or a casual blog—it’s a formal, structured announcement designed to capture the attention of journalists and media professionals who might want to cover your story. A press release (sometimes called a news release or media release) is a written communication that announces something newsworthy—like a product launch, a merger, an award, a research finding, or a leadership change. It’s written in a journalistic style, with the most important information at the top and supporting details below. Its goal isn’t to sell something directly, but to give journalists the key facts, quotes, and context they need to decide whether to write their own story about your news. Press releases have been a cornerstone of public relations (PR) for over a century. They were once sent by fax or mail to newsrooms, but today, they’re typically distributed digitally through PR platforms, email, or news wires. Despite the rise of social media and blogs, press releases remain a trusted and effective way to get your news in front of the people who matter most: journalists, editors, and industry influencers who can amplify your message to a wider audience. --- Why Press Releases Matter in Business and Media Press releases aren’t just relics of the past—they’re powerful tools for credibility, visibility, and strategic communication. Here’s why they matter: 1. They Build Credibility and Trust When a news outlet picks up your press release and publishes a story about your company, it’s not just advertising—it’s third-party validation. 2. Identifying Your Press Release Goal and Audience

Why One Press Release Doesn’t Fit All Imagine two companies releasing the same product on the same day. One writes a press release packed with technical specs and superlatives, while the other highlights a heartwarming customer story. The first disappears into a void of ignored emails; the second gets picked up by seven regional newspapers. Same product. Two different press releases. Two different outcomes. The difference? Goal and audience. A press release isn’t a generic announcement. It’s a targeted communication designed to serve a specific purpose and reach the right people. Without a clear goal and a well-defined audience, your press release becomes noise—not news. This chapter shows you how to define what you want your press release to achieve and who you need to reach to make it matter. --- Setting the Right Goal: What Do You Want the Press Release to Do? Every press release has a purpose. But not all purposes are the same. You wouldn’t announce a new CEO the same way you’d share a data breach update. The goal shapes everything: what you say, how you say it, and where you send it. There are three main types of press releases, each with a distinct purpose: Informational Press Releases Purpose: Share facts, updates, or insights. Best for: Announcements that inform rather than persuade. Examples: - Launching a new product feature - Sharing quarterly earnings results - Reporting on a company milestone (e.g., 10,000 customers) - Announcing a new hire or executive appointment Key trait: Focuses on what happened and why it matters, not on selling. Promotional Press Releases Purpose: Generate interest and drive action. Best for: Sales-driven or visibility-focused announcements. Examples: - Product launch campaigns - Grand openings or special events - Partnerships aimed at market expansion - Limited-time offers or discounts Key trait: Uses persuasive language to encourage readers to act—buy, attend, or learn more. Crisis-Related Press Releases Purpose: Manage reputation and clarify facts during a critical situation. Best for: Negative news, recalls, scandals, or emergencies. Examples: - Product recall due to safety concerns - Response to a data breach - Addressing negative publicity or misinformation - Crisis-related executive statements Key trait: Prioritizes transparency, accountability, and clarity over promotion. Jargon Alert: Newsworthy means something that journalists and the public would consider worth reporting. A product launch can be newsworthy if it’s unique, timely, or relevant to a broad audience. --- Let’s look at how the same event can be framed differently based on the goal. 3. The Five Ws and One H: Crafting the Core Story

Imagine you’re a journalist sitting at your desk, scanning through dozens of press releases in your inbox. Your time is limited—maybe you have 30 seconds before your next meeting. You glance at the headline, read the first sentence, and then… you either move on or keep reading. What makes you pause? What makes you think, “This is worth my attention”? The answer lies in the Five Ws and One H—a timeless framework that journalists and PR professionals rely on to quickly understand the essence of a story. These six simple questions—Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How—are the backbone of every well-crafted press release. They ensure that your message is clear, concise, and compelling from the very first line. Without them, a press release risks becoming a confusing ramble. With them, it becomes a story that journalists can easily grasp, rewrite, or pitch to their editors. Even if you’re not a journalist, mastering the Five Ws will help you communicate more effectively in any professional context. In this chapter, we’ll break down each of these elements, show how they fit into a press release, and explain why they’re essential for getting your news noticed. --- Understanding the Five Ws and One H The Five Ws and One H are a set of questions that journalists use to gather all the critical details about an event or announcement. By answering these questions upfront, you ensure that your press release is complete, accurate, and ready for publication. Here’s what each one represents: - Who: The people, organizations, or entities involved in the story. - What: The event, announcement, or news itself. - When: The date and time the news is happening or was announced. - Where: The location of the event or where the news is relevant. - Why: The reason behind the news—its significance or purpose. - How: The process or method behind the news—how it happened or will happen. These questions aren’t just for journalists—they’re for anyone who needs to communicate information clearly. Whether you’re writing a press release, an email update, or a social media post, asking these questions forces you to distill your message into its most essential parts. --- Why This Framework Works for Press Releases Press releases are not creative writing exercises. They’re communication tools designed to deliver news efficiently. Journalists and editors receive hundreds of press releases daily, and they have little patience for vague or overly wordy content. 4. Writing a Strong Headline and Subheadline

Why Headlines Matter More Than You Think Imagine scrolling through your inbox at 7 a.m., caffeine barely kicking in. You see a press release titled: "Company X Announces New Product Launch" Your brain does a quick calculation: "New product? Probably marketing fluff. Next." Now imagine you see: "FDA Approves Breakthrough Alzheimer’s Drug Developed by Company X" Your fingers pause. This isn’t just another announcement. This is news—something that affects real people, has regulatory backing, and could change lives. The difference isn’t in what’s being announced; it’s in how it’s packaged. Headlines aren’t just the first line of a press release—they’re the deciding factor between whether a journalist opens your email or hits delete. They’re the gateway to your story. A weak headline buries your news. A strong one makes journalists lean in, even at 7 a.m. This chapter is about learning to write headlines that don’t just describe your news—they demand attention. We’ll break down what makes a headline effective, how to craft one that aligns with journalistic standards, and how to avoid the pitfalls that make editors roll their eyes before they even read the first sentence. --- What Is a Headline in a Press Release? A headline is the bold, attention-grabbing title at the top of your press release. It summarizes the main news in as few words as possible. Think of it as the headline of a newspaper article—short, clear, and compelling. A well-written headline does three things: - Announces the news: It tells the reader what happened. - Highlights the significance: It implies why this matters. - Invites curiosity: It makes the reader want to learn more. For example: "Local Robotics Startup Wins $2M Grant to Scale AI-Powered Manufacturing Tools" This headline does more than say, “A startup got money.” It tells us: - Who: A local robotics startup - What: Won a $2M grant - Why it matters: To scale AI tools for manufacturing That’s not just a title—it’s a promise of what’s inside. --- The Three Pillars of an Effective Headline Not all headlines are created equal. To stand out in a crowded inbox, your headline must balance clarity, brevity, and newsworthiness. Let’s break down each one. --- Clarity: Say What Happened—Don’t Make Them Guess A clear headline leaves no room for confusion. It answers the question: What is the news? Good examples: - "TechCorp Unveils First-Ever Carbon-Negative Laptop" - "City Council Approves Downtown Revitalization Plan" Weak examples: - "TechCorp Makes Big Move in Sustainability" - "Important Decision Made by Council" Why the difference? 5. The Lead Paragraph: Hooking Readers and Journalists

Why the Lead Paragraph Is Your Press Release’s Make-or-Break Moment Imagine you’ve spent hours crafting the perfect press release—a clear message, compelling quotes, and a headline that begs to be clicked. You press send, and… nothing. Reporters don’t open it. Social shares are nonexistent. The story dies before it’s born. What went wrong? In most cases, the lead paragraph—the very first sentences after your headline—failed to deliver. It didn’t answer the critical questions fast enough. It didn’t spark curiosity. It didn’t sound urgent. It didn’t matter. This is the moment that separates a press release that gets ignored from one that gets picked up. The lead paragraph isn’t just an introduction—it’s your first pitch. It must convince a busy journalist, editor, or reader, in seconds, that your story is worth their time. In this chapter, you’ll learn how to write a lead that hooks readers and journalists—the ones who matter most. You’ll discover why it’s the most important paragraph in your release, the two main types of leads, and how to craft one that answers the Five Ws (who, what, when, where, why) clearly and compellingly. Most importantly, you’ll practice writing leads that are concise, active, and impossible to ignore. --- The Purpose of the Lead Paragraph: More Than Just a First Sentence The lead paragraph is the opening act of your press release. Its purpose is simple: to grab attention and answer the most important questions immediately. Think of it as a handshake. A weak handshake leaves a bad impression. A firm, confident one makes people want to listen. In journalism, the lead does the same thing for your story. When you write a press release, you’re not writing for a general audience—you’re writing for journalists who may scan dozens of releases a day. Your lead must: - Answer the Five Ws in one to two sentences. - Create urgency or curiosity—make the reader think, “I need to know more.” - Establish credibility by naming key players, events, or stakes right away. - Guide the reader into the next paragraph—it should flow naturally to the details. A weak lead makes readers (and journalists) ask: - Who is this about? - What actually happened? - Why should I care? A strong lead answers those questions before they’re asked. 6. Body Content: Structuring the Main Message

From the Hook to the Story: The Purpose of the Body Imagine you are a journalist. You’ve just read a headline that grabs your attention and a lede (the lead paragraph) that tells you exactly who, what, where, when, and why. You’re interested. But now you have a problem: you can’t write a full news story based on a single paragraph. You need the "meat." You need the evidence, the context, and the details that turn a brief announcement into a compelling narrative. This is where the body of your press release comes in. If the lede is the "hook," the body is the "story." Its primary purpose is to provide the supporting evidence that validates your claims. While the lede delivers the news, the body explains why the news matters and how it will impact the world. In the body paragraphs, you move from the most critical information to the least critical. This ensures that even if a journalist decides to cut your release for space, the most important points remain at the top. Organizing Content with the Inverted Pyramid To structure your body content logically, you should follow the Inverted Pyramid style of writing. This is the gold standard for journalism. Instead of building up to a big reveal at the end (like a mystery novel), you front-load the most essential information and taper off into secondary details. The Primary Layer: Supporting the Lede Immediately following your lede, your first one or two body paragraphs should expand on the core announcement. If your lede announced a new product launch, this section should explain the product's main features and the specific problem it solves. Ask yourself: If a reader stopped reading after the second paragraph, would they have a clear understanding of the value of this news? The Secondary Layer: Evidence and Validation Once the "what" is established, you move into the "proof." This is where you integrate: Statistics: Hard numbers that prove a trend or a need (e.g., "Market research shows that 60% of small businesses struggle with X"). Context: How this news fits into the current industry landscape. Comparisons: How this new development differs from what existed before. The Tertiary Layer: Background and "Nice-to-Haves" The final section of your body contains the "background information." This is the context that is helpful but not essential to the core story. This might include a brief history of the project, mentions of secondary partners, or general industry trends. 7. Including Quotes: Adding Human Voice and Credibility

The "Robot" Problem in Press Releases Imagine you are a journalist scanning your inbox. You open a press release for a new eco-friendly water bottle. The text is perfectly structured; it follows the Five Ws and One H, the Headline is punchy, and the Body Content is clear. But as you read, you realize the entire piece feels like it was written by a machine. It is a list of features, dates, and corporate claims. There is no soul, no passion, and no person behind the product. Most journalists will skim this and move on. Why? Because news isn't just about what happened; it’s about why it matters and who it affects. This is where the quote comes in. A well-placed quote transforms a dry announcement into a human story. It moves the narrative from "The company released a product" to "The CEO believes this product will save a million gallons of ocean plastic." The Purpose of Quotes: More Than Just Filler In a press release, a quote is not meant to deliver raw data or a list of specifications. You have already handled the facts in the Body Content. Instead, quotes serve three specific strategic purposes: 1. Humanizing the Story Corporate language can often feel cold and distant. A quote allows a real person to speak directly to the reader. It introduces emotion, enthusiasm, and a personal touch that makes the organization feel approachable and authentic. 2. Providing Perspective and "The Why" While the body of the release tells the reader what is happening, the quote explains the significance. It provides the "broader significance" we discussed in earlier chapters. It is the place to explain the vision, the motivation, or the intended impact of the news. 3. Adding Authority and Endorsement When a high-ranking executive or a recognized industry expert puts their name next to a statement, it adds a layer of credibility. It acts as a formal endorsement of the news, signaling to the journalist that the organization stands firmly behind this announcement. --- Crafting a Quote That Sounds Human The biggest mistake beginners make is writing "corporate speak." Corporate speak consists of jargon-heavy, vague sentences that sound professional but mean very little. The "Corporate Speak" Trap: "We are thrilled to leverage our synergistic capabilities to optimize the paradigm of sustainable hydration for our global stakeholders." If a journalist sees a quote like this, they will either ignore it or rewrite it entirely—and they might change your meaning in the process. 8. The Boilerplate: Crafting a Consistent About Section

The "Who Are You?" Moment Imagine a journalist reads your press release. They love your headline, the lead paragraph is gripping, and the body content clearly explains the news. They are convinced that your story is worth publishing. Then, they hit a wall. They realize they have no idea who your company actually is. Do you specialize in sustainable tech? Are you a family-owned bakery that just went national? Are you a venture-backed AI startup or a century-old non-profit? If the journalist has to leave your press release and start a separate Google search just to find a basic summary of your business, you have created "friction." In the fast-paced world of news, friction leads to ignored stories. This is where the boilerplate comes in. A boilerplate is a short, standardized "About" section placed at the very end of a press release. It serves as a professional biography for your organization, providing the essential context a reader needs to understand your credibility and mission without distracting from the main news story. The Role of the Boilerplate While the main body of your press release focuses on the current news (the "what" and "why" of the moment), the boilerplate focuses on the permanent identity of the organization (the "who"). The boilerplate serves three primary functions: 1. Instant Context: It tells the reader exactly what the company does, who it serves, and what it stands for. 2. Establishing Authority: By mentioning company history, awards, or scale, it proves that the organization is a legitimate entity capable of delivering on the promises made in the press release. 3. Brand Consistency: Because the boilerplate remains largely the same from one release to the next, it reinforces your brand identity. Every time a journalist or partner sees it, they are reminded of your core mission. Anatomy of a High-Impact Boilerplate A boilerplate is not a marketing brochure; it is a factual summary. It should be concise—usually between 50 and 150 words—and written in the third person. To build a comprehensive boilerplate from first principles, include these four key elements: 1. The "One-Sentence" Identity Start with a clear, punchy statement that defines the company. Avoid vague jargon like "leveraging synergistic solutions." Instead, use plain language. Weak: "EcoStream is a leader in providing innovative liquid-management paradigms for the modern era." Strong: "EcoStream is a water-filtration company that provides affordable, solar-powered purification systems to rural communities in Sub-Saharan Africa." 2. The Mission and Value Proposition Explain why the company exists and what problem it solves. This connects the company's identity to a broader purpose. 9. Formatting a Press Release for Clarity and Professionalism

The "Split-Second" Rule of Formatting Imagine a busy news editor. Their inbox is overflowing with hundreds of pitches a day. They open your email, glance at your attached press release for exactly three seconds, and then make a decision: Is this professional, or is this an amateur attempt? If your document is a wall of dense text, uses five different fonts, or hides the contact information at the bottom of page two, the editor will likely delete it without reading a single word of your story. Formatting is not about making a document "look pretty." In the world of public relations, formatting is a signal of credibility. When you follow the industry-standard layout, you are telling the journalist, "I know how this industry works, and I am providing this information in a format that makes your job easier." The Essential Anatomy of a Press Release While you have already crafted your Headline, Lead Paragraph, and Boilerplate, those elements must be placed in specific locations to meet professional standards. The Header and Contact Information The very top of your page should be dedicated to the "who" and "how." Before the reader even sees your headline, they need to know who is sending the news and how to reach them for verification or interviews. Place your Letterhead (company logo and name) at the top center or top left. Immediately below or beside this, include a "Media Contact" section. This should be left-aligned and include: Contact Name: The specific person the journalist should talk to. Email Address: A professional email (avoid generic "info@" addresses if possible). Phone Number: A direct line where the contact can be reached quickly. The Release Timing Directly above the headline, you must indicate when the news is allowed to be published. This is known as the Release Date. FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE: Use this if the news can be published the moment the journalist receives it. This is the most common designation. EMBARGOED UNTIL [Date and Time]: An embargo is an agreement between the sender and the journalist that the information will not be published until a specific date and time. This is used for major product launches or coordinated announcements where timing is critical. The Dateline The Dateline is the bridge between your headline and your lead paragraph. It tells the journalist where the news is originating and when the announcement was made. The standard format is: CITY, State – Month Day, Year – Example: CHICAGO, IL – October 14, 2023 – The dateline should be bolded and followed by an em-dash (—) or a hyphen, leading directly into the first sentence of your lead paragraph. 10. Adding Multimedia: Images, Videos, and Links

The Power of a Visual Hook Imagine you are a busy journalist scanning through fifty emails in your inbox. You see two press releases about a new eco-friendly packaging invention. The first is a wall of text. It is well-written, follows the structure of the Five Ws and One H, and has a professional Boilerplate. But it is just words. The second press release includes a high-resolution photo of the product in action, a 30-second video demonstration showing the packaging dissolving in water, and a link to a detailed technical whitepaper. Which one are you more likely to click? Which one is easier to share on social media? Which one provides the "proof" of the story immediately? While the writing is the foundation of your story, multimedia—the collective term for images, audio, video, and interactive content—is what makes that story "sticky." In a digital-first world, multimedia transforms a press release from a simple announcement into a complete media kit. When and Where to Use Multimedia Multimedia should never be used as "filler" to make a page look pretty. Every image or video must serve a strategic purpose: to provide evidence, simplify a complex idea, or evoke an emotion that words alone cannot. Identifying the Right Asset Before adding an element, ask yourself: Does this add new information or simply repeat what I’ve already written? Images: Best for showing a new product, introducing a key executive, or providing a "sense of place" (e.g., a photo of a new storefront). Infographics: These are visual representations of data or knowledge. Use them when your Body Content contains complex statistics, a timeline of events, or a step-by-step process that would be tedious to read in paragraph form. Videos: Ideal for demonstrations, "teaser" trailers for an event, or short interviews. A video can convey the tone and energy of an announcement more effectively than a quote. Links: Use these to lead the reader to deeper evidence, such as a full research study, a sign-up page, or a high-resolution image gallery. Strategic Placement Placement depends on the goal of the asset: 1. The "Hero" Image: Place your most impactful visual (usually a high-quality photo) immediately following the Lead Paragraph. This anchors the story and gives the reader a visual reference for the rest of the text. 2. Supporting Visuals: Place infographics or secondary photos within the Body Content, specifically next to the paragraphs they illustrate. 3. The Resource Block: Links to supporting documents are typically placed toward the end of the release, often just before the Boilerplate, or embedded naturally within the text. Distribution is the bridge between your internal announcement and external awareness. If you choose the wrong bridge, your message may never reach the other side. Understanding Your Distribution Options When it comes to getting your story out, you generally have two paths: using a professional distribution service or handling the process yourself. 13. Writing for Journalists: What Editors Look For

The "Delete" Key: Why Most Press Releases Fail Imagine you are a city editor at a major daily newspaper. It is 9:00 AM on a Tuesday. You open your inbox to find 150 new emails. Most of them are press releases. You spend approximately six seconds looking at each one. You aren't looking for a reason to publish the story; you are looking for a reason to delete it. If the headline is too vague, if the first paragraph sounds like a sales brochure, or if the news is three weeks old, you hit "Delete" and move to the next. To get published, you must stop thinking like a promoter and start thinking like a journalist. A journalist’s job is not to help you market your product; their job is to provide value, insight, or urgency to their readers. If your press release feels like an advertisement, it will be treated like spam. The Journalist’s Filter: What Makes a Story "Newsworthy"? Before a journalist even considers your Body Content, they run your story through a mental filter called "News Values." If your release doesn't hit at least one of these markers, it isn't news—it's just an announcement. 1. Timeliness (The "Why Now?" Factor) News is perishable. A story about a product launch that happened last month is no longer news. Journalists look for a "hook"—a reason why this story matters today. Strong Timeliness: "Local bakery opens today to combat food desert in East District." Weak Timeliness: "Local bakery has been serving the community for six months." 2. Impact and Magnitude How many people does this affect? A story that impacts 10,000 local residents is more newsworthy than a story that impacts ten people. Journalists look for the broader significance of your announcement. Instead of focusing on the fact that you launched a new app, focus on how that app solves a problem for thousands of users. 3. Proximity People care about what is happening in their own backyard. A small business opening in a specific neighborhood is a huge story for a local community paper, but a non-story for a national magazine. Always consider the geographic reach of the journalist you are targeting. 4. Conflict or Controversy Journalists are drawn to tension. This doesn't mean you should manufacture drama, but if your news resolves a long-standing conflict or challenges a common belief in your industry, it is much more likely to be picked up. 5. 14. Measuring Success: Tracking Impact and Adjusting Strategy

The "Send and Forget" Trap Imagine you’ve spent two weeks perfecting your press release. You nailed the Headline, crafted a compelling Lead Paragraph, and carefully selected Multimedia to make the story pop. You hit "send" to fifty journalists, close your laptop, and wait. A week later, you see one small mention of your story on a local news blog. Was the campaign a failure? Or did that one mention lead to a thousand new website visitors and ten new partnership inquiries? If you aren't tracking your results, you are guessing. In the world of public relations, guessing is expensive. Whether you are a solo entrepreneur or part of a large corporation, the difference between a "shot in the dark" and a professional strategy is the ability to measure impact. Measuring success isn't just about counting how many people saw your announcement; it’s about understanding why some journalists responded while others ignored you, and using that data to make your next release even more effective. Defining Your Success: What are KPIs? Before you can measure success, you have to define what "success" actually looks like. You cannot track everything, so you must choose a few specific metrics to watch. These are called Key Performance Indicators (KPIs). A KPI is a quantifiable measure used to evaluate the success of an organization or a specific activity. Instead of saying, "I want a lot of people to see this," a KPI allows you to say, "I want five articles published in industry-specific magazines." Depending on the goal you set back in Chapter 2, your KPIs will change. Common KPIs for Press Releases 1. Media Pickups (The "Coverage" Metric) A pickup occurs when a journalist or publication takes your press release and turns it into an actual story. Quantitative measure: The total number of articles published. Qualitative measure: The "tier" of the publication. A story in The New York Times is a higher-value pickup than a mention on a personal blog. 2. Website Traffic (The "Action" Metric) If your press release included links to your website, you can track how many people clicked through. Referral Traffic: This tells you which specific websites are sending readers to your page. Conversion Rate: The percentage of people who arrived from the press release and then took a desired action (e.g., signing up for a newsletter or buying a product). 3.
