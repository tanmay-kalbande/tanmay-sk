1. TEAS Exam Overview and Study Planning

Imagine two nursing school applicants, Maria and Janice. Both work full-time, both have exactly eight weeks to prepare for the TEAS exam, and both need a competitive score to secure their admission. Janice dives right in, taking random online quizzes during her lunch breaks and skimming through a massive prep textbook whenever she finds a spare moment. Maria takes a different approach. She spends her first three days mapping out the exact structure of the exam, taking a full-length diagnostic test to see where she stands, and building a day-by-day study calendar. When test day arrives, Janice feels overwhelmed and exhausted, realizing too late that she neglected the science section entirely. Maria walks in confident, knowing she has systematically addressed her weakest areas and mastered the exam’s format. The difference between these two outcomes rarely comes down to raw intelligence or baseline knowledge. It comes down to strategy. The TEAS (Test of Essential Academic Skills) is not just a measure of what you know; it is a measure of how well you can prepare. To pass the TEAS and secure your spot in a competitive nursing or allied health program, you need to understand exactly what you are up against and build a systematic plan to conquer it. Anatomy of the TEAS Exam The TEAS exam, currently in its seventh version (ATI TEAS 7), is a standardized test administered by the Assessment Technologies Institute (ATI). It is designed to evaluate a candidate's academic readiness for nursing and health science programs. Because the exam is heavily timed, understanding its structural breakdown is your first critical step toward success. You cannot pace yourself if you do not know how many questions you face or how much time you have to answer them. The Four Content Areas The exam is divided into four distinct sections, each designed to test a specific set of academic skills essential for clinical practice. You will face a total of 170 questions across these four sections, with a strict overall time limit of 209 minutes (roughly 3.5 hours). Here is the exact breakdown of the exam structure: 1. 2. Reading – Key Ideas and Details

The Anatomy of a Reading Question Imagine you are a nurse on a busy ward. A physician leaves a brief, handwritten note about a patient's changing condition and a new medication protocol. If you focus only on the word "fever" and miss the instruction to administer the medication "only if the fever exceeds 101.5°F," the consequences could be severe. This is the core reality of the TEAS Reading section. As outlined in your study planning, the Reading section contains 45 questions to be answered in 55 minutes. The test-makers are not evaluating your ability to read words on a page; they are evaluating your ability to extract actionable, clinical intelligence from a text under time pressure. The largest portion of the Reading section falls under the category of Key Ideas and Details. To master this section, you must move beyond passive reading and adopt an interrogative approach—actively hunting for the author's purpose, the hierarchy of information, and the specific instructions embedded in the text. Topics, Main Ideas, and Supporting Details Before attempting to summarize a passage, you must understand its internal hierarchy. Every well-structured passage is built on a three-tiered framework: the topic, the main idea, and the supporting details. Distinguishing the Three Tiers 1. Topic: The broad subject of the passage. It is usually expressible in a word or a short phrase. (e.g., Wound care). 2. Main Idea: The specific point the author is making about the topic. It is a complete sentence. (e.g., Proper wound care requires keeping the injury moist rather than dry to promote faster tissue regeneration.) 3. Supporting Details: The facts, statistics, examples, and steps that prove or explain the main idea. (e.g., A study showing that moist wounds heal 50% faster, or a list of specific hydrocolloid dressings used for this purpose.) Intermediate readers often confuse the topic for the main idea. If a TEAS question asks, "What is the main idea of this passage?" and one of the answer choices is just a single word or a noun phrase, that is the topic, not the main idea. Eliminate it. Explicit vs. Implicit Main Ideas The TEAS exam will test your ability to identify both explicit and implicit main ideas across three types of passages: expository (informational), narrative (story-driven), and technical (instructions or manuals). Explicit Main Ideas are stated directly by the author, often in a thesis statement. They are commonly found in expository passages and technical texts. Strategy: Look for the main idea in the first or last sentence of the first paragraph. If it’s not there, scan the concluding paragraph. 3. Reading – Craft, Structure, and Integration

Decoding the Author's Blueprint: Text Structure Imagine a nurse reviewing two patient shift reports. The first nurse says, "The patient in Room 4 is experiencing severe nausea. This started immediately after the administration of the new IV antibiotic." The second nurse says, "The patient in Room 4 is experiencing severe nausea. We should administer an antiemetic and hold the next antibiotic dose." Both convey information, but the underlying logic is entirely different. The first is a cause-effect relationship; the second is a problem-solution setup. In Reading – Key Ideas and Details, you learned how to extract the explicit message from a text. Now, we look behind the curtain. The TEAS exam tests your ability to see the author's blueprint—how a text is organized to maximize its impact. Recognizing text structure allows you to anticipate where information is going, which is a massive advantage under strict time limits. You will typically encounter four primary structures on the TEAS: Cause-Effect: The author explains why something happened (cause) and the results of that event (effect). Clue words: consequently, therefore, as a result, due to, since. Problem-Solution: The author presents a significant issue and proposes one or more ways to resolve it. Clue words: issue, dilemma, proposed, resolve, solution, challenge. Comparison-Contrast: The author examines the similarities (comparison) and differences (contrast) between two or more subjects. Clue words: similarly, likewise, conversely, however, on the other hand, whereas. Chronological/Sequential: The author presents events in the order they occurred or outlines steps in a process. Clue words: first, next, finally, before, subsequently. Identifying Structure on the Exam Do not rely solely on clue words; test-makers know students look for them and will occasionally use them as distractors. Instead, map the logical relationship between the sentences. If a paragraph introduces a new medication and then details its mechanism of action, side effects, and administration guidelines, the structure is description. If it discusses the rise of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and then outlines a new hospital protocol to combat it, the structure is problem-solution. When asked to identify the structure, ask yourself: What is the author doing with these pieces of information? Are they showing how A led to B? Are they showing how A is different from B? Beyond the Words: Author's Purpose and Tone An author's purpose is their reason for writing—typically to inform, persuade, or entertain. In a healthcare context, texts are predominantly informational, but you will encounter persuasive elements, particularly in passages about public health or patient compliance. Tone, on the other hand, is the author's attitude toward the subject. Tone is revealed through word choice (diction) and sentence structure (syntax). 4. Mathematics – Numbers and Operations

The Pharmacy Order That Trips Up Everyone A physician orders 0.4 mg of a medication. The pharmacy stocks it in 250 mcg tablets. How many tablets do you administer? If you mentally froze for a second, you are not alone. This single problem requires you to convert decimals to fractions, translate between metric units, and set up a proportion—all before you even touch a calculator. On the TEAS exam, questions like this are ubiquitous. They do not exist just to test your math skills; they test whether you can safely manipulate numbers in a clinical setting. As you work through this module, remember the study framework established in TEAS Exam Overview and Study Planning. When you miss a practice question here, ask yourself: Was it a content gap? Was it a comprehension error? Or was it a pacing issue? Arithmetic errors are usually comprehension or pacing issues—rushing through decimal placements or skipping steps. Let’s build the precision required to master these numbers and operations. Converting Between Fractions, Decimals, and Percentages The TEAS exam frequently requires you to fluidly translate a number from one format to another. Rather than memorizing isolated rules, it helps to understand that fractions, decimals, and percentages are simply three different languages describing the exact same value. Fraction to Decimal and Back To convert a fraction to a decimal, simply divide the numerator (top number) by the denominator (bottom number). Example: 3/8 = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 To convert a decimal to a fraction, say the decimal out loud using place value, then simplify. Example: 0.375 is read as "three hundred seventy-five thousandths," or 375/1000. Simplify by dividing the numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor (25): 375 ÷ 25 = 15; 1000 ÷ 25 = 40. You get 15/40. Divide by 5: 3/8. Decimal to Percentage and Back To convert a decimal to a percentage, multiply by 100 and attach the percent sign (%). This is equivalent to moving the decimal point two places to the right. Example: 0.375 becomes 37.5% To convert a percentage to a decimal, do the exact opposite: divide by 100, or move the decimal point two places to the left. Example: 45% becomes 0.45 Percentage to Fraction and Back To convert a percentage to a fraction, place the number over 100 and simplify. Example: 45% = 45/100. Simplify by dividing by 5: 9/20. 5. Mathematics – Algebra, Measurement, and Data

Algebraic Equations and Inequalities A patient is prescribed 250 mg of a medication. The tablets you have on hand contain 50 mg each. How many tablets do you administer? While this might seem like a basic arithmetic problem, it is fundamentally an algebraic equation: $50x = 250$. Building directly on the arithmetic and order of operations covered in Mathematics – Numbers and Operations, algebra introduces variables—letters that represent unknown quantities. On the TEAS exam, algebra is less about abstract theory and more about translating real-world healthcare scenarios into solvable equations. Solving One-Step and Multi-Step Linear Equations The goal of solving any linear equation is to isolate the variable on one side of the equals sign. To do this without changing the value of the equation, you apply the inverse operation to both sides. Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. One-Step Equations require a single inverse operation to solve. Addition/Subtraction: $x - 15 = 42$ → Add 15 to both sides → $x = 57$ Multiplication/Division: $4y = 32$ → Divide both sides by 4 → $y = 8$ Multi-Step Equations often require you to combine like terms or use the distributive property before isolating the variable. Scenario: A healthcare clinic budgets $500 for flu vaccines. If the clinic pays $15 per vaccine and has a fixed administrative fee of $50, how many vaccines ($v$) can they purchase? 1. Set up the equation: $15v + 50 = 500$ 2. Subtract 50 from both sides: $15v = 450$ 3. Divide by 15: $v = 30$ The clinic can purchase 30 vaccines. Linear Inequalities Inequalities differ from equations because they show a range of possible values rather than a single exact value. The TEAS exam uses four inequality symbols: $<$ less than $$ greater than $\le$ less than or equal to $\ge$ greater than or equal to Solving inequalities is almost identical to solving equations, with one critical exception: if you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, you must flip the inequality sign. Example: Solve $-3x 12$ 1. Divide both sides by -3. 2. Because you divided by a negative, flip the $$ to $<$. 3. Result: $x < 12$ If a patient's heart rate ($h$) must remain strictly below 110 beats per minute, the inequality is $h < 110$. If the parameters are inclusive (e.g., a safe systolic blood pressure is between 90 and 120), you would use $\le$ and $\ge$ accordingly. 6. Science – Human Anatomy and Physiology

The Body as an Integrated Machine Imagine a marathon runner crossing the finish line. In that single moment of exertion, her integumentary system is sweating to dump excess heat, her respiratory system is rapidly pulling in oxygen, her cardiovascular system is racing to deliver that oxygen to her leg muscles, and her nervous system is firing signals to keep her legs moving in a coordinated stride. None of these systems operate in isolation. The TEAS exam tests your ability to understand the human body not as a collection of isolated parts, but as a highly integrated machine. You will need to recall the structures of major systems, explain their physiological processes, and predict how they interact. Just as the Mathematics – Algebra, Measurement, and Data chapter taught you to look for the relationships between variables, this chapter requires you to look for the relationships between bodily systems. We will move briskly through the structures to focus heavily on how these systems function together. Support and Movement: Skeletal, Muscular, and Integumentary These three systems form the body’s outer framework, providing protection, structure, and the capacity for movement. The Skeletal System The skeletal system provides the rigid framework for the body, protects vital organs, and serves as a storage site for calcium and phosphorus. It also acts as the site for hematopoiesis (blood cell formation) within the red bone marrow. Key structural components include: - Long bones: Feature a shaft (diaphysis) and rounded ends (epiphyses). The epiphyses are covered in articular cartilage to reduce friction at joints. - Joint types: - Fibrous: Immovable (e.g., skull sutures) - Cartilaginous: Slightly movable (e.g., intervertebral discs) - Synovial: Freely movable (e.g., knee, elbow). These contain synovial fluid for lubrication. Physiologically, bone is a living tissue that constantly remodels. Osteoblasts build bone, while osteoclasts resorb it. The endocrine system tightly regulates this calcium balance via the parathyroid hormone (PTH) and calcitonin. The Muscular System Muscles generate movement, maintain posture, and produce heat. There are three distinct types of muscle tissue: 1. Skeletal muscle: Voluntary, striated, and attached to bones. 2. Cardiac muscle: Involuntary, striated, and found only in the heart. Features intercalated discs that allow for rapid electrical signaling, causing the heart to beat as a synchronized unit. 3. Smooth muscle: Involuntary, non-striated, and found in the walls of hollow organs (e.g., intestines, blood vessels). 7. Science – Life, Physical, and Scientific Reasoning

A patient enters the emergency room confused and breathing rapidly. Their blood pH has dropped dangerously low, a condition known as metabolic acidosis. To stabilize them, the medical team must administer an intravenous buffer solution. Understanding why this intervention works requires a grasp of basic chemistry—specifically, how acids, bases, and buffers interact in the human body. As you prepare for the science portion of the TEAS exam, remember that nursing is fundamentally an applied science. While Science – Human Anatomy and Physiology covered the macroscopic structures of the body, this chapter zooms in on the microscopic and molecular foundations that keep those structures functioning. We will explore the chemistry of life, the biology of the cell, the genetic code that dictates protein synthesis, and the scientific reasoning that underpins all medical research and evidence-based practice. The Building Blocks: Matter, Atoms, and Bonds To understand how medications interact with the body or how cells generate energy, you must first understand basic atomic structure and chemical bonding. Atomic Structure All matter is composed of atoms, the smallest units of an element that retain its chemical properties. An atom consists of a central nucleus containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons. The TEAS exam often tests your understanding of isotopes and ions: - Isotopes: Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Some isotopes are radioactive and used in medical imaging or cancer treatment. - Ions: Atoms that have gained or lost electrons, acquiring a net electrical charge. A loss of electrons creates a positive ion (cation), while a gain creates a negative ion (anion). For example, sodium (Na) readily loses an electron to become Na+, a crucial electrolyte in nerve signaling. Chemical Bonds and Reactions Atoms bond to achieve a stable electron configuration, usually an outer shell of eight electrons (the octet rule). The type of bond formed dictates the properties of the resulting molecule. - Ionic Bonds: Formed when one atom transfers electrons to another. The resulting opposite charges hold the atoms together. Application: Ionic bonds are easily broken in water. When table salt (NaCl) enters the bloodstream, it dissociates into Na+ and Cl- ions, which conduct electrical impulses essential for muscle contractions. - Covalent Bonds: Formed when atoms share electrons. These are the strongest bonds in biology. Application: Water (H₂O) relies on polar covalent bonds, where oxygen pulls electrons closer, creating a slight negative charge on the oxygen and slight positive charges on the hydrogens. 8. English – Grammar, Sentence Structure, and Mechanics

A nursing student named Maria submits her clinical reflection paper. She writes: "The patient, along with their family members, were anxious about the procedure. Walking into the room, the chart was dropped on the floor by the nurse." While Maria’s clinical skills might be excellent, her writing contains four distinct grammatical and mechanical errors that distort her meaning. On the TEAS exam, you will act as the editor. The English and Language Usage section relies heavily on your ability to spot and fix these exact types of errors quickly. Building on the active reading strategies covered in Reading – Craft, Structure, and Integration, this chapter shifts the focus from comprehending passages to evaluating the technical mechanics of individual sentences. Subject-Verb Agreement and Verb Tense The foundation of a clear sentence is a subject and a verb that agree in number and make sense in time. The TEAS exam will test your ability to navigate interruptions and shifts in tense. Navigating Interrupters and Complex Subjects Subjects and verbs must both be singular or both be plural. Errors typically occur when the test-maker places phrases between the subject and the verb to distract you. The "Intervening Phrase" Trap: Prepositional phrases often sneak between the subject and verb. To check agreement, mentally cross out the phrase between them. - Incorrect: The box of medical supplies are on the table. - Correction: The box (of medical supplies) is on the table. The "Along With" Rule: Words like along with, as well as, and in addition to do not make a singular subject plural. The verb must still agree with the main subject. - Incorrect: The head nurse, along with the nursing students, are attending the seminar. - Correction: The head nurse, along with the nursing students, is attending the seminar. Indefinite Pronouns: The following words are always singular and take singular verbs: each, every, everyone, everybody, someone, anybody, neither, either, one. - Correct: Neither of the patients has a fever. Correlative Conjunctions: When using either/or or neither/nor, the verb must agree with the subject closest to it. - Correct: Neither the residents nor the attending physician is available. (Singular verb matches singular "physician"). - Correct: Neither the attending physician nor the residents are available. (Plural verb matches plural "residents"). Mastering Verb Tense Consistency The TEAS rarely tests obscure tenses; instead, it tests tense consistency. A sentence should not arbitrarily jump from the past to the present. - Incorrect: The patient complained of chest pain, so the nurse calls the rapid response team. - Correction: The patient complained of chest pain, so the nurse called the rapid response team. 9. English – Vocabulary and Language Knowledge

A patient presents with tachycardia and bradypnea. The attending physician orders an antipyretic and notes that the patient's hematocrit is low. If you are an aspiring nurse, you need to understand this information instantly to provide safe care. However, you cannot possibly memorize every medical and academic term in the English language. Instead, you must rely on structural clues—prefixes, suffixes, and roots—and contextual clues to decode unfamiliar terminology rapidly. As you learned in Reading – Craft, Structure, and Integration, understanding how authors construct texts is critical. Now, we will apply that structural lens to the words themselves. The TEAS exam tests your ability to dissect words, distinguish between easily confused terms, and adjust your language to fit the clinical and academic contexts you will operate in daily. Decoding Word Meanings: Prefixes, Suffixes, and Roots English is a composite language, heavily borrowing from Greek and Latin. This is a massive advantage on the TEAS exam. If you know the core components of a word, you can deduce its meaning even if you have never seen it before. Let us move briskly through the fundamentals. A root carries the primary meaning of the word. A prefix is attached to the beginning to modify that meaning. A suffix is attached to the end to determine the word's part of speech (noun, verb, adjective) or its specific condition. High-Yield Greek and Latin Roots For the TEAS, focus your energy on roots that frequently appear in medical and academic settings. - Cardi/o (Heart): Carditis (inflammation of the heart), cardiomyopathy (disease of the heart muscle). - Hepat/o (Liver): Hepatitis (inflammation of the liver), hepatomegaly (enlargement of the liver). - Nephr/o & Ren/o (Kidney): Nephropathy (disease of the kidney), renal failure. - Neur/o (Nerve): Neuropathy (nerve damage), neurotransmitter. - Oste/o (Bone): Osteoporosis (porous bones), osteomyelitis (infection of the bone). - Pneum/o & Pulm/o (Lung): Pneumothorax (air in the chest cavity), pulmonary embolism. - Hema/o & Sangu/i (Blood): Hematology (study of blood), sanguine (optimistic, or relating to blood). - Cyt/o (Cell): Cytology (study of cells), cytotoxic (toxic to cells). Essential Prefixes Prefixes often indicate location, time, number, or status. - Brady- (Slow) / Tachy- (Fast): Bradycardia (slow heart rate), Tachypnea (fast breathing). - Hyper- (Excessive/High) / Hypo- (Deficient/Low): Hypertension (high blood pressure), Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar). - Eu- (Normal/Good): Eupnea (normal breathing), euthyroid (normal thyroid function). - Inter- (Between) / Intra- (Within): Intercostal (between ribs), intravenous (within a vein). - Ab- (Away from) / Ad- (Toward): Abduction (moving away from the midline), adduction (moving toward the midline). 10. Test-Taking Strategies and Pacing

You have exactly 1 minute and 5 seconds. That is the average time you have per question on the TEAS Science section. For the Reading section, it is 1 minute and 53 seconds. You can know the anatomy of the human heart inside and out, but if you spend four minutes decoding a single paragraph, you will not finish the exam. Knowing the content—mastered through the Active Learning, Spaced Repetition, and Practice Questions cycles established in Chapter 1—is only half the battle. The other half is execution under pressure. This chapter bridges the gap between knowing the material and applying it efficiently within the rigid constraints of the exam. Mastering the Clock: Section-Specific Pacing Time management on the TEAS is not about rushing; it is about maintaining a steady, deliberate rhythm. Because the exam is administered on a computer and does not allow you to return to previous sections once time expires, you must develop an internal clock for each of the four sections. As a baseline, recall the structure from Chapter 1: Reading (53 questions / 64 minutes), Mathematics (36 questions / 54 minutes), Science (53 questions / 63 minutes), and English and Language Usage (28 questions / 28 minutes). Note that some questions are unscored pretest items, but you must treat every question as if it counts. Reading: Budgeting for Passages The Reading section presents a unique pacing challenge: you must read passages before answering the associated questions. You cannot simply divide 64 by 53 and assume you have 1 minute and 12 seconds per question. The Passage Tax: Budget roughly 2 to 3 minutes to read and mentally map a standard passage. For a single passage with 4 to 6 questions, aim to spend no more than 5 to 6 minutes total. Skim for Structure: Do not get bogged down in details on the first read. Identify the main idea, the author’s tone, and where specific information is located. When a question asks about a specific detail, return to the text rather than relying on memory. Flag and Move: If a question requires deep inference and you find yourself rereading the paragraph for the third time, flag it, make an educated guess, and move on. You can return to flagged questions if time permits. Mathematics: The Two-Pass System The Mathematics section allows 1 minute and 30 seconds per question, but some questions (like multi-step word problems) will naturally take longer than others (like identifying place values). First Pass (High-Yield): Fly through the section answering questions you immediately know how to solve. If you can solve it in under 45 seconds, do it. 11. Practice Exams and Targeted Review

Imagine sitting for the TEAS exam. You are 45 minutes into the Science section, staring at a question about the directional flow of blood through the heart. You know you studied this. You read the Science – Human Anatomy and Physiology chapter twice. Yet, under the pressure of the clock, the distinction between the pulmonary valve and the tricuspid valve suddenly feels murky. This scenario is incredibly common. Reading about a concept and successfully retrieving that concept under timed, high-stakes conditions are two entirely different cognitive skills. Full-length practice exams are the bridge between passive knowledge and active, test-ready proficiency. They are not just assessment tools; they are the final, most critical phase of your study plan. By this point in your preparation, you have built a strong foundation across all four content domains. Now, it is time to put that knowledge to the test. This chapter focuses on executing full-length, timed practice exams, conducting a meticulous analysis of your performance, and developing a targeted review plan to close remaining content gaps before exam day. The Anatomy of a Full-Length Practice Exam A practice exam only yields useful data if the testing environment closely mimics the actual TEAS. If you take a practice test in your bed, listening to music, and pausing for snacks, your score will reflect your comfort level, not your exam readiness. Simulating Test Day Conditions To get an accurate baseline of your performance, you must simulate test day. This means replicating not just the content, but the physical and psychological conditions of the exam. 1. Adhere strictly to the time limits: The TEAS is a strictly timed exam. You will have 64 minutes for Reading, 54 minutes for Mathematics, 63 minutes for Science, and 28 minutes for English and Language Usage. Do not give yourself an extra five minutes to finish a math problem. If the time is up, the test is over. 2. Take it in one sitting: The TEAS is an endurance test. Taking the four sections across two days defeats the purpose of building the mental stamina required for exam day. Clear a block of approximately 3.5 hours to complete the entire exam, including the short breaks you would normally be permitted. 3. Use only permitted resources: You will not have your phone, textbooks, or internet access during the real exam. You will only have a calculator (usually built into the computer interface) and scratch paper. Recreate this exactly. 4. Minimize distractions: Choose a quiet space like a library or a dedicated home office. Inform family or roommates that you are unavailable.
