For example, a 10 lb test line should break when it’s holding 10 pounds of weight. But remember—this is a guideline, not a guarantee. Sharp rocks, sudden jerks, or a fish’s thrash can still break a line even below its rated strength. - Stretch: How much the line elongates under pressure. More stretch gives you a cushion against sudden pulls (like a fish lunging), but less stretch helps you feel subtle bites and set the hook more precisely. - Visibility: How easily fish (or other anglers) can see the line. Fish have keen eyesight, especially in clear water, so a highly visible line might spook them. These three factors—strength, stretch, and visibility—are the foundation for choosing the right line. Why Your Fishing Trip Could Succeed or Fail Before You Even Cast Picture this: You arrive at your favorite shore fishing spot at sunrise, the water is calm, and the air smells fresh. These aren’t just background details; they actively shape where fish feed, when they’re active, and how they respond to your bait. Ignore them, and you’re fishing blind. Use them wisely, and you tip the odds in your favor—even as a beginner. This chapter will break down these three critical factors into simple, actionable ideas. You won’t need a marine biology degree or expensive equipment. Just a basic understanding and a way to check forecasts—both of which you’ll learn here. --- The Tides: The Ocean’s Breath That Moves Fish What Are Tides, Really? Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea levels caused by the gravitational pull of the moon and, to a lesser extent, the sun. As the moon orbits Earth, its gravity pulls water toward it, creating a bulge on the side of Earth facing the moon. A second bulge forms on the opposite side due to the centrifugal force of Earth’s rotation. These bulges cause high tide in those areas, while areas between them experience low tide. Most coastlines experience two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes—a cycle slightly longer than a day. That’s why tides shift about 50 minutes later each day. Tide Terms You Need to Know - High Tide: The peak of the water level when the sea reaches its highest point on the shore. Fish often move into shallower areas to feed. - Low Tide: When water levels drop to their lowest point. Fish may retreat to deeper channels or pools. - Flood Tide: The period when water is rising toward high tide. - Ebb Tide: The period when water is falling toward low tide. - Slack Tide: The brief period when the water is not moving significantly—between flood and ebb. Fish activity can slow down here. 💡 Tip: Slack tide isn’t necessarily the best time to fish. Many anglers actually prefer the first hour of flood tide or the last hour of ebb tide, when currents are strong and bring food into the shallows.
