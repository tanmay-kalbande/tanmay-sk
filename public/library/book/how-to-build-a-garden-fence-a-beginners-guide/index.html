1. Planning Your Garden Fence

Imagine stepping out into your backyard on a crisp Saturday morning, coffee in hand, ready to admire the heirloom tomatoes you’ve nurtured from tiny seeds—only to find them decimated overnight. The culprit? A hungry deer, a sneaky rabbit, or perhaps a family of groundhogs that treated your vegetable patch like an all-you-can-eat buffet. A garden fence is the ultimate line of defense, but jumping straight into building without a blueprint is a recipe for wasted time, wasted money, and a barrier that fails to do its job. Before you ever pick up a shovel or a bag of concrete, you need a solid plan. Planning involves understanding exactly what you are trying to keep out, navigating the legal rules of your neighborhood, and choosing a style that matches your skill level. Identifying Your Foes: Pests, Animals, and Your Garden The single biggest mistake beginners make is building a fence based purely on how it looks, rather than what it needs to stop. A beautiful three-foot picket fence might look charming, but it won't even slow down a deer. To determine the required height and mesh size—the measurement of the open spaces between the wires in a fence—you must first identify the specific animals targeting your garden. Different pests have different behaviors. Some are jumpers, some are diggers, and some are small enough to squeeze through tiny gaps. Common Garden Pests and How to Stop Them Deer Deer are high jumpers. If deer are your primary concern, your fence needs to be tall. A deer fence should be at least 7 to 8 feet high to prevent them from leaping over it. Because deer have poor depth perception, a solid fence or a fence with a visible top rail can sometimes deter them at lower heights, but for true security, height is your best friend. The mesh size for deer does not need to be incredibly small; a 4-inch by 4-inch grid is usually sufficient because deer are large animals that will not squeeze through a gap that size. Rabbits Rabbits are not jumpers, but they are squeezers and chewers. A fence to keep rabbits out only needs to be about 2 to 3 feet high. However, the mesh size is critical. You need a mesh size of 1 inch or smaller (often called hardware cloth or poultry netting) to prevent them from squeezing through. Additionally, rabbits are notorious diggers. While the actual digging of a trench happens later in the building process, your plan must account for burying the bottom 6 to 12 inches of the fence mesh underground or flaring it outward along the grass to prevent them from burrowing underneath. 2. Gathering Tools and Materials

Imagine walking into a massive home improvement store on a Saturday morning. You have your plan in hand—you know your property setbacks, you’ve navigated the HOA approval process, and you’ve decided on a 4-foot-high fence with a 2-inch by 4-inch mesh to keep your dogs safely in the garden. But as you stand at the mouth of the lumber aisle, staring at endless rows of wood, metal wire, and unfamiliar hand tools, the sheer volume of choices makes your head spin. Which wood lasts the longest? What exactly is a "post hole digger," and do you really need a level? Building a fence requires a strategic shopping list. If you buy the wrong wood, your fence might rot in two years. If you forget a crucial tool, your Saturday project turns into a Monday headache. This chapter breaks down the anatomy of a fence, explains how to choose the right materials for your budget, and provides a comprehensive tool checklist so you can walk into the hardware store with confidence. The Anatomy of a Fence Before you can select materials, you need to know the basic components of a fence. Whether you are building a solid wooden privacy barrier or a simple wire mesh enclosure to keep out rabbits, almost all fences share a similar skeletal structure. Posts Posts are the heavy, vertical pillars that act as the structural backbone of your fence. They bear the weight of the fence and resist the forces of wind, leaning animals, and gravity. Because they carry the most stress, posts are usually the thickest components of the fence. There are three main types of posts you will encounter: Corner posts: Sit at the exact corners of your fence line. They bear tension from two different directions. Gate posts: Flank the opening where your gate will go. Because a gate is heavy and moves constantly, gate posts need to be extra sturdy and deeply set. Line posts: Placed in a straight line between the corner posts. Their primary job is to support the span of the fence and keep it from sagging. Rails Rails (sometimes called stringers) are the horizontal beams that connect the posts together. They act as the framing to which your fencing material will be attached. Depending on the height of your fence, you will typically have two or three rails running between each post. For example, a 6-foot-high fence usually requires three rails (one near the top, one near the bottom, and one in the middle), while a 3-foot-high rabbit fence might only need two. 3. Measuring and Marking the Layout

Imagine sinking your first fence post with pride, only to step back and realize your supposedly straight fence line looks like a snake slithering across your yard. Or worse, finishing your fence only to find the gaps between the posts are so wide that your wire mesh sags like a hammock. These aren't rare disasters—they are the most common headaches that strike beginner fence builders who rush the measuring and marking phase. The secret to a sturdy, professional-looking garden fence isn't brute strength; it is a perfectly mapped-out layout. Translating your paper plan onto the actual dirt requires patience, basic math, and an understanding of how to use simple tools to establish undeniable straight lines. The Foundation of a Straight Fence: Mason Line and Stakes Before you can figure out where to dig, you need a physical guide that represents the exact path your fence will take. You cannot trust your eyes alone to make a line straight over a distance of 50 or 100 feet. To establish your fence line, you will use mason line and stakes. Mason line is a brightly colored, highly visible string (usually neon pink, orange, or yellow) made from twisted nylon. It is specifically designed to be pulled incredibly taut without stretching, ensuring the line remains perfectly straight between two points. Stakes in this context are temporary markers driven into the ground. They can be made of wood, metal, or heavy-duty plastic. For layout purposes, 12-to-18-inch wooden stakes are ideal because they are cheap and easy to drive into the soil. Setting Up Your First Line Recall from Planning Your Garden Fence that you already determined your setback distance—the legal distance you must keep from the property line. You also checked for any utility easements. You will want to set your mason line exactly where the face of your fence will sit, keeping those setbacks in mind. To establish a straight line between two points (like two corners of your yard), follow these steps: 1. Drive the starting stake: Find your starting corner. Drive a stake securely into the ground where the center of your corner post will eventually sit. 2. Drive the ending stake: Go to the opposite end of that fence run. Drive a second stake into the ground at the exact distance your fence will span. 3. Tie the mason line: Tie the mason line to the first stake. Pull it toward the second stake. 4. Make it taut: Pull the string as tight as you possibly can without bending or pulling the stakes out of alignment. Tie it off to the second stake. 5. 4. Digging Post Holes

Imagine spending an entire weekend building a beautiful garden fence to keep out deer and neighborhood dogs, only to find it leaning at a precarious angle the following spring. You planned meticulously, checked your setbacks, and measured your layout perfectly, but winter did a number on your fence. This isn't a result of bad materials or poor construction; it's a result of bad holes. When water in the soil freezes, it expands. If your fence posts aren't buried deep enough, this expanding ice will literally push the earth—and your posts—upward. Digging post holes is the most physically demanding part of building a fence, but doing it correctly is the difference between a structure that lasts decades and one that fails in a single season. Safety First: Calling Before You Dig You have your layout marked from the previous module, and you are eager to break ground. Before a shovel ever touches the soil, you must identify what lies beneath it. Most yards are crisscrossed with a network of underground utilities—buried pipes and cables that deliver electricity, water, gas, and communications to your home. Striking one of these lines with a shovel can cause neighborhood-wide outages, costly repairs, severe injuries, or even fatal explosions. Using Utility Marking Services To prevent this, you must use a utility marking service. In the United States and Canada, this is a free service accessed by dialing 811 (often called "Call Before You Dig"). In other regions, similar services exist under different names, but the process is generally the same: 1. Place the call: Contact the service a few business days before you plan to dig. You will provide your address and the specific area where you intend to excavate. 2. The wait: Utility companies are notified and will send technicians to your property. 3. The marking: Technicians will use colored spray paint or small flags to mark the approximate location of buried lines on your lawn. Understanding the Color Codes Utility workers use a standardized color code so you know exactly what is buried where. Familiarize yourself with these colors before you start digging: Red: Electric power lines Yellow: Gas, oil, or steam lines Orange: Communication, alarm, or signal lines Blue: Potable (drinking) water Green: Sewers and drain lines White: Proposed excavation limits (often painted by you to show the utility workers where you plan to dig) Pink: Temporary survey markings Even with these markings, the exact depth of the lines is rarely guaranteed. Soil shifts over time, meaning a line originally buried two feet deep might now be just inches below the surface. 5. Setting and Plumbing Fence Posts

Imagine waking up the morning after a heavy spring storm to find your newly built garden fence leaning at a 30-degree angle, the mesh sagging, and a family of deer happily munching on your lettuce. The culprit is almost never the fence material itself, nor the horizontal rails, nor the wire mesh. The culprit is the posts. Fence posts are the skeleton of your entire structure. If they are weak, crooked, or loosely anchored, everything attached to them will eventually fail. In this stage of the build, we are transitioning from moving dirt to erecting structure. You have already planned your layout, gathered your tools, and dug your holes. Now, it is time to lock your posts into the earth. Preparing the Post and the Hole Before you drop a post into the ground, you need to understand the mechanics of a fence post hole. In the previous module, you dug your holes to the correct depth—typically below the frost line and deep enough to bury about one-third of the total post length. However, a hole is more than just an empty cylinder of dirt. If water sits at the bottom of the hole, it will pool around the base of a wooden post and cause premature rot, or it can freeze and expand, heaving a concrete anchor out of the ground. To prevent this, you must create a drainage bed: 1. Add gravel: Pour about 4 to 6 inches of crushed gravel or pea gravel into the bottom of every post hole. 2. Tamp it down: Use the heavy end of your digging bar or a piece of scrap wood to compact the gravel until it is firm and level. This gravel bed acts as a French drain, allowing water to flow away from the base of the wooden post rather than pooling around it. A Realistic Scenario: Let’s say you are building a 6-foot-tall fence to keep out deer, which means your posts need to be 8 feet long (6 feet above ground, 2 feet below). You dug your holes 2 feet deep. After adding 6 inches of compacted gravel, your hole is now 18 inches deep. When you drop your 8-foot post in, 6.5 feet will stick out above the ground. This is perfectly fine, as you can always trim the tops of the posts to be perfectly even later, but it highlights why measuring twice is critical before the concrete is poured. 6. Attaching Horizontal Rails

Picture your fence posts standing tall in a row. After the sweat equity of digging holes, mixing concrete, and plumbing posts, you finally have a solid skeleton. But right now, that skeleton is just a row of isolated pillars. On their own, they can’t keep out a determined dog, block a deer, or stop a groundhog from waddling into your vegetable patch. To become an actual barrier, those posts need a spine. That spine is made of horizontal rails—the beams that connect the posts to one another and provide the backing for whatever fence covering you choose. Understanding the Role of Horizontal Rails Rails (sometimes called stringers or crossbeams) are the horizontal pieces of lumber that span the gap between your vertical posts. Their primary job is to tie the posts together, creating a rigid frame. Without rails, a fence has no structure to hold mesh, wire, or wooden pickets. Depending on the height and purpose of your fence, you will typically install either two or three rails: Top rail: Runs along the upper section of the fence. It defines the upper visual line of the fence and provides an anchoring point for the top of your mesh or pickets. Bottom rail: Runs near the ground. It keeps the bottom of your fence covering secure, preventing animals from pushing underneath and stopping the material from sagging over time. Middle rail (optional): Used for taller fences. If you are building a 7- to 8-foot fence to keep deer out, a middle rail is highly recommended to prevent the center of the fence from bowing under pressure. Measuring and Marking Rail Heights Before you cut a single piece of wood, you need to determine exactly where your rails will live on your posts. Consistency is critical here; if your top rails waver up and down, your final fence will look wavy and unprofessional. Determining Rail Placement Your rail placement depends on the animals you are trying to manage (as discussed in Planning Your Garden Fence) and the total height of your fence. Top Rail: Place this flush with the top of your posts, or a few inches below the top, depending on your design. If you are using wire mesh, placing the top rail an inch or two below the top of the mesh allows you to fold the mesh over the rail for a clean, snag-free edge. Bottom Rail: This should sit high enough off the ground to avoid constant contact with wet soil, which causes wood to rot quickly. A standard height is 6 to 12 inches off the ground. The barrier you choose—wooden pickets or wire mesh—determines not only how the fence looks, but also what animals it will stop, how much maintenance it will need, and how quickly you can finish the project. - Pickets give a classic, decorative look and work well for keeping out rabbits, groundhogs, and small dogs when spaced correctly. - Wire mesh (also called “fencing netting”) is the go‑to for deer, larger pets, or any pest‑control fence where visibility and flexibility are priorities. 8. Building and Hanging a Gate

Designing the Gate for Your Garden A gate is the most used part of a fence, so it deserves a little extra planning. Imagine Emma, who just finished a 150‑ft perimeter fence for her backyard garden. The fence keeps out deer and rabbits, but the opening where the garden path meets the driveway still needs a gate that will stay square, swing freely, and keep small pets from escaping. Before any sawdust hits the ground, answer these three questions: 1. What size will the gate be? - Measure the opening between the two existing posts (refer back to Measuring and Marking the Layout). - Add a clearance gap of ¼‑½ in. on each side so the gate does not bind against the posts. 2. What material matches the existing fence? - If the fence is a 2‑by‑4‑inch mesh (as recommended for dogs and pets), a matching wooden frame works best. - For a wire‑mesh fence, you can still use a wooden frame and attach the same mesh to the gate. 3. How will the gate be opened? - A single‑leaf gate (one swinging panel) is simplest for a small opening. - A double‑leaf gate (two panels) is better for wider spans but adds complexity. For Emma’s 3‑ft opening, a single‑leaf, square gate (3 ft × 3 ft) is ideal. Tip: Write the dimensions on a piece of paper and keep it next to your saw. Having the numbers in front of you reduces measurement errors that can cause the gate to be out of square later. 9. Finishing and Maintaining Your Fence

Preparing the Fence for Finishing Before you reach for a brush or roller, the fence must be clean, dry, and structurally sound. Skipping this prep work is the most common reason a protective coating fails within months. 1. Remove debris – Sweep away leaves, twigs, and any soil that has accumulated at the base of the posts. A handheld broom or a leaf blower works well. 2. Inspect for damage – Walk the entire perimeter and note any loose fasteners, cracked pickets, or rotted wood. These issues are addressed in later sections, but they must be fixed first; otherwise the stain will seep into gaps and look uneven. 3. Clean the wood surface – Mild soap solution – Mix a few drops of dish soap in warm water, scrub with a stiff‑bristled brush, then rinse thoroughly. Commercial wood cleaner – For stubborn grime or mildew, a product labeled “deck & fence cleaner” can be sprayed on and left to work according to the manufacturer’s directions. Allow to dry – Wood must be at least 24 hours dry (ideally 48 hours) before any coating is applied. In humid climates, a dehumidifier or a fan can speed up drying. Realistic scenario: Emma built a 7‑foot high wooden fence for deer protection last spring. After one summer, the lower 2 feet of the fence is covered in pine needles and a faint green mildew ring. By following the three‑step prep routine above, Emma can safely apply a new coat of sealant without the mildew bleeding through. --- Applying a Weatherproof Wood Stain or Sealant Why a coating matters Wood exposed to the elements absorbs moisture, which leads to swelling, rot, and insect intrusion. UV (ultraviolet) radiation breaks down lignin, causing the wood to gray and become brittle. A weatherproof coating creates a barrier that repels water and reflects harmful UV rays, dramatically extending the fence’s service life.
