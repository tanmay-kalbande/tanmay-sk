1. Foundations of Ayurveda

What Is Ayurveda? Imagine waking up with a nagging headache, feeling unusually cold, and noticing that you’re craving sweet, comforting foods. You might chalk it up to a “bad night’s sleep,” but what if the body were sending you a clearer signal—one that points to an underlying pattern rather than an isolated symptom? Ayurveda (pronounced ah‑yur‑VA‑da) is the ancient Indian science of life that interprets exactly those patterns. It views health as a dynamic state of harmony between the body, mind, and environment. When this harmony is disturbed, the body expresses the imbalance through symptoms, cravings, moods, and even skin texture. By learning the language of Ayurveda, you acquire a roadmap that helps you read those signals and restore equilibrium with simple, everyday practices. --- The Roots of Ayurveda: A Brief History - Origins: The word Ayurveda combines the Sanskrit words āyus (“life”) and veda (“knowledge”), literally “the science of life.” Its foundations are traced to the Vedic period of ancient India (c. 1500–500 BCE), a time when hymns, rituals, and philosophical insights were recorded in the Rig‑Veda. - Classical Texts: The systematic codification of Ayurvedic knowledge appears in three major treatises known as the Samhitas: 1. Charaka Samhita – focuses on internal medicine. 2. Sushruta Samhita – concentrates on surgery and anatomy. 3. Vagbhata’s Ashtanga Hridaya – integrates both and adds a practical, concise format. These texts were written in Sanskrit, compiled by physician‑scholars, and have been studied continuously for over two millennia. - Living Tradition: Unlike a static textbook, Ayurveda has evolved through oral transmission, regional practices, and interaction with other medical systems (e.g., Unani, Tibetan medicine). Modern Ayurveda still respects the classical principles while adapting to contemporary lifestyles, nutrition, and scientific research. - Global Reach: In the early 20th century, Ayurvedic concepts entered the West through scholars, travelers, and Indian diaspora communities. Today, Ayurvedic clinics, educational programs, and product lines exist worldwide, reflecting a growing interest in holistic, preventive health. --- The Three Doshas: Vata, Pitta, Kapha At the heart of Ayurvedic theory lies the concept of doshas—bio‑energetic forces that govern physiological and psychological functions. Think of doshas as the primary operating systems of the body. Each person carries a unique combination of the three, known as their prakṛti (constitution). Understanding Dosha Terminology - Dosha (plural doshas) – a Sanskrit term meaning “that which governs.” - Prakṛti – your innate, birth‑time dosha makeup; it remains relatively stable throughout life. - Vikāra – any deviation from the natural state of your prakṛti, i.e., an imbalance. 7. Stress, Anxiety, and Sleep Support

In the Ayurvedic tradition, the same underlying imbalances that fuel anxiety also disturb the natural sleep cycle. By recognizing the signs, selecting appropriate herbs, and building a calming routine, Maya (and anyone in a similar situation) can restore balance and enjoy restorative sleep. --- Recognizing Physical and Mental Signs of Stress and Insomnia Understanding the body‑mind signals is the first step toward effective self‑care. While the earlier chapters introduced the three doshas (Vata, Pitta, Kapha) and their influence on health, this section focuses on how stress and sleeplessness manifest in each. Physical Indicators - Tension & Restlessness – Muscle tightness, especially in the neck, shoulders, and lower back (often Vata‑dominant). - Headaches – Throbbing or “pin‑prick” pain, commonly linked to Vata or Pitta excess. - Digestive Disruption – Irregular bowel movements, bloating, or “butterfly” sensations in the stomach (Vata). - Temperature Fluctuations – Feeling unusually hot or cold, sweating at night (Pitta) or feeling heavy and sluggish (Kapha). - Skin Changes – Dry, flaky skin or increased acne breakouts (Vata/Pitta) that can accompany stress. When these signs appear together—especially when they interfere with sleep—they suggest a vata‑pitta disturbance that needs soothing, grounding, and cooling support. --- Calming Herbs for Stress Relief and Sleep Ayurveda offers a concise toolbox of herbs that target the nervous system, balance the doshas, and promote relaxation. Each entry should capture four essential pieces of information: 1. Date & Time – When you noticed the symptom and when you applied the remedy. 2. Symptom Description – Use plain language; note intensity (e.g., “dry cough, 3/10”). 3. Dosha Insight – Briefly record the dominant dosha you identified for that moment (refer to the dosha‑assessment tools from Foundations of Ayurveda). 4. Remedy Details – List the ingredient(s), preparation method, dosage, and any modifications. 5. Outcome & Follow‑up – After a set period (e.g., 30 minutes, 2 hours, next day), note changes in symptom intensity, new sensations, or side effects. Optional Extras These fields are not required for every entry but can enrich the data set: - Food & Drink – What you ate or drank in the preceding 4 hours (helps link digestive health from Digestive Health Remedies). - Mood / Stress Level – A quick check‑in (e.g., “calm,” “anxious”) ties into the Stress, Anxiety, and Sleep Support chapter. - Weather / Season – Temperature, humidity, or seasonal markers (Ayurveda emphasizes seasonal rhythms). - Sleep Quality – Hours slept and perceived restfulness, which often influences dosha balance.
