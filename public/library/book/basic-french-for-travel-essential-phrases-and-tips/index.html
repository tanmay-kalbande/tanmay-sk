1. Pronunciation and the French Alphabet

A Real‑World Spark: “Where’s the métro?” You’ve just stepped off the train at a bustling Paris station. A local gestures toward a sign that reads “Métro 1 – La Défense” and asks, « Où est le métro ? » (Where is the metro?). In the split‑second that follows, you need to recognize the letters on the sign, map them to their sounds, and repeat the word so the person knows you understood. Mastering the French alphabet and its pronunciation is the first key that unlocks every conversation you’ll have while traveling in France. --- The French Alphabet: Letters and Their Sounds French uses the same 26 letters as English, but many of them are pronounced differently, and a handful acquire special sounds when paired with diacritics (accent marks). 2. Greetings and Polite Expressions

You step off the Métro 1 – La Défense and the station’s echo fades into the bustling streets of Paris. A friendly‑looking vendor behind a stand of croissants smiles and says, « Bonjour ! ». You smile back, but you’re not sure whether to reply with “Bonjour” again, add a “please”, or ask his name. The next few minutes could set the tone for the whole day—knowing the right greeting, a polite “please”, and a quick self‑introduction will turn a simple exchange into a pleasant interaction. 1. Time‑Based Greetings French speakers adjust their greetings according to the time of day, just as English speakers say “good morning” or “good evening”. The core words are short, easy to pronounce, and follow the pronunciation rules you already practiced (silent final consonants, liaison, etc.). | Time of Day | Standard Greeting | Literal Translation | Pronunciation tip | |-------------|-------------------|---------------------|-------------------| | Morning – until about 12 pm | Bonjour | “Good day” | The final ‑r is silent; link the n to the following word if it starts with a vowel (liaison). | | Early afternoon – 12 pm to 5 pm | Bonjour (still common) or Bon après‑midi | “Good afternoon” | Après‑midi keeps the ‑d silent; the ‑s in après is pronounced /a.pʁɛ/. | | Evening – after 5 pm | Bonsoir | “Good evening” | The ‑r is silent; if you follow it with a vowel word, use liaison: Bonsoir ami. | | Night (when parting) | Bonne nuit | “Good night” | The ‑e in bonne is pronounced /bɔn/; ‑t in nuit is silent. | How to choose the right greeting 1. Arriving – Use Bonjour the moment you meet someone, even if you are on a train or entering a shop. 2. Leaving a venue after dusk – Switch to Bonsoir if you’re greeting someone after 5 pm. 3. Before bedtime – When you say goodbye late at night, Bonne nuit is appropriate; it’s not used as a casual hello. Tip: If you’re unsure, Bonjour works safely for most of the day. It is the most versatile greeting you’ll hear in cafés, hotels, and on the streets. 2. Mastering them will let you ask for anything—from a coffee to directions—without sounding abrupt. 2.1 “Please” – S’il vous plaît - S’il vous plaît (formal or plural) – Please - S’il te plaît (informal, singular) – Please Pronunciation: /sil vu plɛ/ – notice the silent ‑t in s’il and the liaison between vous and plaît (the ‑z sound is heard). 3. Numbers, Dates, and Time

1. A Real‑World Situation: The “Marché du Petit‑Café” Imagine you have just arrived in Paris and your stomach is growling. You wander into a bustling outdoor market, the marché du Petit‑Café, where stalls overflow with fresh fruit, cheese, baguettes, and souvenirs. To enjoy the experience you’ll need to: Ask the price of a ripe pomme (apple) and a slice of camembert. Pay the correct amount in euros. Confirm the day (is it a market‑day?) and the date (today’s 15 mai 2024). Ask the vendor what time the market closes, and understand the answer, whether it’s given in 24‑hour or 12‑hour style. Every one of those tasks hinges on the numbers, dates, and time expressions you will master in this chapter. Let’s dive in, building from the very first French numeral up to the full‑day clock. --- 2. The aroma of freshly baked croissants drifts out of a small café, and a handwritten menu hangs in the window. You’re ready to order, but the French words feel like a puzzle. This chapter will give you the pieces you need—vocabulary, phrases, and a step‑by‑step script—so you can read the menu, ask about ingredients, place a confident order, request recommendations, and settle the bill with the right amount of pourboire. --- 1. Decoding a French Menu 1.1 Common Menu Sections | French | English | Typical Placement | |--------|---------|-------------------| | Entrées | Starters / Appetizers | Top of the menu | | Plats | Main dishes | Middle | | Desserts | Desserts | Bottom | | Boissons | Drinks | Separate column or side panel | | Vins | Wines | Often beside Boissons | Tip: In many cafés, the menu is a single‑page list. Prices are written in euros, using a comma for the decimal separator: - 12,50 € (twelve euros and fifty cents) - 7 € (seven euros, no cents) When you see a price, pronounce the euro sign « euros » after the number: Douze euros cinquante → “12 € 50” 1.3 Recognising Key Food Terms Below are the most common words you’ll meet on a French menu. 8. Asking for Help and Directions

1. A Real‑World Snap‑Shot You’ve just stepped off the Métro 1 at Châtelet–Les Halles and the map on the wall looks like a maze. The tourist information desk is a few doors away, but you’re not sure which way to go. A passerby glances at your puzzled expression, and you have to ask: « Excusez‑moi, pourriez‑vous m’aider ? » In the next few minutes you’ll learn exactly how to turn that brief exchange into a clear set of directions that gets you from the metro to the Eiffel Tower—and how to ask for help again if anything goes wrong. --- 2. Getting Someone’s Attention Politely | French phrase | Literal translation | When to use | |---|---|---| | Excusez‑moi | Excuse me | To interrupt politely (already introduced in Greetings and Polite Expressions) | | Pardon | Sorry / Pardon me | Slightly more informal, works in a crowd | | Monsieur / Madame | Sir / Madam | Adds extra courtesy, especially with strangers | Tip: Pair the opener with a smile and a brief eye contact. In French culture, a friendly demeanor often encourages the other person to respond positively. --- 3. When You Didn’t Hear or Understand 3.1 Core Phrases for Clarification - « Pouvez‑vous répéter, s’il vous plaît ? » – “Could you repeat, please?” - « Je n’ai pas compris. » – “I didn’t understand.” - « Parlez plus lentement, s’il vous plaît. » – “Speak more slowly, please.” - « Comment ? » – “What?” (very informal, best reserved for familiar settings) All of these contain polite particles (s’il vous plaît) that you already know. Notice the liaison in « excusez‑moi » and « pouvez‑vous » – the final ‑z and ‑s are pronounced because the next word begins with a vowel sound. This mirrors the liaison rules discussed in the Pronunciation chapter. 3.2 Quick Practice Drill 1. 9. Cultural Etiquette and Common Traveler Phrases

1. A QUICK SCENARIO TO SET THE STAGE You’ve just arrived in Paris after a long‑haul flight. The airport’s barrio (terminal) is buzzing, and a friendly‑looking attendant hands you a carte of the city. She says, “Bienvenue à Paris !” and adds, “Si vous avez besoin d’aide, n’hésitez pas à me demander.” You smile, reply with a quick bonjour, and head toward the exit. On the way, a local family at a café greets you with a warm sourire and a quick bise (two cheek kisses). You pause, unsure whether to kiss or shake hands. Later, at a bistro, the waiter asks, “Vous désirez boire quelque chose ?” You answer, “Un verre d’eau, s’il vous plaît.” While waiting, you hear other diners say, “Ça marche” and “Pas de souci” as they chat. By the end of the evening you’ve learned three things: French greetings have subtle regional variations. Table manners are guided by a few simple rules that keep the meal pleasant for everyone. Adding a few idiomatic fillers makes you sound natural and eases conversation. The rest of this chapter unpacks those observations, gives you the exact phrases you’ll need, and shows how to practice them in short role‑plays. --- 2.
