1. Introduction to Resin Crafting

What Is Resin and Why Use It for Coasters? Imagine you’re holding a glossy, crystal-clear circle in your hand. It feels solid, cool to the touch, and can protect your furniture from rings left by cold drinks. This isn’t glass—it’s made from something far more versatile: resin. Resin is a liquid material that, when mixed with a hardener, transforms into a durable solid. For coasters, it’s a game-changer because it’s not only functional but also endlessly customizable—you can embed flowers, glitter, or even tiny messages inside. Let’s break down exactly what resin is, how it becomes a coaster, and why it’s such a popular choice for beginners and crafters alike. --- Understanding the Basics: What Is Epoxy Resin? At its core, epoxy resin is a two-part liquid system made up of a resin and a hardener. When these two components are mixed in the right proportions, a chemical reaction occurs. This reaction turns the liquid into a hard, plastic-like solid—a process called curing. Think of it like baking a cake: you need both the wet ingredients (resin) and the dry ones (hardener) in the right amounts. Without both, nothing happens. But when they combine, they create something entirely new. Epoxy resin is especially popular for coasters because it: - Is waterproof: Once cured, it won’t absorb spills or stains. - Is scratch-resistant: It holds up well under glasses and mugs. - Has a high-gloss finish: It looks polished and professional. - Can be colored or decorated: You can add pigments, dried flowers, or metallic flakes to personalize your creation. Unlike some other materials, epoxy resin cures completely clear, making it ideal for projects where transparency and depth are desired—like coasters that look like frozen water or stained glass. --- What Exactly Is a Coaster? A coaster is a small, flat object placed under a drink to protect surfaces from moisture rings, heat, and scratches. Traditional coasters might be made of cork, wood, or ceramic. But a resin coaster is something different: it’s a coaster made by pouring liquid resin into a mold or onto a flat surface, letting it harden, and then removing it once set. Resin coasters can be round, square, hexagonal, or any shape you like. They can be plain or decorated with embedded objects. And because resin is so versatile, you can even make coasters that glow in the dark or change color with temperature. Fun Fact: The first recorded coasters were made in the 17th century in England and were often silver or porcelain. Today, resin coasters are a modern twist on a classic necessity. --- Why Choose Resin for Coasters? Three Big Advantages If you’re new to crafting, you might wonder: Why use resin instead …

3. Understanding Resin Types and Their Properties

Resin Types: Finding the Right One for Your Coaster Imagine this: you’ve just mixed your resin, carefully poured it into the mold, and now you’re waiting for it to harden into a crystal-clear coaster. But instead of the glossy finish you expected, the surface is cloudy or the coaster never fully cures. Frustrating, right? The problem might not be your technique—it could be the type of resin you chose. Resin isn’t just one material—it’s a category with different formulas, each with its own strengths and quirks. For coaster making, the three most common types are epoxy resin, UV resin, and polyester resin. Each behaves differently in terms of curing time, clarity, and ease of use. Choosing the right one can mean the difference between a smooth, professional-looking coaster and a sticky, cloudy mess. Let’s break down these resins so you can pick the best one for your project. --- Epoxy Resin: The All-Around Choice for Beginners Epoxy resin is the most popular type of resin for coaster making, especially among beginners. It’s a two-part system: you mix the resin with a hardener, and together they chemically react to form a solid, durable material. Once cured, epoxy resin creates a high-gloss, waterproof finish that’s perfect for coasters. How Epoxy Resin Works When you mix epoxy resin with its hardener, the two liquids undergo a chemical reaction called curing. This reaction converts the liquid mixture into a solid plastic. The curing time depends on factors like temperature, humidity, and the specific brand of resin you’re using. Most epoxy resins take 24 to 72 hours to fully cure, though they may feel firm enough to demold within 12 to 24 hours. Clarity and Finish Epoxy resin is known for its crystal-clear finish, making it ideal for projects where you want to showcase colors, dyes, or embedded objects like dried flowers or glitter. Unlike some other resins, epoxy doesn’t yellow over time, so it stays clear for years. Hardener Ratio and Mixing One of the most important things to remember with epoxy resin is the mixing ratio. This is the proportion of resin to hardener required for the chemical reaction to work properly. For example, a common ratio is 2:1, meaning two parts resin to one part hardener. If you use too much hardener, the resin may cure too quickly and become brittle. If you use too little, it might never fully harden. Always check the product instructions for the exact ratio—it can vary between brands. Most epoxy resins come with a measuring cup or syringe to help you get the mix precise. 4. Safety First: Handling and Storing Resin Properly

Why Safety Isn’t Optional in Resin Crafting Resin is a magical material. It starts as a clear liquid, then transforms into a crystal-clear, waterproof, scratch-resistant surface that can hold your morning coffee without a worry. You’ve already learned how versatile it is—turning pigments, dried flowers, or glitter into a professional-looking coaster with just the right high-gloss finish. But here’s the catch: resin isn’t harmless. It’s made of chemicals that can irritate your skin, damage your eyes, and even cause breathing problems if you’re not careful. Imagine this: You’re excited to make your first resin coaster, but halfway through measuring, you spill a few drops on your bare arm. Within minutes, your skin starts to sting, turns red, and develops a rash that lingers for days. Or worse, you accidentally splash resin into your eye while mixing. These aren’t just hypothetical risks—they’re real scenarios that happen to beginner crafters every day. The good news? You can avoid these problems entirely. Nitrile holds up best. - Fit matters: Gloves should be snug but not too tight—you still need dexterity to measure and mix. - Replacement: Change gloves every 30–60 minutes, or immediately if they get a drop of resin on them. 6. Selecting and Preparing Coaster Molds

The Foundation of Your Design: Why the Mold Matters Imagine you’ve spent an hour meticulously mixing your epoxy resin and selecting the perfect combination of gold leaf and deep blue pigments. You pour your mixture with confidence, only to find that once the piece has finished curing, the edges are jagged, the bottom is sticky, or the coaster is so thin it feels flimsy. What went wrong? The resin didn't fail—the mold did. In resin crafting, the mold is more than just a container; it is the "negative" of your final artwork. Every imperfection, speck of dust, or scratch inside that mold will be mirrored on the surface of your finished coaster. Choosing the right mold and preparing it with precision is the difference between a piece that looks homemade and one that looks professional. Understanding Mold Materials Not all molds are created equal. Depending on the material, a mold will affect how easily your coaster releases, how long the mold lasts, and the final texture of the piece. Silicone (The Industry Standard) The vast majority of resin coasters are made using silicone molds. Silicone is a rubber-like polymer that is naturally flexible and heat-resistant. Pros: Easy Release: Because silicone is flexible, you can "peel" the mold away from the cured resin. Durability: High-quality silicone can be reused dozens of times. Variety: Available in almost any shape or texture. Cons: Dust Magnets: Silicone naturally attracts lint and hair, which can get trapped in your resin. Tearing: If you pull too hard during demolding, the edges can rip. Plastic/Acrylic (Hard Molds) These are rigid molds often used for industrial production or very specific geometric shapes. Pros: Perfect Walls: They provide extremely crisp, straight edges. Stability: They don't bulge or warp under the weight of the resin. Cons: Difficult Release: Because they don't flex, the resin can "bond" to the walls. You almost always need a chemical release agent (a specialized spray or liquid) to prevent the resin from sticking permanently. Fragility: They can crack if dropped. DIY Molds (Tape and Plastic) Some beginners create "custom" molds using plastic containers or by building walls with heavy-duty packing tape. Pros: Cost: Virtually free. Customization: You can make a coaster of any size. Cons: Leaking: Resin is thin; it will find any microscopic gap in your tape and leak onto your workspace. High Effort: Requires significant preparation to ensure the walls are leak-proof and the bottom is level. Identifying Mold Types and Their Uses When shopping for molds, you will encounter various styles. The shape you choose dictates the "vibe" of your coasters and how they function in a home. 7. Measuring and Mixing Resin Correctly

The "Sticky" Truth About Precision Imagine this: You’ve spent an hour carefully preparing your workspace, your coaster molds are pristine, and you’ve picked out the perfect decorative elements. You mix your resin, pour it into the mold, and head to bed. You wake up the next morning, excited to demold your first creation, only to find that the resin is a tacky, sticky mess. It never fully hardened. No matter how long you wait, it remains like a piece of half-dried glue. What happened? In most cases, the culprit isn't a "bad batch" of resin, but a tiny mathematical error. Epoxy resin is not like paint or glue where "a little extra" doesn't matter. It is a chemical reaction. If the ratio of resin to hardener is off by even a small percentage, the chemical bond cannot complete itself. This results in a failure to cure, leaving you with a project that cannot be saved. Precision isn't just a recommendation; it is the fundamental requirement for a successful resin coaster. Understanding the Mixing Ratio Before you touch your measuring cups, you must identify the specific mixing ratio required for your brand of resin. The ratio is the recipe that tells you exactly how much resin to combine with how much hardener to trigger the chemical reaction. There are two primary types of ratios you will encounter: Volume-Based Ratios These are measured by "how much space" the liquid takes up. You will see these listed as 1:1 or 2:1. 1:1 Ratio: You use equal parts resin and hardener (e.g., 100ml of resin and 100ml of hardener). 2:1 Ratio: You use twice as much resin as hardener (e.g., 200ml of resin and 100ml of hardener). Weight-Based Ratios These are measured using a digital scale. Because resin and hardener have different densities (one is "heavier" than the other even if they take up the same amount of space), a 1:1 volume ratio is almost never a 1:1 weight ratio. Never use a volume measurement for a weight-based resin, or vice versa. Pro Tip: Always check the back of your bottles or the manufacturer's website. If the instructions say "By Volume," use a measuring cup. If they say "By Weight," use a scale. Tools for Accurate Measurement Using the wrong tool is the fastest way to ruin a project. To ensure your coaster comes out crystal clear and rock-hard, use the following tools: For Volume Measuring Graduated Mixing Cups: Use clear plastic cups with milliliter (ml) or ounce (oz) markings printed on the side. 8. Adding Colors and Decorative Elements to Resin

Bringing Your Coasters to Life: The Magic of Color and Texture Imagine two coasters sitting side-by-side. Both are perfectly clear, glass-like circles of epoxy resin. One is a blank slate; the other is a swirling nebula of deep midnight blue and shimmering gold, with a single, delicate white daisy frozen in time at its center. The chemistry—the mixing of resin and hardener—is identical for both. The only difference is the intentional addition of color and decorative elements. Adding aesthetics to your resin is where the science of chemistry transforms into the art of crafting. Whether you want a minimalist, professional look or a bold, eclectic statement piece, the way you introduce color and objects will determine the final character of your work. Understanding Your Color Palette: Types of Colorants Not all colors behave the same way in resin. Depending on the product you choose, you can achieve a "stained glass" transparency, a solid "plastic" look, or a metallic shimmer. Liquid Resin Dyes Resin dyes are specifically formulated to bond with epoxy. They are typically the most reliable way to change the color of your resin without affecting the curing process. Transparent Dyes: These act like watercolors. They change the hue of the resin but allow light to pass through. These are ideal for "ocean" effects or gemstone-style coasters. Opaque Dyes: These contain pigments that block light. If you want a coaster that looks like a solid piece of colored acrylic or ceramic, use opaque dyes. Alcohol Inks Alcohol inks are high-pigment, fast-drying dyes dissolved in alcohol. They are beloved by resin artists for their ability to create "blooms" and organic, flowing patterns. The Effect: Because alcohol is less dense than resin, these inks often dance and swirl, creating wispy, cloud-like formations. The Warning: Use these sparingly. Adding too much alcohol to your mix can occasionally interfere with the resin's ability to harden properly. Mica Powders Mica is a naturally occurring mineral that has been ground into a fine powder. Unlike dyes, mica does not "stain" the resin; instead, it consists of tiny shimmering particles suspended in the liquid. The Effect: Mica creates a pearlescent, metallic, or shimmering finish. When you move the coaster in the light, the color appears to shift. The Texture: Mica is inherently opaque. Even a small amount will hide whatever is underneath the resin. Pigment Pastes These are highly concentrated, thick pastes. They are the "heavy hitters" of the color world. The Effect: They provide the most intense, saturated colors possible. A tiny dot of pigment paste can color a large volume of resin. Best Use: Use these when you need a completely solid, matte, or high-gloss bold color with zero transparency. --- The Art of …

9. Pouring Resin into Molds Without Creating Bubbles

The "Invisible" Enemy: Why Bubbles Happen Imagine this: You’ve spent an hour carefully measuring your epoxy resin and hardener, you’ve mixed in a stunning swirl of metallic pigments, and you’ve meticulously placed dried flowers in your mold. You pour the resin, wait 24 hours for it to cure, and peel back the silicone to find... a thousand tiny, frozen bubbles trapped inside your coaster. It looks less like a professional piece of art and more like a sponge. This is the most common frustration for beginners. These bubbles are essentially pockets of air that were whipped into the resin during the mixing process or trapped during the pour. Because resin is viscous—meaning it is thick and sticky—air bubbles struggle to travel upward to the surface on their own. Once the resin cures, those bubbles are locked in forever. The good news is that achieving a "glass-like" finish isn't about luck; it's about controlling how the resin enters the mold. The Art of the Slow Pour The way you move the resin from your mixing cup into your coaster mold determines whether the air stays trapped or escapes. If you dump the resin in quickly, you create turbulence, which forces more air into the liquid. The "High and Thin" Technique The most effective way to minimize bubbles is to pour the resin in a thin, steady stream from a slight height. When resin falls through the air in a thin stream, the liquid stretches. This stretching action helps larger air bubbles pop or merge and rise to the surface before they even hit the mold. If you pour the resin directly against the side of the cup or "glug" it into the mold, you are essentially trapping air pockets in the flow. How to execute the perfect pour: 1. Hold your mixing cup about 2 to 4 inches above the mold. 2. Tilt the cup slightly to create a narrow "lip" for the resin to flow over. 3. Pour slowly into the center of the mold, allowing the resin to naturally spread outward toward the edges. Avoiding the "Splash" Avoid pouring the resin directly onto decorative elements like glitter or dried flowers. If you "blast" your inclusions with a heavy stream of resin, you can push air underneath them, creating "silver bubbles" (tiny pockets of air trapped under a flat surface). Instead, pour the resin into the empty space of the mold and gently nudge your decorations into place with a tool. 10. Removing Air Bubbles from Resin After Pouring

The "Invisible" Enemy: Dealing with Post-Pour Bubbles Imagine this: you’ve carefully measured your epoxy resin and hardener, mixed them to perfection, and poured your favorite pigments and dried flowers into your coaster mold. Everything looks beautiful. But as you step back to admire your work, you notice a cluster of tiny, shimmering spheres clinging to the sides of the mold, or a few stubborn bubbles floating right on the surface. These bubbles are the "invisible enemy" of a professional-looking finish. While we covered how to minimize them during the mixing and pouring phases in the previous chapter, the reality of resin crafting is that some air almost always finds its way into the mold. The good news? You don't have to live with them. As long as your resin is still in its liquid state, you have several effective ways to eliminate these imperfections and achieve that crystal-clear, high-gloss finish. Using Heat to Pop Surface Bubbles The most efficient way to remove bubbles from the surface of your resin is by applying a concentrated burst of heat. Heat lowers the viscosity (the thickness or "syrupiness") of the resin, which allows trapped air bubbles to expand and rise to the surface more quickly, where they pop and disappear. The Heat Gun Method A heat gun is a handheld tool that blows hot air. It is the gold standard for beginner resin artists because it covers a wide area quickly. 1. Set the Temperature: If your heat gun has adjustable settings, use a low-to-medium setting. Too much heat can cause the resin to overheat or "flash cure" (harden too quickly), which can ruin your piece. 2. The "Sweeping" Motion: Hold the gun about 3 to 6 inches above the resin. Move it in a steady, sweeping motion across the surface. Never hold the heat gun in one spot for more than a second, as this can create a divot or burn the resin. 3. Watch the Pop: You will see the bubbles shimmer and then vanish almost instantly. Once the surface is clear, move to the next section. The Torch Method A small butane torch (often called a culinary torch or crème brûlée torch) provides a more intense, pinpointed heat source. 1. The "Quick Pass": Pass the flame quickly over the surface of the resin. You aren't trying to "cook" the resin; you are simply using the heat from the flame to break the surface tension. 2. Precision Work: Torches are excellent for popping bubbles that are clinging to the edges of the coaster mold or trapped in small gaps around decorative elements like glitter or metallic foils. 3. 11. Curing Resin: Timing, Temperature, and Troubleshooting

The "Wait" That Defines Your Work Imagine this: You’ve spent an hour carefully measuring your resin and hardener, swirling in your favorite pigments, and meticulously popping every single bubble. Your coasters look perfect in the mold. Eager to see the final result, you reach in after 12 hours and gently peel back the silicone. Instead of a hard, glass-like surface, your finger sinks into the resin like it's warm taffy. Or worse, the surface is covered in a sticky, oily film that won't wipe away. This is the frustration of a failed cure. In resin crafting, the "pour" is the art, but the "cure" is the science. Understanding how your resin transforms from a liquid to a solid is the difference between a professional-grade coaster and a sticky mess that has to be thrown away. Understanding the Curing Process When you mixed your resin and hardener in Chapter 7, you didn't just create a mixture; you started a chemical reaction. This process is called polymerization. Essentially, the molecules in the resin and the hardener link together to form long, strong chains. As these chains interconnect, the liquid thickens, hardens, and eventually becomes the durable, scratch-resistant plastic we love. However, this reaction doesn't happen instantly, and it doesn't happen all at once. Curing Time vs. Full Cure Time One of the most common mistakes beginners make is confusing "set time" with "full cure." If you demold your coasters too early, you risk warping the edges or leaving fingerprints in the surface. Curing Time (or Set Time) This is the window when the resin transitions from a liquid to a solid. Depending on the brand of epoxy resin you are using, this usually takes anywhere from 12 to 24 hours. At this stage, the resin feels hard to the touch and can usually be removed from the mold (though we will cover the specifics of that in a later chapter). However, the chemical reaction is still happening beneath the surface. Full Cure Time This is the time it takes for the resin to reach its maximum hardness, strength, and chemical stability. For most resins, this takes 7 to 30 days. Even if the coaster feels rock-hard after 24 hours, it is still "off-gassing" and strengthening. Why the difference matters: If you apply a sealant, paint, or heavy pressure to a coaster that has "set" but hasn't "fully cured," you may find that the finish peels off or the resin develops permanent indentations. Because it relies on a chemical reaction, anything that affects the movement of molecules—specifically temperature and humidity—will change how fast your coasters harden. 12. Demolding and Finishing Your Resin Coasters

The Moment of Truth: The Art of the Reveal You’ve spent hours measuring, mixing, and carefully pouring. You’ve waited patiently through the curing process, perhaps glancing at your molds every few hours. Now comes the most rewarding part of the process: the reveal. However, for a beginner, this is also where the most heartbreak can happen. A rushed pull can lead to a torn edge; a premature demold can result in a coaster that bends or sticks to the silicone. The difference between a piece that looks "homemade" and one that looks "professional" often comes down to what happens in the ten minutes after the resin leaves the mold. Mastering the Demold Demolding is the process of removing your cured resin piece from its mold. While it seems straightforward, there is a technique to doing it without compromising the structural integrity or the surface finish of your coaster. Timing the Pull Before you touch your mold, you must be certain the resin is fully cured. As discussed in Curing Resin: Timing, Temperature, and Troubleshooting, resin goes through stages. Even if the surface feels hard to the touch, the center may still be soft. The Thumb Test: Gently press your thumb into the thickest part of the resin. If it gives even slightly or feels "tacky" (sticky), leave it alone. If it feels like hard plastic, you are ready to proceed. The Step-by-Step Demolding Process To ensure a clean release, follow these steps: 1. Stretch the Mold: Gently pull the edges of the silicone mold away from the resin. Silicone is flexible; by stretching the walls outward, you break the vacuum seal between the resin and the mold. 2. Work from the Edges: Do not try to pull the coaster straight up from the center. Instead, peel the mold back from the perimeter toward the middle. 3. The "Pop" Technique: If the coaster is stubborn, flip the mold over and gently push the resin from the bottom. You will often hear a satisfying "pop" as the air enters the gap and releases the piece. 4. Avoid Prying: Never use a screwdriver, knife, or metal tool to pry the resin out. This will leave permanent gouges in both your coaster and your expensive silicone mold. Common Demolding Mistakes The Premature Pull: Removing the piece too early. This often results in "fingerprints" permanently pressed into the resin or a piece that warps as it finishes curing outside the mold. Over-Tugging: Pulling too hard on a stuck piece. Why Seal Your Resin Coasters? Imagine this: You’ve spent hours carefully mixing your epoxy resin, embedding delicate dried flowers, and waiting patiently for the curing process to finish. While we established in Understanding Resin Types and Their Properties that resin is naturally waterproof and relatively durable, it is not invincible. Sealing is the process of applying a secondary protective layer over the cured resin. Think of it as a "sacrificial layer." Instead of the heat from a mug or the friction of a sliding glass wearing down your actual artwork, those forces hit the sealant first. There are three primary reasons why sealing is a critical final step: 1. Heat Resistance: Many resins can soften slightly when exposed to very high temperatures (this is known as the heat deflection temperature). A specialized sealant can provide a thermal barrier, preventing "coaster rings" from forming. 2. Scratch Prevention: While resin is scratch-resistant, it is not scratch-proof. Repeatedly sliding ceramic mugs across the surface will eventually dull that high-gloss finish. A hard sealant fills in microscopic imperfections and adds a layer of armor. 3. UV Protection: Even if you used resin with UV stabilizers, prolonged exposure to direct sunlight can cause some resins to yellow over time. Certain sealants contain UV inhibitors that act like sunscreen for your coasters. Choosing the Right Sealant Not all sealants are created equal. 14. Troubleshooting Common Resin Coaster Problems

When Things Don't Go According to Plan Imagine this: You’ve spent an hour carefully mixing your epoxy resin, meticulously placing dried flowers in a beautiful pattern, and waiting patiently for the curing process to finish. You reach for the mold, peel it back with excitement, and... your heart sinks. Maybe the surface is sticky to the touch. Maybe there are tiny, stubborn bubbles trapped right against the flowers. Or perhaps the crystal-clear resin you expected has turned a strange, cloudy shade of milky white. First, take a deep breath. Every single resin artist—from the absolute beginner to the seasoned professional—has experienced "failed" pours. In the world of resin crafting, a mistake isn't a ruined project; it's a diagnostic tool. Most common problems are caused by a few specific variables: temperature, mixing ratios, or environmental contaminants. Once you know how to identify the cause, you can almost always fix the piece or ensure it never happens again. Diagnosing Surface Issues The surface of your coaster is the first thing people notice. When it isn't a smooth, high-gloss finish, it's usually due to one of three issues: stickiness, unevenness, or "fish eyes." The Sticky or Tacky Surface If you demold your coaster and it feels like the back of a sticker or a piece of tape, you have a sticky cure. This is one of the most common frustrations for beginners. The Likely Causes: Incorrect Mixing Ratio: As discussed in Measuring and Mixing Resin Correctly, epoxy resin requires a precise chemical balance between the resin and the hardener. If you were off by even a small amount, some molecules never find a partner to bond with, leaving unreacted resin on the surface. Incomplete Mixing: You may have measured correctly, but didn't stir thoroughly. If "streaks" of unmixed hardener remain, those areas will stay liquid or tacky. Cold Environment: If the room temperature dropped significantly during the curing process, the chemical reaction may have slowed down or stalled. How to Fix It: 1. The Wipe Down: If the stickiness is very slight, try wiping the surface with a lint-free cloth dampened with isopropyl alcohol. 2. The Top-Coat Fix: If the piece is mostly hard but tacky on top, the best solution is to lightly sand the surface and pour a thin, fresh layer of correctly mixed resin over the top. This "doming" layer will bond to the old layer and provide a hard, glossy finish. 3. The Heat Treatment: In some cases, using a heat gun (carefully!) can help push the curing process forward, though this is less reliable than a new top coat. 15. Creative Variations and Advanced Techniques for Resin Coasters

Elevating Your Art: From Basic Pours to Signature Styles Imagine walking into a room and placing a drink on a coaster that doesn't just protect the table, but looks like a slice of a polished amethyst geode or a captured moment of a spring meadow. Up until now, you have mastered the technical foundations: mixing your epoxy resin and hardener, managing bubbles, and achieving a smooth cure. Now that the "science" of resin is second nature, you can shift your focus to the "art." The beauty of resin is its versatility; it acts as both a medium and a preservative. By manipulating how you layer your pours and what you embed within the liquid, you can transform a simple functional object into a piece of home decor. Creating Depth with Layering and Geode Styles Most beginner coasters are "single pours," where all the resin and color are added at once. To create a professional, three-dimensional look, you will use a technique called layering. The Layering Process Layering involves pouring a thin amount of resin, allowing it to partially or fully cure, and then pouring another layer on top. This prevents different colors from blending together and allows you to "suspend" objects at different heights. 1. The Base Layer: Pour your first layer (usually a solid color or a background texture). 2. The Wait: Refer back to your knowledge of Curing Resin; you can either wait for a full cure or wait until the resin reaches a "tacky" state (like the feel of strong tape). A tacky layer allows the next pour to bond chemically, while a fully cured layer may require a light sanding to ensure the new resin sticks. 3. The Detail Layer: Add your focal points—such as a photo or a specific pigment swirl—on top of the first layer. 4. The Top Coat: Finish with a clear layer of resin to create a protective, high-gloss dome. The Geode Look Geode coasters mimic the look of natural crystalline rock formations. This style relies on "clustering" materials rather than mixing them evenly. The Ring Effect: Pour a circle of colored resin in the center, then a ring of a different color around it, and finally a clear or white edge. Adding Texture: To get the "crystal" look, do not mix your glitter or crushed glass into the resin. Instead, pour the resin first, then sprinkle metallic foils or coarse glitter onto the surface in concentrated clusters. The "Crushed Glass" Technique: Use small pieces of mirrored glass or clear acrylic shards. Place them along the inner edge of your design to create a jagged, mineral-like border.
