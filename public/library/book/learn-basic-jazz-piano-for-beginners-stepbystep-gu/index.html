1. Introduction to Jazz Piano and the Keyboard

The Piano: Your First Step into Jazz Imagine walking into a dimly lit jazz club on a Friday night. The air hums with anticipation as the pianist at the grand piano begins to play. The first notes ripple through the room—not sharp or rigid like a classical piece, but fluid and alive, with a rhythm that feels like it’s swaying slightly off the beat. That’s the sound of jazz piano, and it starts with the very instrument under your fingers. If you’ve never touched a piano before, the keyboard might feel overwhelming at first. But here’s a secret: the piano is the most logical instrument in the world. Every note has its place, and once you understand that place, you’re already on your way to making music. In this chapter, you’ll learn how to navigate the keyboard, feel the difference between classical and jazz piano, and set yourself up for success with good habits from the start. --- Meet the Keyboard: A Map of Notes The piano keyboard is made up of repeating patterns of white keys and black keys. To understand how these keys relate to music, we need to know a few basic terms: - Pitch: How high or low a note sounds (like the squeak of a mouse versus the roar of a lion). - Note names: The letters A through G, which repeat across the keyboard. - Octave: The distance between two notes with the same letter name (for example, from A to the next A). The Pattern of White Keys Start by looking at the white keys. They follow a simple repeating pattern of seven letters: A – B – C – D – E – F – G After G, the pattern starts again at A. This sequence is the musical alphabet. Now, look at the black keys. They sit between some of the white keys in a pattern that repeats every 12 notes. This pattern is the foundation of all Western music. The Black Keys: Sharps and Flats The black keys serve two purposes, depending on the musical context: - Sharp (): Raises a note by one half step (the smallest distance between two notes on the piano). - Flat (♭): Lowers a note by one half step. Here’s how they fit in: - Between A and B: There’s no black key. The notes are a whole step apart. - Between B and C: Also no black key—another whole step. - Between C and D, D and E, F and G, G and A: Each has a black key in between, called a sharp or flat note. 2. Music Theory Foundations for Jazz Piano

The Hidden Map of Sound: Notes, Keys, and the Jazz Landscape Imagine you step into a jazz club, the air thick with the hum of conversation and the scent of coffee. The bandleader nods, and the pianist begins. Without a word, they launch into a tune you’ve never heard—but somehow, you can sing along. How? Because the notes, the chords, the way they flow—it’s all built on patterns anyone can learn. Jazz isn’t magic. It’s a map. And today, you start drawing it. This isn’t about memorizing rules. It’s about seeing the keyboard not as a sea of white and black keys, but as a landscape of relationships—where every note has a family, every step has a direction, and every sound tells a story. By the end of this chapter, you’ll be able to look at any key on the piano and know not just what it is, but where it lives in the grand design of jazz harmony. Let’s begin by learning the language. --- The 12 Musical Notes: Your Alphabet in Sound Every piece of music is built from a set of 12 unique sounds called pitches. On the piano, these are represented by the 7 white keys (A, B, C, D, E, F, G) and 5 black keys (the sharps and flats). But here’s the first key insight (no pun intended): the black keys have two names. For example, the black key between C and D is called C (C sharp) or D♭ (D flat). These are called enharmonic equivalents—two names for the same pitch. Why does this matter in jazz? Because jazz thrives on flexibility. A C can sound bluesy. A D♭ can sound lush. Knowing both names helps you read sheet music, communicate with other musicians, and understand chord symbols later on. Visualizing the Notes Let’s map the 12 notes in order, ascending from C: C → C/D♭ → D → D/E♭ → E → F → F/G♭ → G → G/A♭ → A → A/B♭ → B → C Each step is called a semitone or half step—the smallest distance between two notes on the piano. - The white keys are natural notes. - The black keys are accidentals (sharps or flats). - Between B and C, and E and F, there are no black keys—so the step from B to C is a semitone, and so is E to F. 🎹 Try this: Play every key in a row from C to C. 3. Basic Jazz Chords: Triads and Inversions

What Even Are Jazz Chords? Picture this: You’re at a small jazz club, and the pianist is comping behind the soloist. Their left hand crunches out rich, full chords while their right hand dances with sparkling melodies. Those chords—sounding lush, colorful, and effortlessly cool—are the foundation of jazz harmony. But here’s the secret: every single one of those chords starts with a triad. A triad is the simplest chord you can build, made of just three notes. Mastering triads and their inversions unlocks the door to jazz harmony. You’ll soon see how these three-note building blocks expand into the vibrant chords you hear in jazz standards. In this chapter, you’ll learn: - What a triad is and how to construct it - How to play major, minor, diminished, and augmented triads in root position - How to play first and second inversions of triads - How to move between triads smoothly - How triads connect to the harmony you’ll play in jazz By the end, you’ll have a solid foundation to build richer chords—and eventually, full jazz voicings—with confidence. --- Triads: The Building Blocks of Jazz Chords What Is a Triad? A triad is a chord made of three notes stacked in thirds. “Stacked in thirds” means you skip one note in the musical alphabet to form each new note. For example, starting on C: - C (first note) - Skip D → E (second note) - Skip F → G (third note) So, C–E–G is a C major triad. Triads are the simplest form of chord and the starting point for almost all jazz harmony. Even when pianists play big, rich chords, they’re often built by stacking additional notes onto triads. --- How Triads Are Constructed: Intervals in Thirds To build any triad, you need to understand intervals—the distance between two notes. Specifically, you’ll use thirds, which are intervals spanning two or three semitones (half steps). There are two types of thirds: - Major third: 4 semitones (e.g., C to E) - Minor third: 3 semitones (e.g., C to E♭) A triad is built by stacking two thirds on top of a starting note (called the root). Here’s how it works: 1. Start with the root (e.g., C). 2. Add a third above the root. 3. Add another third above the second note. That gives you three notes total: root, third, fifth. The quality of the triad (major, minor, etc.) depends on whether the thirds are major or minor. --- The Four Triad Types There are four basic triad types, each with a distinct sound and emotional color. 4. Seventh Chords: The Heart of Jazz Harmony

From Triads to Seventh Chords: Adding the "Jazz Spice" Imagine you're sitting at a piano, playing a simple C major chord. It sounds bright, stable, and familiar—like a clear sunny day. Now, add one more note: the B between the C and the next higher C. Suddenly, the chord feels open, colorful, and slightly unresolved, like the sky just before sunrise. That added note is the seventh, and this chord is now a C major seventh chord—a foundational sound in jazz harmony. In earlier chapters, we built chords using three notes—triads—like C-E-G forming a C major triad. Jazz doesn’t stop there. It adds another layer of depth and movement by stacking a fourth note on top: the seventh. This simple addition transforms stable chords into rich, expressive, and highly functional building blocks of jazz harmony. Seventh chords are the "heart of jazz harmony" because they create tension, color, and direction—qualities essential to the jazz sound. Whether you're comping behind a soloist or improvising yourself, understanding seventh chords gives you access to the language of jazz. Let’s explore them step by step. --- What Is a Seventh Chord? A seventh chord is a four-note chord built by stacking thirds—the same interval used to build triads. Just as a triad has a root, third, and fifth, a seventh chord adds a seventh above the root. Mathematically, a seventh chord spans 10 semitones from the root to the seventh. In terms of intervals: - Root to third: major or minor third - Root to fifth: perfect fifth - Root to seventh: major seventh or minor seventh This gives us the core structure of all seventh chords. 🔍 Interval reminder: A third is the distance of two or three semitones (e.g., C to E is a major third, C to E♭ is a minor third). A seventh is the distance from the root to the note a seventh above (e.g., C to B is a major seventh; C to B♭ is a minor seventh). So, every seventh chord is defined by two key qualities: 1. The quality of its triad (major or minor) 2. The quality of its seventh (major or minor) This creates four main types of seventh chords in jazz: - Major seventh (maj7) - Minor seventh (m7) - Dominant seventh (7) - Half-diminished seventh (m7♭5) We’ll explore each one in detail. --- The Four Core Seventh Chords in Jazz Let’s build each chord from the root note C to hear their distinct flavors. 1. 5. Jazz Voicings: Shell Voicings and 3-Note Shapes

Why Good Voicings Make You Sound Like a Jazz Pianist Imagine sitting down at a piano and playing a simple chord like C major—just three white keys: C, E, and G. That’s nice, but it doesn’t sound like jazz. Now play a C major 7th chord—C, E, B. Still clean and clear, but now you’re starting to hear jazz. But here’s the thing: you don’t need to play all seven notes of a chord to make it feel rich and jazzy. In fact, with just three carefully chosen notes, you can outline the function of a chord and make it groove. That’s what voicings are all about. A voicing is how you arrange the notes of a chord across the keyboard. It’s not just about which notes you play, but where and how you play them. A good voicing can make a chord sound warm, open, mysterious, or punchy—just by changing the order and spacing of the notes. In jazz, we often use compact voicings—shapes that fit neatly under the hand and clearly communicate the chord’s identity. And one of the simplest, most powerful tools in the jazz pianist’s toolkit is the shell voicing. --- What Is a Shell Voicing? A shell voicing is a compact three-note chord shape that includes the root, the third, and the 7th of a chord. That’s it. Three notes. No fifth (though you can add it later). Just enough to define the chord’s quality and function. Why skip the fifth? Because in the context of jazz harmony, the third tells us if the chord is major or minor, and the 7th tells us if it’s a dominant, major 7th, or minor 7th chord. Together, they give us the essence of the chord—its identity—without clutter. For example: - A C major 7 shell voicing: C (root), E (major 3rd), B (major 7th) - A C minor 7 shell voicing: C (root), E♭ (minor 3rd), B♭ (minor 7th) - A C dominant 7 shell voicing: C (root), E (major 3rd), B♭ (minor 7th) Notice how the third and seventh change based on the chord type, while the root stays the same. That’s the magic of shell voicings—they’re efficient, clear, and easy to move around. --- Building Shell Voicings Step by Step Let’s build shell voicings from the ground up, starting with major and minor chords, then adding the 7th. 1. 6. Basic Jazz Progressions: ii-V-I and the Blues

Why These Two Progressions Matter Imagine you're at a jazz club, and the band starts playing a tune you've never heard. Within the first few bars, your ears recognize a familiar pattern—even if you can't name it. That pattern is likely a ii-V-I or a 12-bar blues, the building blocks of jazz harmony. These progressions appear in thousands of jazz standards, from "Autumn Leaves" to "Now’s the Time." Learning them isn’t just about playing exercises—it’s about unlocking the language of jazz itself. In this chapter, you’ll stop treating chords as isolated blocks and start hearing them as connected stories. The ii-V-I is the most common chord sequence in jazz, acting like a musical "home base" that gives structure to improvisation. The 12-bar blues is the foundation of an entire genre, flexible enough to swing hard or lay back with a smoky feel. By mastering these, you’ll instantly recognize them in recordings, fake books, and jam sessions—and you’ll have the tools to play along confidently. --- The ii-V-I Progression: Your First Jazz "Roadmap" What is a ii-V-I? A ii-V-I progression is a sequence of three chords that creates a strong sense of arrival and resolution. It’s built using the diatonic chords (chords that naturally occur in a key) in this order: 1. ii chord (supertonic) – A minor 7th chord (e.g., Dm7 in the key of C major) 2. V chord (dominant) – A dominant 7th chord (e.g., G7 in the key of C major) 3. I chord (tonic) – A major 7th chord (e.g., Cmaj7 in the key of C major) Why "ii-V-I" and not "2-5-1"? Jazz musicians use Roman numerals to describe chords by their scale degree. Lowercase "ii" means a minor chord, uppercase "V" means a dominant chord, and "I" means a major chord. This system works in any key. --- The Role of Each Chord Let’s break down why each chord fits where it does: - ii chord (minor 7th) - Acts as a preparation chord. 7. Rhythm and Swing: The Jazz Pulse

Feel the Swing – A Real‑World Snapshot Imagine you’re at a cozy coffee‑house jam night. The pianist at the piano, eyes closed, rolls a gentle “doo‑doo‑doo‑doo” that feels like a relaxed heartbeat. A saxophonist joins in, and the whole room sways as if pulled by an invisible current. That invisible current is swing – the rhythmic pulse that makes jazz feel alive. For a beginner, capturing that pulse is the first step toward playing authentic‑sounding jazz piano. In this chapter you’ll learn what swing really means, how it differs from the straight rhythms you may already know, and how to practice it with a metronome, a walking bass line, and syncopated chord “comping”. --- Straight Rhythm vs. Swing Rhythm What “Straight” Means In many pop, rock, or classical pieces you’ve likely counted 1‑2‑3‑4 with each beat divided evenly into eighth‑note pairs (1‑&‑2‑&‑3‑&‑4‑&). The two eighth notes in each beat have equal duration – think of a marching drum that clicks “ta‑ta, ta‑ta”. The Swing Feel Swing unequals those eighth‑note pairs. Instead of “ta‑ta”, the rhythm becomes “long–short” (often approximated as a triplet feel). If you write a measure in 4/4 swing, each beat is divided into three equal parts (triplet subdivision), and the first two parts are combined to make the “long” note, while the third part becomes the “short” note. - Long note ≈ 2/3 of a beat (the first two triplet subdivisions). - Short note ≈ 1/3 of a beat (the third triplet subdivision). The exact ratio can vary from 3:1 (very “lazy” swing) to 1.5:1 (tight, almost straight). Most early‑stage jazz players aim for a ≈2:1 feel, which sounds natural without sounding overly “lopsided”. Listening Test Play a simple two‑measure melody twice: first with straight eighths, then with swing eighths. Notice how the swing version propels forward, encouraging your body to sway. That forward motion is the essence of jazz groove. --- Using a Metronome to Build the Swing Pulse A metronome is your most reliable practice partner. It keeps the beat steady while you focus on the subdivision (the long–short pattern). Step‑by‑Step Metronome Exercise 1. Set the Metronome to a comfortable tempo (e.g., 80 BPM). 2. Turn on the “triplet” click if your metronome offers it. If not, keep the regular click but tap your foot to feel the triplet pulse. 3. Count aloud: “1‑a‑2‑a‑3‑a‑4‑a”. Say “1” on the downbeat, “a” on the third triplet subdivision (the short note). 4. Play a single note (middle C) on every “1” and “a”. This creates the long–short pattern. 5. 8. Left-Hand Patterns: Comping and Bass Lines

What Does a Jazz Pianist Do When the Soloist Starts to Soar? Imagine you’re sitting at a small‑room piano while a saxophonist launches into a soaring improvisation. The melody is clear, but without anything underneath it feels empty, like a bird flying over a barren landscape. Your left hand is the “ground” that gives the soloist something to perch on. By laying down a steady rhythm and a harmonic foundation, you turn a simple line into a full‑blown jazz conversation. That “ground” is created through comping and bass lines—the two core left‑hand techniques every beginner jazz pianist must master. --- Defining Comping and Its Role in Jazz Piano Comping (short for accompanying) is the art of playing short, rhythmic chordal figures that support a soloist or vocalist. Unlike playing full, blocky chords, comping is: Sparse – you leave space for the soloist to breathe. Rhythmic – you lock into the swing feel introduced in Rhythm and Swing: The Jazz Pulse. Harmonic – you outline the chord changes you already know from Basic Jazz Chords and Seventh Chords. In a typical jazz combo, the pianist’s left hand supplies the harmonic “road map,” while the right hand may be free to improvise or add melodic embellishments. When you comp, you are essentially painting a harmonic backdrop that: 1. Reinforces the chord progression. 2. Gives the soloist cues about where the harmony is moving. 3. Creates momentum through rhythmic drive. --- Two Fundamental Left‑Hand Patterns 1. Stride Piano Stride is a classic early‑jazz style where the left hand alternates between a low bass note and a chord a fourth or fifth above it. The pattern creates a “boom‑chick” feel that propels the music forward. 10. Solo Piano: Combining Hands for a Full Sound

You have just finished a short solo on the melody—your right hand dancing over the tune’s head. The room is quiet, waiting for what comes next. Instead of stepping away or letting a bassist fill the low end, you stay seated, pull your left hand down, and begin to lay down a walking bass line while your right hand continues to comp the chords. The sound that fills the room is surprisingly full—almost as if a whole rhythm section were behind you. That instant “full‑sound” feeling is the goal of this chapter: learning how to combine left‑hand bass patterns with right‑hand chords so that a single pianist can create a rich, balanced texture. We’ll build the skill step by step, from the very first coordinated pattern to a simple jazz standard that showcases both hands working together. --- 1. Why Combine Hands? 1.1 The Musical Benefits - Harmonic depth – The left hand provides the root and the “foundation” of the chord, while the right hand supplies the color tones (3rds, 7ths, extensions). Together they outline the full harmony. - Rhythmic drive – A walking bass or a steady comping rhythm gives the piece forward motion, essential for the swing feel introduced in Rhythm and Swing: The Jazz Pulse. - Dynamic balance – Controlling volume between hands lets you shape phrases, make a line “pop” or “sink,” and keep the overall texture cohesive. 1.2 The Pedagogical Benefits - Hand independence – Developing the ability to think and move each hand separately is a cornerstone of piano technique. - Coordination of timing – Synchronizing the syncopated swing eighth‑note feel across both hands reinforces the rhythmic concepts you already know. - Confidence for solo performance – Once you can sustain a groove with both hands, you’re ready to tackle solo piano arrangements of standards and even original compositions. --- 2. 12. Jazz Articulation: Dynamics, Touch, and Expression

The Power of a Single Note: From “Just Playing” to “Saying Something” Imagine you’re sitting at a piano in a small jazz club. The band has just finished a smooth ii–V–I progression, and the audience leans in as the soloist begins a melody. The notes glide together, some bite sharply, others whisper gently. Even though the melody uses the same set of pitches you practiced in earlier chapters, the articulation—how each note is started, sustained, and released—creates a story that moves the listeners. In the same way a painter chooses brushstrokes, a jazz pianist chooses touch, dynamics, and articulation. This chapter shows you how to turn the notes you already know into expressive phrases that breathe life into any tune. --- 1. Legato vs. Staccato: Two Fundamental Articulations What the Terms Mean - Legato (Italian for “tied together”) – notes are played smoothly and connected, with little or no silence between them. Think of a river flowing without interruption. - Staccato (Italian for “detached”) – notes are played short and separated, with a brief silence after each attack. Imagine a series of crisp clicks. These are the two ends of a spectrum; most jazz playing lives somewhere in between, but mastering the extremes gives you control over the middle ground. How to Produce Legato 1. Finger Weight – Keep a gentle weight on the finger that will strike the key. The weight helps the note sound full without a hard attack. 2. Minimal Release – As you lift a finger, let the next finger already be in position to press the following key. The goal is to avoid any audible gap. 3. Use the Thumb for Transitions – When moving from a black key to a white key (or vice‑versa), the thumb can “slide” under the hand, keeping the hand relaxed and the line connected. Practice tip: Choose a simple three‑note pattern (e.g., C – E – G) and play it legato in quarter‑note rhythm, then repeat in eighth‑note swing rhythm. Listen for any tiny gaps and smooth them out. How to Produce Staccato 1. Quick Attack – Snap the finger onto the key with a light but decisive motion. 2. Immediate Release – As soon as the note sounds, lift the finger sharply, creating a brief silence. 3. Control the Length – In jazz, “staccato” rarely means “dead” – aim for a note that lasts about ½ to ⅔ of its nominal value, leaving space for the next note. Practice tip: Using the same three‑note pattern, play it staccato in eighth‑note swing rhythm. Notice how each note pops out of the texture. 13. Putting It All Together: Practicing and Performing

Why a Structured Practice Routine Matters Imagine you’re sitting at a café on a Saturday night, the mic is on, and the band asks you to “play something you’ve been working on.” The lights dim, you take a breath, and… nothing comes out. This is the moment most beginners dread, and it usually isn’t a lack of talent—it’s a lack of systematic practice. A practice routine turns scattered, occasional rehearsals into steady, measurable progress. By allocating specific time blocks to the skills you’ve already built—chord voicings, swing feel, comping patterns, and improvisation—you create a feedback loop that reinforces muscle memory, sharpens your ear, and builds the confidence needed to perform in front of an audience. Quick fact: Professional musicians often cite “consistent, focused practice” as the single biggest factor in their development, more than innate ability or the amount of time spent at the instrument. --- Designing Your Weekly Practice Schedule A beginner’s schedule doesn’t need to be elaborate; it just needs balance and repeatability. Warm‑up (5–10 min) Goal: loosen the fingers, reinforce basic coordination. Activities: - Chromatic runs up and down the keyboard, focusing on even tempo. - Thumb‑under exercises from the “Introduction to Jazz Piano and the Keyboard” chapter. - Light scale fragments (major and minor) in the key of the piece you’ll practice later. 2. Review Core Material (10–15 min) Rotate through the core concepts you’ve already learned.
