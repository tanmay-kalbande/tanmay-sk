1. Introduction to Hangul and Syllable Blocks

A King, A Problem, and an Alphabet Imagine sitting down to learn a new language, only to discover that its writing system is essentially a massive puzzle with no picture on the box. For hundreds of years, this was the reality for the common people of Korea. They spoke Korean, but the only writing system available to them was Classical Chinese. Because Chinese characters are based on meaning rather than sound, learning to read and write required years of intense, specialized study. It was a luxury reserved for the wealthy male elite. If you were a farmer, a merchant, or a woman, you were effectively locked out of the written word. In the 15th century, a king named Sejong the Great decided this was unacceptable. He believed that the ability to read and write was a fundamental right, not a privilege. To solve this, he and a committee of scholars invented an entirely new alphabet from scratch. In 1443, they unveiled their creation, initially called Hunminjeongeum (which translates to "The Correct/Proper Sounds to Instruct the People"). Today, we know this alphabet as Hangul. Hangul is entirely unique in the world of writing systems. Most alphabets evolved slowly over thousands of years, their origins lost to time. Hangul, by contrast, is a deliberately engineered script. It was designed with a specific purpose: to be so simple and logical that a smart person could learn it in a single morning, and even a fool could learn it in ten days. The genius of Hangul lies in its visual logic. Instead of arbitrary squiggles on a page, the basic letters are drawings of the shape your mouth makes when you pronounce them. For example, the consonant representing the "k" or "g" sound is shaped like the side profile of a raised tongue touching the back of the roof of the mouth: ㄱ. The consonant for the "n" sound is shaped like a tongue resting flat against the back of the front teeth: ㄴ. While we will dive into the specific shapes of the vowels and consonants in the upcoming chapters, it is this underlying logic—this marriage of sound, mouth anatomy, and geometry—that makes Hangul one of the most scientific and efficient writing systems on Earth. The Logic of the Square Block If Hangul is an alphabet, you might expect it to be written in a straight line, just like English or Spanish. If we write the English word "dog," we simply place three letters side by side: d - o - g. Hangul does not work this way. 2. The Basic Vowels

The Breath of Language Imagine standing on a sunny hillside, pointing at the sky. The sound that comes out of your mouth might be a long, sustained "Ah!" Or imagine the moment you finally understand a difficult concept—you might mutter a short, clipped "Uh." Vowels are the open sounds of human speech. They are the raw vocal energy that carries our words. While consonants act as the boundaries and breaks in our speech, vowels are the breath that flows between them. In the previous chapter, we explored how King Sejong the Great designed Hangul using square blocks. We learned that a syllable block requires at least one consonant (the Initial) and one vowel (the Medial). We also saw how vertical vowels sit to the right of the initial consonant, while horizontal vowels sit below it. But what are those vowels? How do they look, how do they sound, and how do we write them? Hangul has a total of ten basic vowels. Unlike the English alphabet, where vowels (A, E, I, O, U) often have multiple, unpredictable pronunciations, Hangul vowels are highly consistent. Once you learn the sound of a Korean vowel, it will virtually always make that exact sound. The Design Philosophy of Hangul Vowels Before we dive into the specific shapes, it helps to understand a secret about Hangul: the letters are not random. King Sejong the Great designed the vowels based on three fundamental elements of the universe, as understood in traditional Eastern philosophy: A vertical line ( | ) representing the upright human. A horizontal line ( — ) representing the flat earth. A short dot ( · ) representing the sun or heaven. While modern Hangul no longer uses the short dot—replacing it with a short perpendicular line instead—the philosophy remains. When the "heaven" mark is added to the "human" line, it creates one sound. When added to the "earth" line, it creates another. As you learn these ten basic vowels, you will see that they are essentially combinations of a long line and a short line. For each vowel, we will provide a pronunciation guide based on English approximations. Keep in mind that English approximations are never 100% perfect, but they will get you very close. 3. The Basic Consonants

The Genius of Shapes: Consonants as Art Imagine trying to teach an entire nation to read in the 15th century. Before King Sejong the Great created Hangul, the people of Korea relied on Classical Chinese characters to write. Because those characters did not match the Korean language, learning to read and write was a monumental task reserved only for the wealthy elite. Sejong wanted a system so intuitive that "a wise man can learn it in a morning, and a stupid man in ten days." To achieve this, he didn’t just invent random squiggles. Instead, he looked at the human body. When you speak, your vocal organs—your lips, tongue, and throat—shift into specific shapes to block or shape the air coming from your lungs. The basic Hangul consonants are essentially blueprints of those shapes. Every basic consonant letter is a simple drawing of what your mouth, lips, or throat looks like when you make that specific sound. In the previous chapter, we learned how to build simple syllable blocks using a placeholder consonant (ㅇ) and basic vowels. Now, we will replace that placeholder with real, meaningful consonants. By learning the nine basic consonants, you will unlock the ability to read and write hundreds of foundational Korean words. The Nine Basic Consonants There are nine basic consonants in Hangul. We will group them by the part of the vocal tract they mimic: the velum (back of the throat), the tongue, and the lips. As we go through these, remember that we are looking for the closest English equivalent. Korean pronunciation is not perfectly identical to English, but these approximations will give you a strong foundation. The Velar Consonants (The Back of the Throat) The first set of consonants is drawn by looking at the back of your mouth. The velum is the soft part of the roof of your mouth at the very back, near your throat. 1. ㄱ (g/k) The Shape: Look at the letter ㄱ. It mimics the shape of the root of your tongue slightly raised to touch the velum (the back of the roof of your mouth). The corner represents the tongue blocking the airway. The Sound: Pronounced like a soft "g" as in "game," or sometimes a soft "k" as in "kite." It is not a sharp, explosive "k," but rather a gentle, relaxed sound. 4. Aspirated and Tense Consonants

The Breath and the Tension Imagine you are holding a single sheet of tissue paper a few inches in front of your mouth. If you say the English word "pot," a small burst of air will escape your lips and cause the tissue to flutter. If you whisper the word "spot," the tissue will barely move. You are making the exact same lip and tongue movement, but you are manipulating the air differently. In Chapter 3, we met the basic Hangul consonants. These letters represent the foundational sounds of Korean, but Korean takes air control a step further. By taking five of the basic consonants you already know and applying a simple visual modification—adding an extra stroke—King Sejong the Great and the creators of Hangul designed a way to represent two specific types of airflow: aspiration (a burst of air) and tension (a tight, restricted airflow). This brilliant design means that once you know the shape of a basic consonant, you already know the shapes of its modified counterparts. You just need to learn how to change your breath. The Three-Way Contrast To master Korean consonants, you need to train your mouth to hear and feel the difference between three categories of sound. Let's define our jargon: Basic Consonants: These are the standard, relaxed sounds we covered in Chapter 3. They are voiced (your vocal cords vibrate) if they appear between two vowels, but they are voiceless at the beginning of a word. Think of the 'p' in "spot" or the 'k' in "skate." Aspirated Consonants: The word aspiration is a linguistics term that simply means "a burst of air." When you pronounce an aspirated consonant, you release a puff of air from your lungs. Think of the 'p' in "pot" or the 'k' in "kite." In Hangul, these are created by adding one extra horizontal stroke to a basic consonant. Tense Consonants: Also known as "double consonants," these sounds have no direct equivalent in English. They are pronounced with a glottal tension—meaning you squeeze your vocal cords and throat muscles tightly together, stopping the air briefly before releasing it with a sharp, almost popping sound. Think of the abrupt 'p' in "upbeat" or the 't' in "entreat," where the sound is held back and tense. In Hangul, these are created by writing the basic consonant twice, side-by-side. Korean uses a five-way system for these sounds, but for now, we will focus on the five basic consonants that have aspirated and tense counterparts: ㅂ, ㅈ, ㄷ, ㄱ, and ㅅ. 5. Complex Vowels (Diphthongs)

The Magic of Merging Vowels Imagine you are looking at a menu in a Seoul coffee shop. You recognize the basic consonants and vowels from previous chapters, but you keep seeing vowels that look... crowded. Letters like ㅐ, ㅚ, and ㅢ suddenly appear where you expect a single, simple vowel line. You might wonder if the printer made a mistake, or if these are entirely new characters you have to memorize from scratch. The good news is that neither is true. These are complex vowels, and they are essentially just basic vowels stacked or placed side-by-side. In Chapter 2, you learned the basic vowels (like ㅏ, ㅓ, ㅗ, ㅜ). King Sejong the Great and his scholars designed Hangul to be highly logical. Rather than inventing completely new symbols for every possible sound, they combined existing basic vowels to create new, gliding sounds. These combined vowels are technically known as diphthongs. A diphthong is simply a sound that begins with one vowel and glides smoothly into another within the same syllable. Think of the English word "cow." Your mouth starts with an "ah" sound and glides into a "w" sound. You don't pause in the middle; it's one continuous motion. Korean complex vowels work the exact same way. In this chapter, we will unlock the 11 complex vowels. You will learn to recognize them by sight, pronounce them as smooth gliding sounds, and write them using the correct stroke order. The "Y" Vowels (Iotized Vowels) Let’s start with the easiest group of complex vowels. These are formed by taking four of the basic vertical vowels you already know and adding one extra short stroke to them. When you add this extra stroke, you are essentially adding a "Y" sound to the front of the vowel. In linguistics, this is called iotization (adding a "y" sound), but you can just think of them as the "Y vowels." Look at how a single extra stroke transforms the sound: ㅏ (a) + extra stroke = ㅑ (ya) ㅓ (eo) + extra stroke = ㅕ (yeo) ㅗ (o) + extra stroke = ㅛ (yo) ㅜ (u) + extra stroke = ㅠ (yu) Pronouncing the "Y" Vowels Because you already know the basic vowels, pronouncing these is incredibly straightforward. Just add a "Y" to the front: 1. ㅑ (ya): Sounds like the "ya" in "yard." 2. ㅕ (yeo): Sounds like "yuh" in "young," but softer. 3. ㅛ (yo): Sounds like "yo" in "yo-yo." 4. ㅠ (yu): Sounds like "you" in "you." 6. Building Syllable Blocks

The Magic of the Square Imagine trying to fit a square peg into a round hole. If you tried to write English by stacking its letters on top of each other in a rigid grid, the result would be a chaotic, unreadable tower. Yet, this grid-based approach is exactly what makes Hangul so visually striking and organized. As we saw in our introduction to Hangul, King Sejong the Great designed this alphabet to be written in square blocks. Instead of writing letters in a straight horizontal line like you do in English, Hangul groups letters into syllable-sized boxes. Each block represents one syllable, and each block fits neatly into an imaginary square, much like the characters in Chinese or Japanese. But how do we know exactly where to place each letter inside that square? The answer lies in understanding the spatial rules of the block. By the end of this chapter, you will be able to assemble consonants and vowels into valid written blocks, add final consonants to the bottom of those blocks, and read and write simple 2-3 character syllables with confidence. The Three Positions of a Syllable Block Every Hangul syllable block is built using specific positions. You already learned the names for these positions in earlier chapters, but let's look at how they function spatially within the square: 1. Initial (Top/Left): The starting consonant of the syllable. 2. Medial (Right/Bottom): The vowel of the syllable. 3. Final (Very Bottom): The ending consonant of the syllable (if there is one). The most important rule to remember is this: The shape of the Medial (vowel) dictates the layout of the entire block. In the chapters on Basic Vowels and Complex Vowels, you learned that Hangul vowels are drawn with either vertical lines or horizontal lines. This seemingly simple design feature is the master key to building syllable blocks. Let's look at how this works in practice. Building Two-Letter Blocks (C + V) The simplest syllable blocks consist of just two letters: one consonant (C) and one vowel (V). To build these, you need to look at your vowel and ask one question: Does this vowel stand tall, or does it lay flat? 7. Batchim and Basic Sound Changes

The Mystery of the Silent Traveler Imagine you are walking through Seoul and you see a sign for a famous traditional market. The sign reads: 앞문. Based on what we learned in Building Syllable Blocks, you know how to break this down. The first syllable is 앞 (애 + ㅂ). The second syllable is 문 (ㅁ + 우 + ㄴ). If you try to read this exactly as it is written, letter by letter, you might pronounce it as ap-mun. But if you listen to a native speaker say this word, it sounds distinctly like am-mun. Where did the p sound go? Why did it turn into an m? Welcome to the most fascinating quirk of the Korean alphabet: Batchim (받침). While Hangul is incredibly logical, spoken Korean prioritizes the smooth flow of sound. When a consonant sits at the bottom of a square block, it doesn't always keep its original voice. Sometimes it shifts entirely, and sometimes it reaches out and grabs the next syllable. What is Batchim? In Introduction to Hangul and Syllable Blocks, we looked at how Korean syllables are built. A syllable block can have up to three parts: - Initial (Top/Left): The starting consonant - Medial (Middle): The vowel - Final (Bottom): The ending consonant The word Batchim literally translates to "support" or "base." It refers to any consonant (or pair of consonants) that occupies the Final position at the bottom of a syllable block. Not all syllables have a batchim. As we saw in The Basic Vowels, a syllable like 가 (ga) or 나 (na) ends cleanly on a vowel. But when a syllable does have a batchim, like 곧 (god) or 옷 (os), that bottom consonant dictates how the syllable closes. Here is the catch: while there are many Korean consonants, the human mouth only has so many ways to cleanly end a sound. Because of this, Hangul relies on a system of Representative Final Consonant Sounds. The 7 Representative Final Consonant Sounds When you pronounce a syllable with a batchim in isolation (meaning, you are just reading the single syllable out loud), the final consonant will always resolve into one of exactly seven sounds. Think of your mouth as a door. To make a final consonant sound, you have to close the door. There are only seven ways to close it comfortably in Korean. Let's explore them. 1. 8. Reading Practice and Keyboard Typing

From Letters to Words: Reading Practice You have spent the last seven chapters assembling the pieces of Hangul. You learned how Sejong the Great designed these square blocks, how to pair basic consonants and vowels, how to navigate complex vowels, and how to handle the tricky shifts in pronunciation caused by batchim. Now comes the moment of truth: looking at a full word and reading it aloud without hesitation. Reading Hangul fluently requires you to stop seeing individual letters and start seeing whole syllable blocks as single units. When a native English speaker sees the word "cat," they don't sound out "c-a-t" every time—they simply recognize the shape of the word. Your goal in this section is to begin that process of visual recognition in Korean. Reading Aloud: A Step-by-Step Approach When approaching a new Korean word, follow this sequence to ensure correct pronunciation: 1. Identify the syllable blocks: Remember that Korean words are strings of square blocks. Each block represents one syllable. 2. Parse the components: Quickly glance at the Initial, Medial, and Final positions within the block. 3. Apply the rules: If a block has a Final (batchim), check if it carries over to the next syllable or changes its sound based on the rules we covered in Chapter 7. 4. Blend and speak: Instead of reading letter-by-letter, try to blend the sounds into a single fluid syllable, then string those syllables together into a word. Common Vocabulary Practice Let’s apply this to some everyday Korean words. Read each word aloud, paying attention to the syllable structure and any sound changes.
