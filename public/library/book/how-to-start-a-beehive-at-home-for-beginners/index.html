5. Purchasing or Catching Bees: Sources and Options

The first living residents of your hive will shape everything that follows. Will you buy bees from a supplier? Will you install a small, established colony? Or will you let nature deliver a swarm to your doorstep? Each path has distinct benefits, challenges, and risks. Your choice depends on your goals, your timeline, and your comfort level with risk and patience. Choosing the wrong source can lead to weak colonies, disease, or even failure before your first honey harvest. But the right source—matched to your experience and resources—can set you up for success. This chapter breaks down your options, explains how to get bees safely, and helps you decide which method is best for you. --- Understanding Your Bee Options When starting a beehive, you have three main ways to acquire bees: - Package bees: A box containing 2–3 pounds of worker bees (about 10,000 bees) and a caged queen. - Nucleus colonies (nucs): Small, established colonies with a laying queen, brood (developing bees), honey, and pollen in 4–5 frames. - Wild swarms: A natural colony that has split from an existing hive, often found clustering on a bush or fence post. Each has pros and cons. Let’s explore each one in detail. --- Package Bees What is a package bee? A package bee is a temporary colony sold by commercial bee suppliers. It typically includes: - 2–3 pounds of worker bees (about 10,000 bees) - One mated queen in a small cage - A feeder can filled with sugar syrup for the bees during transport The queen is usually introduced to the package just before shipping and is separated from the workers by a candy plug. The bees eat through the plug over several days, allowing them to accept her. How do you install a package? Installing a package is a hands-on process: 1. Shake the bees gently into the hive body (the box that holds the frames). 2. Place the queen cage inside the hive, often between two frames. 3. Provide a feeder with sugar syrup (1:1 sugar-to-water ratio) for the first week or two. 4. Close the hive and avoid disturbing it for at least 3–5 days. Pros of package bees: - Widely available from commercial suppliers. - Predictable timing—you can order them to arrive when your hive is ready. - Usually disease-free if purchased from a reputable supplier. - Ideal for beginners who want a fresh start. 6. Assembling and Setting Up Your First Beehive

From a Pile of Wood to a Bee Home Imagine you’ve just received a large, heavy shipment of cedar or pine. At first glance, it looks like a disorganized collection of wooden slats, rectangles, and wax sheets. It can feel overwhelming—like a piece of IKEA furniture, but with the added pressure that the "end users" are thousands of living insects with very specific architectural requirements. However, the Langstroth Hive (the industry standard we are using) is designed with a brilliant logic: modularity. Every piece is designed to be interchangeable and scalable. Once you understand how the components stack and how the internal "furniture" fits together, assembly becomes a meditative process of preparation. Your goal for this stage is not just to build a box, but to create a climate-controlled, defendable fortress where your bees can raise brood and store honey without the hive tilting, rotting, or leaking. Anatomy of the Langstroth Hive Before you drive a single nail or slide a frame into place, you must organize your components. A Langstroth hive is essentially a vertical skyscraper. The bees live at the bottom and expand upward as the colony grows. The Outer Shell (The Boxes) You will likely have two types of boxes: Deep Boxes (Brood Chambers): These are the tallest boxes. They are used for the "nest," where the queen lays eggs and the workers raise the young (brood). Most beginners start with two deeps. Mediums or Shallows (Honey Supers): These are shorter boxes placed on top of the brood chambers. Supers are the areas where bees store surplus honey for you to harvest later. The Internal Structure (Frames and Foundation) The magic of the Langstroth system is Bee Space. This is a precise gap (between 1/4 and 3/8 of an inch) that bees leave open. If a gap is smaller, bees seal it with propolis (a sticky resin "glue"); if it is larger, they fill it with burr comb (extra, messy wax). The frames are designed to maintain this exact space. Frames: Rectangular wooden or plastic inserts that hold the wax. Foundation: The thin sheet of beeswax or plastic embossed with a hexagonal pattern. This acts as a "blueprint," telling the bees exactly where to build their cells. The Management Layers (The Accessories) Bottom Board: The floor of the hive. It provides the landing pad for the bees and the entrance to the colony. Inner Cover: A flat lid with a hole in the center that sits between the top box and the outer cover. It prevents the outer cover from sticking to the wax and helps manage ventilation. Outer Cover (Telescoping Cover): The "roof" that protects the hive from rain and snow. This is the "Moment of Truth." For many beginners, this is the most nerve-wracking part of the process. You are moving living creatures from a temporary transport container into their permanent home. If done correctly, it is a seamless transition; if done haphazardly, you may end up with thousands of bees covering your driveway or a queen who disappears into the grass. The goal of installation is simple: get the bees and the queen into the hive safely, minimize their stress, and provide them with the immediate resources they need to begin building their colony. Preparing for Installation Before the bees even arrive, your hive must be "move-in ready." Bees are highly sensitive to their environment; if they arrive to find a chaotic setup, they are more likely to swarm (leave the hive in search of a new home) or become stressed. The best time to install bees is in the late morning or early afternoon. 8. Monitoring Your Hive: First Few Weeks and Beyond

The First Flight: Your First Seven Days Imagine standing by your hive three days after installation. You see a steady stream of bees flying in and out of the entrance. Some are carrying bright orange pollen on their legs; others are hovering nearby, seemingly undecided. To the untrained eye, it looks like chaos. To a beekeeper, this is a data stream. The first few weeks after you introduce your bees—whether you started with a package or a nuc—are the most volatile period in a colony's early life. The bees are transitioning from the stress of transport to the effort of building a home. Your job during this phase is not to "manage" the bees, but to observe them. If you intervene too often, you disrupt the delicate temperature and humidity levels the bees are trying to maintain. If you don't intervene enough, you might miss a critical failure, such as a queen who failed to survive the trip. The "Hands-Off" Window For the first 7 to 10 days after installation, resist the urge to open the hive every day. Opening the hive lets out heat and can stress the queen, potentially causing her to stop laying eggs. Instead, perform entrance observations. Stand a few feet back and look for: Foraging Activity: Are bees returning with pollen (small colorful clumps on their hind legs)? This indicates the colony is feeding the larvae and growing. The "Beard": Are bees clustering in a large mass on the front of the hive? In hot weather, this is often just cooling behavior; in cooler weather, it may mean they are struggling to stay warm. Fighting: Are bees from your hive fighting with other bees at the entrance? This could be a sign of "robbing," where bees from a stronger nearby colony try to steal honey. Performing Your First Basic Inspection Once the initial settling period has passed, it is time for your first internal inspection. The goal of a beginner's inspection is not to move things around, but to verify three things: Is the queen present (or is she laying)? Are the bees healthy? Do they have enough space? Preparing for the Inspection Before you lift the lid, ensure your smoker is lit and producing a cool, thick white smoke. The Rhythm of the Hive Imagine waking up on a crisp Tuesday in March. You walk out to your apiary and notice a sudden, frantic energy in the air. Thousands of bees are pouring out of the hive entrance in a shimmering cloud, and the colony's population is exploding as the first willow blossoms open. If you aren't prepared for this surge, your bees might decide they've outgrown their home and leave you behind in a massive swarm. Beekeeping is not a static hobby; it is a dance with the seasons. A honey bee colony is a living organism that breathes, grows, and shrinks in direct response to the environment. If you manage your hive based on the calendar rather than the bees' needs, you risk losing the colony to starvation in winter or overcrowding in spring. To be a successful beekeeper, you must learn to anticipate the needs of the hive one step ahead of the weather. Spring: The Great Awakening Spring is the most critical time for a beekeeper. After the dormant winter months, the colony is in a race against time to build up its population and store enough food to support the coming brood (developing baby bees). The Spring Build-Up As temperatures rise, the queen begins laying eggs at an accelerated rate. This leads to Hive Expansion, the process of adding more space to the hive to accommodate the growing population. If the bees feel cramped, they will trigger a swarm instinct (which we will cover in detail in Chapter 12). Key Spring Tasks: First Inspection: Once temperatures consistently stay above 50°F (10°C), perform your first thorough inspection. Check for the presence of the queen (or evidence of her eggs) and ensure the colony has enough food. Feeding: If the hive is low on honey stores, provide stimulative feeding. This involves feeding a light sugar syrup (1 part sugar to 1 part water) to trick the queen into thinking there is a nectar flow, encouraging her to lay more eggs. Adding Space: When 70–80% of the frames in your current box are filled with bees, pollen, or honey, add a new super (the upper boxes used for honey storage) or another deep brood box. Pollen Check: Ensure the bees have access to pollen. While honey provides energy (carbohydrates), pollen provides the protein necessary to feed the developing larvae. 10. Harvesting Honey: When, How, and What to Expect

The Golden Moment: Knowing When to Harvest Imagine opening your hive on a warm August afternoon. You lift the lid and see a sea of gold—perfectly formed hexagonal cells filled with glistening nectar. It is tempting to take it all right then. However, if you harvest too early, you aren't actually harvesting honey; you are harvesting nectar. The difference is moisture content. Nectar is a watery secretion from flowers. To turn this into honey, bees store it in the comb and fan it with their wings to evaporate the water. If you harvest honey that still has too much water, it will ferment and spoil, turning your hard work into a vinegary syrup. The Secret of the "Cap" How do you know when the bees have finished the evaporation process? Look for the capping. When the moisture content drops to roughly 17–18%, the bees seal the cell with a thin layer of white beeswax. This is the bee's way of "vacuum sealing" the food for winter. The Rule of Thumb: Only harvest frames where at least 80% to 90% of the cells are capped. If a frame is only half-capped, the bees are still working on it. Leave it alone, or you risk harvesting unstable honey. The Beekeeping Balance Before you reach for your tools, remember the goals discussed in Seasonal Hive Management. Your primary responsibility is the health of the colony. Bees need a significant amount of honey to survive the winter. As mentioned in earlier chapters, a colony can produce a surplus of 20–60 pounds annually, but this varies wildly based on the local flora and weather. Before harvesting, ask yourself: Do my bees have enough for themselves? In colder climates, it is often safer to harvest in late summer rather than autumn to ensure the bees have a full pantry before the first frost. --- Gathering Your Gear: The Extraction Toolkit Harvesting honey is a messy process. If you do it without the right tools, you will end up with a "sticky" situation—literally. Depending on your budget and how much honey you have, you will use one of two primary methods: Centrifugal Extraction or Crush-and-Strain. Essential Safety and Access Gear Regardless of the extraction method, you need your standard protective gear to keep the bees calm while you remove the frames: Bee Suit and Veil: Full protection as described in Essential Beekeeping Equipment. 11. Common Bee Diseases and Pests: Prevention and Treatment

The Invisible Battle: Why Hive Health Matters Imagine opening your hive on a sunny Tuesday afternoon, expecting to see a bustling city of activity. Instead, you notice a strange, sickly smell—like rotting gym socks—and the bees seem lethargic, crawling slowly across the frames rather than flying. You look closer at the brood (the developing larvae) and see that some of the cells are capped with a sunken, dark brown wax, and when you poke a larva with a toothpick, it stretches out like gooey taffy. This is the moment every beekeeper dreads, but it is also the moment where your knowledge becomes the difference between a thriving colony and a total loss. As mentioned in the early chapters of this book, annual colony loss rates can reach 30–40%. While harsh winters and pesticide exposure play a role, the primary driver of these losses is often a combination of pests and pathogens. Because bees live in a dense, social colony, a single infection can spread through thousands of individuals in days. To keep your bees healthy, you must move from being a passive observer to an active "hive doctor." The Golden Rule: Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Before diving into specific diseases, you need a philosophy for treatment. Many beginners make the mistake of "calendar treating"—applying chemicals on a set date regardless of whether the bees actually need them. This can weaken the colony and lead to chemical resistance. Instead, professional beekeepers use Integrated Pest Management (IPM). IPM is a tiered approach to pest control that prioritizes the least invasive methods first. 1. Cultural Controls (Prevention): This is your first line of defense. It involves practices like providing high-quality nutrition, ensuring the hive is in a well-ventilated location, and buying bees from reputable, disease-free sources. 2. Mechanical/Physical Controls: These are "hands-on" ways to reduce pests without chemicals. Examples include using "drone brood traps" to catch mites or removing infested frames. 3. Biological Controls: Using natural predators or breeding bees that have a genetic resistance to certain pests. 4. Chemical Controls: The last resort. When pest levels reach a dangerous threshold, targeted medications are used to save the colony. By following the IPM ladder, you avoid over-medicating your bees and ensure that when you do use a treatment, it actually works. The Greatest Threat: Varroa Destructor Mites If there is one "villain" in the world of beekeeping, it is the Varroa mite. These tiny, reddish-brown parasites are the primary cause of colony collapse worldwide. They don't just feed on the bee's "fat bodies" (similar to a liver); they act as vectors, meaning they carry deadly viruses from one bee to another. 12. Swarm Prevention and Management: Keeping Control of Your Hive

The Great Escape: What is Swarming? Imagine walking into your backyard on a sunny Tuesday morning to find a buzzing, grapefruit-sized mahogany cloud hanging from your favorite apple tree. Thousands of bees are clustered together, vibrating in a dense mass. You look at your hive and realize half your colony is missing. Panic is the natural first reaction for a beginner, but for the honey bee, this is not a disaster—it is a biological triumph. Swarming is the natural process of colony reproduction. While a queen produces new bees to grow the hive, swarming is how the colony produces a whole new hive. In the wild, a colony that grows too large for its cavity will split; the old queen takes about half the worker bees and a few drones (males) to find a new home, leaving the remaining bees and a new queen behind to manage the original location. While swarming is a sign of a healthy, thriving colony, it is generally undesirable for the home beekeeper. A swarm means a loss of pollinators, a significant drop in your potential honey harvest, and the potential for nervous neighbors to call animal control. The Biological Triggers: Why Bees Decide to Leave Bees do not swarm on a whim. Their decision is driven by specific biological and environmental triggers. Understanding these "why" factors allows you to intervene before the bees make their move. Congestion and Space The most common trigger is a lack of room. As you learned in Seasonal Hive Management, bees ramp up production in the spring. If the hive becomes "honey bound" (where the queen has no open cells to lay eggs because the workers have filled them with honey or pollen), the bees feel claustrophobic. This physical congestion signals to the colony that it is time to expand. Pheromone Dilution The queen emits a chemical scent called queen mandibular pheromone (QMP). This scent acts as a social glue, telling the workers that the queen is present, healthy, and in control. In a small colony, this scent reaches every bee. However, as the population explodes in spring, the pheromone becomes "diluted." When workers in the center of the hive no longer sense a strong concentration of QMP, they assume the colony has outgrown the queen's reach, triggering the swarm impulse. The "Spring Flush" Environmental factors play a massive role. A sudden burst of nectar-producing flowers (the "spring flow") provides a surge of energy. When the colony feels wealthy in resources and the weather is warm, the biological drive to reproduce becomes irresistible. However, the difference between a total colony loss and a successful rescue is diagnosis. Most hive failures are not sudden accidents; they are the result of a gradual decline that, if caught in time, can be reversed. Diagnosing the Decline: Why is My Hive Failing? Before you can fix a hive, you must determine why it is struggling. A "weak" hive is generally defined as one that lacks a sufficient population of worker bees to maintain the brood nest temperature or defend the colony. The "Big Three" Red Flags When assessing a struggling hive, look for these three primary indicators: 1. Lack of Brood: As discussed in Understanding Honey Bees: Biology and Behavior, the queen is the engine of the hive. If you see frames with plenty of bees but no eggs (tiny white specks), larvae (small white "C" shapes), or capped brood (tan, convex cells), the hive is in a state of reproductive failure. 2. Low Population: If you open the hive and find that the bees are only covering two or three frames in a ten-frame box, the colony is critically understaffed. They will struggle to keep the hive warm during the winter or protect their stores from robbers. 3. Erratic Behavior: Bees that are overly aggressive or, conversely, lethargic and "droopy" on the frames, often signal a systemic problem like disease or starvation. 14. Expanding Your Beekeeping Operation: Next Steps

The "Good Problem" of a Thriving Hive Imagine it is a warm Tuesday in May. You open your hive for a routine inspection and find it absolutely bursting with life. The bees are pouring out of the entrance in a thick cloud, every single frame is covered in bees, and the queen has laid eggs in every available cell. You feel a surge of pride—your colony is healthy and strong. But then you notice it: several swarm cells (large, peanut-shaped cells where the bees are raising a new queen) hanging from the bottom of the frames. You are now facing the "good problem" of beekeeping. Your hive has become so successful that it has outgrown its home. If you do nothing, half your colony will leave in a swarm, taking your honey production and your queen with them. However, if you act strategically, this abundance is your golden ticket. Instead of losing your bees to the wind, you can use this growth to double your apiary without spending a dime on new bees. When and How to Split Your Hive A split is the process of intentionally dividing one strong colony into two or more smaller colonies. This is the primary way beekeepers expand their operations organically. Determining When to Split You should only split a hive that is "boiling over." Splitting a weak hive can lead to the collapse of both resulting colonies. Look for these indicators: Congestion: There is very little open space on the frames for the queen to lay eggs. Swarm Preparation: You see queen cells being built. Resource Surplus: The hive has ample honey and pollen stores to support two separate populations. Timing: Splits are best performed in the spring or early summer when nectar flows are high and the colony is naturally expanding. The Basic Split Process While there are many advanced methods, the most common approach for beginners is the "Walk-Away Split." 1. Prepare a New Hive: Have a second hive body ready with frames (some drawn comb and some foundation). 2. Move the Frames: Gently remove 2–3 frames of brood (the frames containing larvae and pupae) and a few frames of honey/pollen from the strong hive and move them into the new hive. 3. Distribute the Bees: Shake a few frames of bees from the original hive into the new one. You don't need to find the queen; the bees clinging to the brood frames will naturally migrate. 4. The Queen Situation: The Parent Hive keeps the original queen. The Split Hive is now "queenless."
