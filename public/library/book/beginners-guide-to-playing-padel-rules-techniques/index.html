1. 1. Introduction to Padel

A First‑Time Encounter on the Court You step onto a glossy, enclosed court. The walls are a mix of glass and mesh, the net is lower than in tennis, and the rackets in your hands feel lighter, almost like a large paddle. Across the net, two smiling strangers—one a teenager, the other a retiree—high‑five each other after a rally that ricochets off the side walls. The ball bounces, hits the glass, and flies back, keeping the play alive. You’ve just experienced padel, a sport that blends the rhythm of tennis with the dynamism of squash, all wrapped in a social, fast‑moving game that welcomes players of any age or ability. If that scene feels new, you’re not alone. Padel is one of the fastest‑growing racket sports in the world, yet many people still wonder what exactly it is, how it differs from more familiar games, and why it has become a social phenomenon. This chapter answers those questions, giving you a clear picture of padel’s definition, its roots, and the values that make it a community‑building activity. What Is Padel? Padel (pronounced pa‑DEL) is a team racket sport played on a compact, enclosed court surrounded by glass and mesh walls. It is most commonly played in doubles, meaning two players per side, although singles formats exist in some clubs. The game uses a solid, perforated paddle—often called a padel racket—and a low‑pressure tennis ball that is slightly smaller and less bouncy than a standard tennis ball. Key characteristics that define padel: | Feature | Description | |---------|-------------| | Court size | 20 m × 10 m (≈ 66 ft × 33 ft), half the size of a doubles tennis court. | | Enclosure | Back and side walls are made of tempered glass (transparent) and metal mesh; the front wall is open except for the net. | | Net height | 0.88 m (≈ 2 ft 10 in) at the centre, slightly lower than tennis. | | Rackets | Solid, perforated paddles without strings, usually 35–38 cm long. | | Ball | Same diameter as a tennis ball, but with reduced internal pressure (≈ 0.6 atm). | | Scoring | Identical to tennis (15‑30‑40‑game, sets, tiebreaks). | | Players | Primarily doubles (2 vs 2), encouraging teamwork and communication. | These elements combine to create a game that is fast‑paced, easy to learn, and highly interactive. The walls are not just boundaries; they are integral to play, allowing the ball to rebound and stay in the rally longer, which in turn fuels longer exchanges and more tactical variety. 2. 2. Court Layout and Equipment

Understanding the Court Dimensions Stepping onto a padel court for the first time can feel like entering a miniature arena. The walls surround you, the net stretches across the centre, and the lines on the floor whisper the limits of every possible shot. Knowing exactly what each line means and how big the playing area really is turns that first nervous step into confidence. Overall Size A standard padel court is 20 m × 10 m (≈ 66 ft × 33 ft). It is divided into two equal halves by the net, each half measuring 10 m × 10 m. The court is enclosed on all four sides, a feature that distinguishes padel from many other racket sports and allows the ball to rebound off the glass or mesh walls. | Dimension | Metric | Imperial | |-----------|--------|----------| | Length (full court) | 20 m | 66 ft | | Width (full court) | 10 m | 33 ft | | Net height (center) | 0.88 m | 2 ft 10 in | | Net height (ends) | 0.92 m | 3 ft 0 in | | Height of glass walls | 3 m‑4 m (depends on venue) | 10‑13 ft | The enclosure is part of the playing surface. Most clubs use tempered safety glass for the front and back walls, topped with a metal mesh or perforated metal panel on the upper 1 m‑1.5 m. This mesh prevents balls from leaving the court while still allowing spectators to see the action. Key Markings Even though the court is smaller than a tennis court, the markings are precise. Each line has a purpose that will become second nature once you recognize it. 1. Baseline – The outermost line running parallel to the net on each side. A player must stay behind this line when serving, but may cross it during a rally. 2. Service Line – Located 3 m (≈ 9.8 ft) from the net toward the baseline. It marks the service box; the ball must bounce between the service line and the net on the opponent’s side. 3. Center Service Line – A short line extending 0.5 m (≈ 1.6 ft) from the net toward the service line, dividing the service box into left and right halves. 4. Side Wall Line – The vertical line where the side glass meets the floor. It is not a “line” you see on the floor, but it defines the lateral limits of the court. Hitting the ball outside this line results in a fault. 5. Back Wall Line – The line where the back glass meets the floor; like the side wall line, it marks the rear boundary. 6. Markings on …

3. 3. Basic Rules and Scoring System

Understanding the Structure of Play Imagine you and three friends have just stepped onto a freshly‑mown padel court. The sun is out, the walls gleam, and the low‑pressure ball bounces softly off the glass. Before the first rally begins, you need to know what a “game,” a “set,” and a “match” actually mean in padel, because the way points are tallied determines when you win, lose, or get a chance to serve again. Game, Set, and Match – The Hierarchy | Level | How it is won | Typical length | |-------|--------------|----------------| | Point | Win a rally | A few seconds to a minute | | Game | First side to reach 4 points with at least a 2‑point margin (0‑15‑30‑40) | Usually 5–12 points total | | Set | First side to win 6 games with a 2‑game margin (e.g., 6‑4). If the score reaches 6‑6, a tiebreak decides the set. | 10–20 games | | Match | Best of three sets (most recreational play). The side that wins 2 sets wins the match. | Up to 3 sets | Why “best of three?” – Pad­el is designed for fast, social play. A three‑set format keeps matches exciting without exhausting beginners. In professional tournaments you’ll sometimes see best‑of‑five, but for learning the basics the 2‑set‑to‑win rule is the standard. The Point‑Scoring Sequence 1. Love (0) – No points yet. 2. 15 – First point won. 3. 30 – Second point. 4. 40 – Third point. 5. Game – Fourth point provided the side leads by two. If both sides reach 40‑40, the score is called deuce. From deuce, a side must win two consecutive points: the first gives advantage, the second wins the game. If the side with advantage loses the next point, the score returns to deuce. The Tiebreak – A Mini‑Game at 6‑6 When a set is tied 6‑6, play shifts to a tiebreak: Players alternate serving every two points (except the first serve, which is a single point). The first side to reach 7 points with a 2‑point lead wins the tiebreak and thus the set 7‑6. If the lead is only one point at 7‑6, play continues until the 2‑point margin is achieved (e.g., 9‑7). A tiebreak is essentially a rapid‑fire version of a regular game, but the scoring numbers (0,1,2…) replace the 15‑30‑40 system to keep counting simple. --- Serving Order and Service Rules The serve is the only moment in a rally where the ball must be hit underhand and the server’s feet must stay behind the service line. 4. 4. Grip, Stance, and Footwork Foundations

The Continental Grip – Your First Tool on the Padel Court Imagine you are about to receive the first serve of a friendly match. The ball rockets toward you, and in a split second you must decide how to swing. The continental grip—the same hand position used for most tennis serves, volleys, and slice shots—gives you the versatility to handle low, fast‑paced balls that are typical in padel. Mastering this grip early removes a major source of inconsistency and lets you focus on positioning and timing. What the Continental Grip Looks Like 1. Hold the racket as if shaking hands with it. - Place the base of your index finger on the flat surface of the racket’s handle (the “bevel”). - The heel of your hand rests on the third bevel (the side opposite the “V” formed by your thumb and index finger). 2. Form the “V”. - When you look down at your grip, the space between your thumb and index finger should create a “V” that points toward the racket’s edge, not the tip. 3. Check the angle. - The racket face should be slightly tilted upward (about 30°–45°) when the racket is held in front of you. This angle allows you to brush the ball, creating spin, and to keep the ball low over the net—crucial for padel’s enclosed walls. Why the Continental Grip Works for Padel - Versatility: Enables forehand and backhand strokes without changing grip, which is essential when you have to react quickly to balls that rebound off glass or walls. - Control: The slightly closed face helps keep the ball from soaring too high, a common mistake for beginners who use an open tennis “eastern” grip. - Transition to the Net: Because the grip already positions the racket for volleys, moving forward after a groundstroke feels natural. Quick Grip Check List - V‑shape points to the racket edge – not the tip. - Knuckles of the index finger rest on the second bevel. - Racket face angled upward, not flat. - Relaxed wrist—avoid a stiff “hand‑like” hold; let the wrist hinge naturally during the swing. Tip: Practice the grip while standing still. Hold the racket, then swing a few “shadow” forehands and backhands, feeling the natural motion. If the racket feels “too closed” (face pointing down) or “too open” (face pointing up), adjust the hand position until the ball would meet the racket at a comfortable, slightly upward angle. --- The Balanced Ready Stance – Your Launch Pad Once your grip is set, the next step is to adopt a stance that lets you move fluidly in any direction. 5. 5. Serving Technique and Return

Starting the Point: A Real‑World Snapshot Imagine you’re on a sunny Saturday afternoon, pads laced, racket in hand, and the match is tied 4‑4 in the deciding game. Your partner gives you a quick nod – it’s time to serve. You step to the service box, take a deep breath, and swing the racket underhand, sending the ball low over the net into the opponent’s corner. The ball lands cleanly, the return comes back weakly, and you seize the rally with a smooth forehand. That single serve set the tone for the whole point. In padel, the serve is the only time you have complete control over the ball’s initial trajectory, and a well‑placed, legal serve can give you a decisive advantage from the very first shot. This chapter breaks down how to execute a low, underhand serve that complies with the rules, how to choose target zones for maximum effectiveness, and how to return the serve using either a forehand or backhand grip. --- 1. The Legal Underhand Serve Padel’s serving rules differ from tennis: the serve must be underhand, the ball must bounce once in the opposite service box, and the server must stay behind the service line until the ball is struck. Let’s unpack each element step‑by‑step. 1.1 Positioning Before the Serve 1. Stand in the correct service box – the one on your side of the court that corresponds to the server’s position (right‑hand side for the first server, left‑hand side for the second). 2. Feet placement – keep both feet behind the service line (the line that runs parallel to the net and marks the start of the service box). Your front foot should be roughly parallel to the net, while the back foot provides balance. 3. Body orientation – face the net at a slight angle, with your non‑dominant shoulder pointing toward the target zone you intend to hit. This alignment naturally opens the hips and shoulders for the underhand swing. Tip: Use the stance introduced in Chapter 4 (“Grip, Stance, and Footwork Foundations”) as a baseline; the serve stance is a subtle variation that keeps you stable while allowing a fluid swing. 1.2 Grip for the Serve A continental grip (the “handshake” grip) is the most versatile for the underhand serve. It allows you to: - Keep the racket face slightly open (tilted upward) to generate a gentle loft. - Transition smoothly to a forehand or backhand grip for the next shot without adjusting your hand position dramatically. 6. 6. Groundstrokes: Forehand and Backhand

The Forehand Drive: From Ready Position to Follow‑Through Imagine you’re at the baseline after a short, low serve. Your partner is ready at the net, the ball bounces just inside the service line, and you have a split‑second to decide where to send it. A solid forehand drive can turn that moment into a point‑winning shot that forces the opponents to scramble. 1. Setting the Stage Ready position – The stance you adopted after the serve (see Grip, Stance, and Footwork Foundations). Keep knees slightly bent, weight balanced on the balls of your feet, and the non‑dominant foot slightly forward. This position lets you react quickly to the ball’s direction and height. Grip – For a forehand drive, the continental grip (the “handshake” grip) is a versatile starting point. Many beginners find it comfortable because it works for both flat and topspin shots. If you later want more topspin, you can rotate the hand a few degrees toward an eastern forehand grip – the palm faces the side of the racket shaft. 2. The Swing Path 1. Backswing – As the ball approaches, rotate your hips and shoulders away from the net. The racket head lifts gently to about waist height; the shaft should be roughly parallel to the ground. 2. Unit turn – Initiate the forward motion with the hips, followed by the shoulders, then the arm. This “chain reaction” maximizes power while keeping the swing smooth. 3. Contact point – Aim to meet the ball slightly in front of your leading hip, at waist height. The racket face should be slightly closed (tilted forward) for a topspin drive, or more square for a flat drive. 4. Follow‑through – After contact, let the racket continue its upward‑forward arc, finishing over the opposite shoulder. A full follow‑through not only adds spin but also helps you stay balanced for the next shot. 3. Adding Basic Topspin Topspin makes the ball dip faster after crossing the net, giving you a higher margin for error over the glass walls. To generate topspin on a forehand: - Racket face angle: Close the face 5–10° relative to the flight path. - Low‑to‑high swing: Emphasize an upward motion from the backswing to the follow‑through. - Brush the ball: Rather than hitting the ball dead‑on, “brush” it by contacting the lower half of the ball and moving the racket upward. A simple drill: Stand at the service line, feed yourself half‑volleys, and focus on keeping the racket trajectory low‑to‑high. After 10 repetitions, move back a step and repeat. 4. 7. 7. Volleys and Net Play

The Net: Your New Best Friend Imagine you’re halfway through a friendly doubles match. Your partner’s serve lands deep, you step forward, and with a crisp forehand volley you tip the ball past the opponents’ side‑wall, winning the point. The crowd’s applause is louder than the echo off the glass. In that split‑second, you’ve done three things every beginner should master: 1. Moved into the optimal net position – cutting the angle of the opponent’s shot. 2. Executed a controlled forehand volley – using firm wrist action. 3. Finished the rally with an overhead smash – when the ball rose high above the net. The net is where points are often decided in padel. Mastering short‑court volleys, overhead smashes, and precise positioning will turn you from a baseline player into a “net‑killer.” The following sections break down each skill from first principles, building on the grip, stance, and footwork foundations you learned earlier. --- Forehand Volley Fundamentals A volley is a shot hit before the ball bounces on the ground. In padel, the forehand volley is the most common net‑play weapon because the court’s reduced width forces most exchanges to stay low and fast. 1. Grip and Wrist Control Use the same continental grip you practiced for serves and groundstrokes (Chapter 4). The “hammer” position—index finger and thumb forming a “V” that points toward the top edge of the racket face—gives you the needed stability. - Firm wrist, not stiff: Keep the wrist locked enough to guide the racket, but allow a gentle “snap” at contact. Think of the wrist as a hinge that releases a small amount of energy, not a rigid block. - Tip of the racket face: Align the face slightly closed (tilted forward) to keep the ball low over the net. 2. Stance and Footwork 1. Split step – As soon as your opponents strike, perform a small hop to land on the balls of your feet. This prepares you for a quick forward or lateral move. 2. Open stance: Position your feet roughly shoulder‑width apart, with the left foot (for a right‑handed player) slightly forward. This stance allows rapid weight transfer into the shot. 3. Step‑in: Take a short, decisive step with your front foot toward the ball, keeping the knee slightly bent. 3. Swing Path 1. Preparation: Bring the racket back a little, keeping the face closed. 2. Contact point: Meet the ball in front of your body, at waist height or a bit lower. 3. Follow‑through: Extend the arm forward, ending with the racket pointing toward the intended target. The follow‑through should be short—excessive length can send the ball too high. 4. Key Cue Checklist - Grip: Continental, “hammer” shape. - Wrist: Firm, …

8. 8. Using the Glass Walls Effectively

Why the Walls Change the Game Imagine you’re at the baseline, your opponent’s forehand has just clipped the top of the net and is racing toward the side wall. The ball ricochets off the glass, drops low, and lands just inside the service box. You have a split‑second to decide: chase the ball, let it bounce, or attack the angle it creates. That moment illustrates the unique “third surface” in padel—the glass walls. Unlike tennis, where the ball is out of bounds once it hits a fence, padel’s enclosure is an active part of play. Mastering wall rebounds turns a defensive situation into an offensive weapon and vice‑versa. This chapter shows you how to recognise legal wall‑bounce scenarios, use a defensive lob that lands on the back wall, and execute a simple wall‑off shot to open angles for the next point. --- Legal Wall‑Bounce Situations Before you can exploit the walls, you must know when a wall rebound is legal. The rules are straightforward, but a beginner can easily misinterpret a ball that has touched the net, the ground, or a wall in the wrong order. 1. Ball Crosses the Net First, Then Hits a Wall Allowed – The ball must first travel over the net and land inside the opponent’s court (or directly hit a wall after crossing). What it looks like – A high lob that lands on the back wall, or a fast drive that skims the side wall after clearing the net. 2. Ball Touches the Wall Before Crossing the Net Not allowed – If the ball contacts any wall (front, side, or back) before it has crossed the net, the point ends immediately. Common mistake – Hitting a shot that bounces off the side wall on your side of the net; the ball never crossed, so the rally stops. 3. Ball Hits the Net, Then the Wall Allowed – A ball that clips the net and then continues to a wall is still in play, provided it has already crossed the net. Tip – Because the ball has lost forward momentum, expect a slower rebound that can be used for a defensive reset. 4. Ball Hits the Ground, Then the Wall Allowed – Once the ball has bounced on the floor inside the opponent’s side, it may subsequently strike a wall and remain live. Strategic use – This is the basis of the wall‑off shot: a groundstroke that lands low, then bounces off the side wall to force a difficult return. 9. 9. Doubles Strategy and Court Positioning

Why Doubles Wins the Game: Teamwork Over Solo Effort Imagine this: you’re standing at the net, paddle raised, watching your partner scramble to retrieve a fast ball from the backcourt. The opponents fire a cross-court shot—it’s heading right at your partner’s feet. You freeze. Should you move forward to cover the space? Should you call for the ball? A second too late, the shot lands in bounds. Game. Set. Match. This moment—tense, fast, and full of split-second decisions—is where padel doubles shines. Unlike tennis, where solo brilliance can carry a match, padel thrives on teamwork, positioning, and clear communication. Even with perfect groundstrokes and volleys, a pair that doesn’t move together will find themselves chasing shadows on the court. In this chapter, you’ll learn not just how to play padel doubles, but how to play it smart. You’ll discover your role as a forehand or backhand player, the two key formations to stay in sync with your partner, and simple signals to coordinate like a well-rehearsed team—even if you’ve only met your partner 10 minutes ago. --- Know Your Role: The Forehand Player and the Backhand Player In doubles padel, every player has a primary zone where they play most naturally—this is usually determined by their strongest stroke: the forehand or the backhand. While both players can move anywhere on the court, assigning roles makes movement predictable and reduces confusion. The Forehand Player: The Natural Leader - Definition: The player whose dominant hand is the forehand side (right-handed players likely play forehand on the right side of the court when facing the net). - Role: Often takes more shots down the middle, especially cross-court volleys or backhand returns from the opponent’s backhand player. - Why it matters: A strong forehand is easier to hit with power and control, so placing it in the middle gives you more options and puts pressure on opponents. Example: You’re the forehand player. Your opponent hits a deep ball to your partner’s backhand. Instead of both rushing, your partner can let it go and you step to the middle, ready to return cross-court with your forehand. The Backhand Player: The Steady Anchor - Definition: The player whose backhand is their second-best stroke (often weaker than their forehand). - Role: Typically covers the backhand side of the court, especially when the ball is hit to their partner’s forehand. - Why it matters: Protecting your weaker side prevents easy winners for opponents and keeps the rally alive. Tip: If you’re unsure who should take a shot, the backhand player usually lets the forehand player take it—unless the ball is clearly on their side. 10. 10. Practice Drills and Progression Plan

Your First Six Weeks: A Step-by-Step Practice Plan You’ve made it to the final chapter. By now you know the court, the rules, and the basic strokes. But padel isn’t learned from reading—it’s learned from doing. The next six weeks will take you from “I’ve held a paddle” to “I can play a competitive point.” The drills in this chapter target the three skills that matter most for beginners: serve, volley, and wall play. Each drill is designed to be done with one partner and one court, so you can practice even if you’re the only one available. --- The Three Pillars of Beginner Drills 1. The “Bounce-Serve-Return” Drill (Serve) Goal: Build consistency from the service box. Setup - One player serves from the right service box. - The server bounces the ball once before hitting it underhand (as per padel rules). - The receiver lets the ball bounce once before returning it over the net. How to Do It 1. Serve to the diagonally opposite service box. 2. After serving, move quickly to the net (volley position). 3. Your partner returns the serve, aiming for the server’s side of the court. 4. Play out the point—first to 11, win by two. Coaching Cues - Footwork: Small, quick steps after the serve. - Contact Point: Hit the ball in front of your leading foot. - Target: Aim for the center of the service box—don’t go for corners yet. Common Mistakes and Fixes - Floating serve: Keep your paddle low and brush up the back of the ball. - Overhitting: Focus on control, not power. - Not moving forward: After the serve, step in immediately—don’t stand still. Progression (Week 3-4) - Serve to the T (center line) instead of wide. - Add a second serve attempt if the first misses. --- 2. The “Wall Target” Volley Drill (Volley) Goal: Improve touch and control at the net. Setup - Stand 3–4 meters from the glass wall. - Use your partner or a wall-mounted target (a piece of tape on the glass works). - Alternate volleys with your partner or against the wall. How to Do It 1. Start with gentle forehand volleys. 2. Aim for the target area (about waist height). 3. Keep your paddle up and eyes on the ball. 4. After 15 clean volleys, switch to backhand. 5. Increase difficulty by moving closer or hitting harder. Coaching Cues - Paddle Angle: Keep it firm, slightly open. - Wrist Lock: Minimize wrist flick—use your forearm. - Soft Hands: Absorb the ball, don’t punch it.
