1. Module 1: Polish Alphabet and Pronunciation Fundamentals

The "Secret Code" of Polish Imagine landing in Warsaw and seeing a sign that reads Szczebrzeszyn. To an English speaker, it looks like a keyboard smash—a chaotic string of consonants with no vowels in sight. But here is the secret: Polish is a phonetic language. Unlike English, where the letter "a" sounds different in apple, father, and gate, Polish letters almost always make the same sound, regardless of where they appear in a word. Once you learn the "code"—the specific sounds assigned to each letter and combination—you will be able to read any Polish word aloud with near-perfect accuracy, even if you have no idea what the word actually means. The Polish Alphabet: The Basics The Polish alphabet consists of 32 letters. Most are identical to the Latin alphabet used in English, but Polish adds several diacritics (small marks added to letters to change their sound) and uses specific combinations of letters called digraphs (two letters representing one sound). The Standard Letters Most letters sound exactly as they do in English. These include: B, D, F, G, K, L, M, N, P, R, S, T, V (not used in Polish), W, Z. Crucial Distinction: The letter "W" In Polish, the letter W is always pronounced like the English V. Example: The word Woda (water) is pronounced Voda. The Vowels Polish has seven vowels. While some are familiar, pay close attention to the "pure" sounds of the others. A: Like the "a" in father. E: Like the "e" in met. I: Like the "ee" in see. O: Like the "o" in bore (but shorter). U: Like the "oo" in boot. Y: This is a sound that doesn't exist in standard English. It is a "hard i," similar to the "i" in bit or sit. Ł: (See Diacritics below). Mastering the Diacritics Diacritics are the "special characters" that give Polish its unique flavor. If you ignore these marks, you aren't just misspelling the word—you are often changing the meaning entirely. The Nasal Vowels Polish is one of the few Slavic languages to keep nasal sounds (sounds where air escapes through both the nose and mouth). Ą (a-ogonek): Sounds like "own" in town, but spoken more softly at the end of a word. It is a nasal "o." Ę (e-ogonek): Sounds like the "en" in pen. Note: When ę appears at the end of a word, Polish speakers often drop the nasal sound and pronounce it as a regular e. The Soft Consonants These letters are "softened," meaning the tongue is placed higher and closer to the roof of the mouth. Ć: A very soft "ch" sound, similar to the "t" in nature. 2. Module 2: Basic Polish Greetings and Introductions

First Contact: The Art of the Polish Greeting Imagine you have just stepped off a train in Warsaw or Krakow. You walk into a small café, and the aroma of fresh coffee and pączki (Polish doughnuts) fills the air. As you approach the counter, the barista looks up and smiles. In this moment, you have a choice: you can rely on English, or you can bridge the cultural gap with a few simple Polish words. Polish culture places a high value on politeness and formal etiquette, especially when meeting someone for the first time. Using a proper greeting doesn't just communicate a message; it signals respect. Because you have already mastered the alphabet and those tricky diacritics and digraphs in Module 1, you are now equipped to pronounce these phrases with confidence. Greetings for Every Hour In Polish, the greeting you choose depends heavily on the time of day and your relationship with the person you are addressing. Formal Greetings When entering a shop, speaking to a teacher, or meeting a stranger, stick to formal greetings. Dzień dobry (Jen-dobry) – "Good morning / Good day." This is the universal gold standard. Use it from sunrise until the sun begins to set. Dobry wieczór (Dobry vye-choor) – "Good evening." Switch to this once it gets dark. Remember the Crucial Distinction: The letter "W" is pronounced like a "V," making this vye-choor. Informal Greetings Use these with friends, family, or people your own age in casual settings. Cześć (Chesh-ch) – "Hi / Hello." This is the most common informal greeting. Interestingly, Cześć is also used to say "Bye" in informal settings. Hej (Hey) – "Hey." Very common among younger generations and borrowed heavily from English. Saying Goodbye Just as greetings vary, so do farewells. Do widzenia (Do vee-dze-nya) – "Goodbye." The formal standard. Literally, it means "Until we see each other again." Do zobaczenia (Do zo-ba-chen-ya) – "See you later." Semi-formal to informal. Use this when you know you will see the person again soon. Dobranoc (Dob-ra-nots) – "Goodnight." Used exclusively when someone is going to sleep or leaving for the night. Introducing Yourself and Others Once the greeting is out of the way, it's time to establish who you are. In Polish, there are different ways to state your name depending on whether you want to be formal or casual. Stating Your Name There are two primary ways to introduce yourself: 1. Nazywam się... (Na-zy-vam she...) – "My name is..." Used for your full name (First + Last). This is the most formal way to introduce yourself. 2. Mam na imię... (Mam na ee-mye...) – "My name is..." 3. Module 3: Polish Nouns, Articles, and Gender System

The Secret Code of Polish Nouns Imagine you are walking through a market in Warsaw. You see a beautiful apple, a sturdy table, and a fresh loaf of bread. In English, you would simply call all of these "it." But in Polish, every single object—whether it is a living person, a piece of furniture, or an abstract concept—belongs to a specific "gender" category. If you try to describe a table using a "feminine" word, or a book using a "masculine" word, a Polish speaker will still understand you, but it will sound as strange as saying "he" when referring to a toaster in English. In Polish, Gender is not just about biological sex; it is a grammatical classification system. This system acts as a "code" that tells you how other words in the sentence (like adjectives or articles) must change to match the noun. Mastering this system is the foundation of the entire language. Understanding the Three Genders Polish divides all nouns into three primary categories: Masculine, Feminine, and Neuter. The good news is that you don't have to guess. In the vast majority of cases, the last letter of the noun tells you exactly which gender it belongs to. 1. Masculine Gender (Rodzaj Męski) Most masculine nouns end in a consonant. A consonant is any letter that isn't a vowel (including the diacritics and digraphs you learned in Module 1). Examples: Dom (House) – Ends in 'm' Pies (Dog) – Ends in 's' Telefon (Telephone) – Ends in 'n' Kawałek (Piece) – Ends in 'k' 2. Feminine Gender (Rodzaj Żeński) Most feminine nouns end in the letter -a. Examples: Kobieta (Woman) – Ends in 'a' Książka (Book) – Ends in 'a' Woda (Water) – Ends in 'a' Kawa (Coffee) – Ends in 'a' 3. Neuter Gender (Rodzaj Nijaki) Neuter nouns typically end in -o, -e, or sometimes -um. Examples: Okno (Window) – Ends in 'o' Piwo (Beer) – Ends in 'o' Imię (Name) – Ends in 'e' Słońce (Sun) – Ends in 'e' Muzeum (Museum) – Ends in 'um' --- The "Cheat Sheet" for Gender Identification To help you categorize nouns quickly, use this visual guide: | Gender | Common Ending | Example | English Translation | | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | | Masculine | Consonant | Chleb | Bread | | Feminine | -a | Gazeta | Newspaper | | Neuter | -o, -e, -um | Mieszkanie | Apartment | The Exceptions (The "Traps") Languages rarely follow rules 100% of the time. 4. Module 4: Present Tense Verbs and Basic Conjugation Patterns

The Engine of the Sentence: What is Conjugation? Imagine you are trying to tell a friend that you drink coffee, but your friend drinks tea. In English, the verb "drink" barely changes: I drink, you drink, we drink—only the "he/she" form gets a tiny "s" (he drinks). In Polish, verbs are more like chameleons. They change their endings based on who is performing the action. This process is called conjugation. Because the ending of the verb tells you exactly who the subject is, Polish speakers often drop the pronoun (like "I" or "you") entirely. If you hear the word piję (I drink), there is no need to say Ja (I), because the -ę ending already screams "I am the one doing this!" To master the present tense, you need to recognize the stem of the verb (the part that stays mostly the same) and the ending (the part that changes to match the person). --- Understanding the Person and Number Before we dive into patterns, let's establish the "who" of the sentence. Polish verbs conjugate based on these categories: 1st Person Singular: The speaker (I) 2nd Person Singular: The person being spoken to (You - informal) 3rd Person Singular: The person/thing being spoken about (He/She/It) 1st Person Plural: A group including the speaker (We) 2nd Person Plural: A group being spoken to (You all) 3rd Person Plural: A group being spoken about (They) --- Pattern 1: The -ować Verbs (The "Easy" Group) Verbs ending in -ować are some of the most common in Polish. They often describe activities or processes (e.g., pracować - to work, studjować - to study). The trick with -ować verbs is that the -owa- part of the stem disappears and is replaced by -uj- before you add the person ending. 6. Module 6: Personal Pronouns and Basic Sentence Structure

Who is Doing What? Meeting Polish Personal Pronouns Imagine you are at a café in Warsaw. You want to tell your friend that you are drinking coffee, but your friend is drinking tea, and the waiter is bringing the bill. In English, we rely heavily on words like I, you, he, she, we, and they to keep track of who is performing which action. In Polish, these words are called Personal Pronouns (zaimki osobowe). While they function similarly to English, there is a fascinating twist: because Polish verbs change their endings based on who is speaking (as you learned in Module 4), the pronoun is often optional. You can say "I am drinking" or simply "am drinking," and the meaning remains perfectly clear. The Singular Pronouns Let’s start with the individuals. Polish distinguishes between "you" (singular/informal) and different genders for "he, she, and it." Ja (I) – Used when referring to yourself. Ty (You) – Used for one person you know well (friends, family, children). On (He) – Used for males. Ona (She) – Used for females. Ono (It) – Used for neuter nouns (which you encountered in Module 3). Pro Tip: The "Hidden" Subject In Polish, the verb ending tells you who the subject is. For example, if you use the verb mieszkać (to live): Ja mieszkam w Warszawie (I live in Warsaw). Mieszkam w Warszawie (Live in Warsaw). Both are correct. Including ja or ty adds emphasis (e.g., "As for me, I live in Warsaw"), but in everyday conversation, Polish speakers often drop the pronoun entirely. The Plural Pronouns When talking about groups, Polish introduces a distinction based on the gender composition of the group. My (We) – Used for any group that includes the speaker. Wy (You all) – Used when addressing two or more people informally. Oni (They - Masculine Personal) – This is a specific category. Use oni if the group contains at least one man. One (They - Non-Masculine Personal) – Use one if the group consists entirely of women, children, animals, or inanimate objects. The "Oni" vs. "One" Logic: If you have a group of 99 women and 1 man, you must use oni. If you have 100 women and no men, you use one. --- Building Your First Sentences: The SVO Pattern Now that we have our subjects (the pronouns), we need to put them into a sentence. Polish generally follows the SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) structure, which is the same as English. Subject (Who) $\rightarrow$ Verb (Action) $\rightarrow$ Object (What/Who) Declarative Sentences A declarative sentence is a simple statement of fact. You have communicated the basic fact, but you haven't described it. Is it a red apple? A big apple? A sweet apple? Without adjectives, language is a black-and-white sketch. Adjectives are the colors, textures, and sizes that allow us to be specific. In English, adjectives are simple: "red" is always "red," whether you are talking about one red car or ten red cars. In Polish, however, adjectives are "social" words—they change their endings to match the noun they describe. 9. Module 9: Prepositions and Locational Language

Where is the Cat? The Logic of Space Imagine you are in a Polish apartment. You are looking for your cat, Kotek. You check the sofa, the table, and the kitchen. To find him, you can't just use nouns; you need the "glue" that connects objects to their positions. In linguistics, these are called prepositions. In English, we say "the book is on the table." In Polish, the word for "on" is na. However, Polish adds a layer of complexity: when you use a preposition, the ending of the noun that follows it often changes. This is part of the case system (which we touched upon in Module 3). For this module, we will focus on the most common patterns used to describe where things are and where they are going. Static Location: Where is it? When describing a stationary object (e.g., "The keys are on the table"), Polish uses specific prepositions. To express "is" or "are" in this context, we use the verb być (to be), specifically the third-person singular jest (is) or plural są (are). The Core Prepositions of Place Here are the most essential prepositions for describing location. For these examples, we will use the word stół (table) and dom (house). W (in / inside): Used for enclosed spaces. Jestem w domu. (I am in the house.) Klucze są w torbie. (The keys are in the bag.) Na (on / onto): Used for surfaces or open spaces. Książka jest na stole. (The book is on the table.) Kawa jest na stole. (The coffee is on the table.) Pod (under): Kot jest pod stołem. (The cat is under the table.) Nad (above / over): Lampa jest nad stołem. (The lamp is above the table.) Za (behind): Piłka jest za domem. (The ball is behind the house.) Przed (in front of): Samochód jest przed domem. (The car is in front of the house.) Między (between): This preposition usually requires two objects. Kot jest między stołem a krzesłem. (The cat is between the table and the chair.) A Note on Noun Changes (The Locative Case) You may have noticed that stół (table) becomes stole and dom (house) becomes domu. In Polish, when you describe a static location (where something is), the noun moves into the Locative case. As a beginner, you do not need to memorize the entire grammar table yet, but keep these general patterns in mind: 1. Masculine nouns ending in a consonant often add -ie or -u (e.g., stół $\rightarrow$ stole). 2. Feminine nouns ending in -a usually change to -ie (e.g., torba $\rightarrow$ torbie). 10. Module 10: Past Tense Verbs and Simple Narratives

Imagine Your Yesterday Imagine you are meeting a friend for coffee in Warsaw. They ask you, "Co robiłeś wczoraj?" (What were you doing yesterday?). Up until now, you have been able to describe your life in the present: I drink coffee, I live in a city, I speak a little Polish. But to share stories, describe a trip, or simply explain why you are tired today, you need the past tense. In English, we often just add "-ed" to a verb (walk $\rightarrow$ walked). In Polish, the past tense is more like a mirror: it doesn't just tell us when something happened, but who did it. The verb changes its ending based on the gender and number of the person speaking or being spoken about. The Anatomy of the Polish Past Tense To form the past tense, we start with the infinitive (the basic "to..." form of the verb). Let’s use the verb czytać (to read). 1. Find the stem: Remove the final -ć from the infinitive. Czytać $\rightarrow$ Czyta- 2. Add the "Past Tense Marker": For most regular verbs, we add the letter -ł-. This is the signal to the listener that we are no longer in the present. 3. Add the Gender Ending: This is where Polish differs from English. You must choose an ending based on whether the subject is male, female, or a group. Singular Forms (I, You, He/She/It) In the singular, there are two distinct paths: one for masculine and one for feminine/neuter. Masculine (Male speaker/subject): I (Ja): czytałem (I read) You (Ty): czytałeś (You read) He/It (On/Ono): czytał (He/It read) Feminine (Female speaker/subject): I (Ja): czytałam (I read) You (Ty): czytałaś (You read) She (Ona): czytała (She read) Neuter (Ono): It (Ono): czytało (It read) Crucial Note: Notice that the "He" form (czytał) and the "She" form (czytała) are simply the stem + the marker (-ł) or the marker + feminine ending (-ła). Plural Forms (We, You, They) When moving to the plural, Polish introduces a distinction between "groups containing at least one man" and "groups containing only women, children, or animals." Virile (Masculine Personal - Groups with men): We (My): czytaliśmy You (Wy): czytaliście They (Oni): czytali Non-Virile (Women, children, objects, animals): We (My): czytałyśmy You (Wy): czytałyście They (One): czytały Putting it into Practice: Regular Patterns Let's apply this to other common verbs. 11. Module 11: Future Tense and Simple Plans

Imagine Your Tomorrow Imagine you are sitting in a café in Warsaw. A friend asks you, "Co robisz jutro?" (What are you doing tomorrow?). To answer, you can't rely on the present tense patterns from Module 4 or the past tense narratives from Module 10. You need a way to project your actions into the future. Whether you are scheduling a doctor's appointment, planning a trip to the mountains, or simply deciding what to eat for dinner, you need the Future Tense. The good news? For beginners, the Polish future tense is one of the most straightforward parts of the grammar because it relies on a "helper verb" system similar to the English "I will..." The Foundation: The Helper Verb 'Być' In English, we often use "will" to indicate the future. In Polish, the word for "will" is derived from the verb być (to be). To form the most common type of future tense, you combine a conjugated form of będę (I will be) with the infinitive (the base form) of the action verb.
