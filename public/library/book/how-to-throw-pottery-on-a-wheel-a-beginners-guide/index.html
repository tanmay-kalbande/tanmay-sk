1. Introduction to Pottery Basics

The Alchemy of Earth and Water Imagine standing before a spinning wheel with a lump of grey, cold earth in your hands. Within an hour, that shapeless mass can become a delicate tea bowl, a sturdy vase, or a wide platter. This transformation feels like magic, but it is actually a precise interaction between chemistry, physics, and geometry. Before you can master the physical act of "throwing" (the process of shaping clay on a potter's wheel), you must understand your medium. Clay is not a static material; it is a living substance that changes its chemical properties based on how much water it holds and how high the kiln (the high-temperature oven used for firing) heats it. If you use the wrong type of clay for your project, or if you try to carve a piece that is too dry, your work will collapse or crack. This foundation—knowing your materials, your tools, and how to keep your workspace safe—is what separates a frustrating experience from a successful one. Understanding Clay Bodies In pottery, we use the term clay body to describe the specific mixture of clays and minerals used to make a piece. Different clay bodies are designed for different purposes, firing temperatures, and aesthetic results. As a beginner, you will primarily encounter three main types: earthenware, stoneware, and porcelain. Earthenware Earthenware is one of the oldest types of pottery. It is fired at relatively low temperatures, which means the clay particles do not fully fuse together. Characteristics: It is typically porous (meaning water can seep through the walls if it isn't glazed) and often comes in earthy tones like terracotta red. Pros: It requires less energy to fire and is often easier to work with for very basic shapes. Cons: Because it remains porous, it is not "vitrified" (glass-like and waterproof). It is more fragile and prone to chipping than higher-fired clays. Stoneware Stoneware is the "workhorse" of the pottery world and the most common choice for beginners learning to throw on the wheel. Characteristics: It is fired at much higher temperatures than earthenware. During this process, the clay undergoes vitrification, where the silica in the clay melts and fills the gaps between particles, making the finished piece dense and waterproof. Pros: It is incredibly durable, chip-resistant, and ideal for functional kitchenware (mugs, bowls, plates). Cons: It is heavier and denser than porcelain, which can be taxing on the wrists during long throwing sessions. Porcelain Often called "white gold," porcelain is a highly refined clay known for its purity and delicacy. Characteristics: It is composed of very fine particles, resulting in a smooth, white finish. 2. Clay Preparation and Wedging

The Invisible Enemy: Why We Wedge Imagine this: You’ve spent two hours at the wheel. You’ve successfully shaped a beautiful, elegant vase. You’ve let it dry to the stage of greenware, carefully loaded it into the kiln, and waited twenty-four hours for the firing to complete. You open the kiln door with anticipation, only to find a pile of jagged, ceramic shards where your vase used to be. What happened? Most likely, you left a tiny, invisible pocket of air trapped inside the clay walls. When clay is fired in a kiln, it undergoes vitrification, turning from a fragile earth material into a hard, glass-like stone. However, air behaves differently than clay. As the kiln temperature rises, any trapped air bubbles expand rapidly. Because the air is trapped inside the dense clay wall, it has nowhere to go. The pressure builds until the bubble literally detonates, blowing the piece apart from the inside out. To prevent this, we use a process called wedging. Wedging is the process of kneading clay by hand to achieve two primary goals: 1. Homogeneity: Ensuring the moisture level and density are consistent throughout the entire mass of clay. 2. De-airing: Forcing out trapped air bubbles so your work survives the kiln. If you skip this step, you aren't just risking an explosion in the kiln; you are making your life harder at the wheel. Air bubbles cause "bumps" and "wobbles" during the throwing process, making it nearly impossible to get the clay perfectly stable. Preparing Your Workspace Before you touch the clay, you need the right surface. You cannot wedge on a polished tabletop or a plastic sheet; the clay will simply stick to the surface, and you’ll spend more time fighting suction than preparing your clay. The ideal surface is a plaster bat or a sturdy piece of canvas stretched over a table. These materials are porous, meaning they absorb a tiny amount of moisture from the surface of the clay, providing just enough "grip" to allow the clay to move without sticking. Pro Tip: If you are using a canvas-covered table, ensure it is clean of dry clay dust. As we learned in the Introduction, remember to use a "Wet Clean" method to keep your area safe from silica. The Mechanics of Wedging Wedging is not just "squishing" the clay. It is a rhythmic, intentional movement. Think of it like kneading bread dough, but with a focus on pushing air out rather than incorporating it. Regardless of the specific technique you use, the goal is to fold the clay over itself repeatedly. 3. Centering the Clay

The Battle of Wills: You vs. The Clay Imagine you are sitting at the wheel. You’ve wedged your clay body perfectly, slapped it onto the center of the wheel head, and kicked the pedal to full speed. As you press your hands into the spinning mass, the clay doesn't move toward the center—instead, it pushes your hands away. It wobbles, it shudders, and it feels like the clay is winning a wrestling match. This is the most common experience for every beginner. The secret to centering is realizing that the clay is not an opponent to be defeated by brute force, but a physical material that responds to stability. Centering is the process of forcing the clay into the exact mathematical center of the rotating wheel. If the clay is not perfectly centered, every subsequent step—opening, pulling, and shaping—will be a struggle. Your walls will be uneven, and the pot will likely collapse. Centering is the foundation of everything that follows. Once the clay is "still" under your hands, the wheel does the work for you. Establishing Your Anchor: Posture and Bracing Before you touch the clay, you must prepare your body. Most beginners make the mistake of trying to center using only their arm muscles. Your arms will tire in minutes, and your hands will shake. To center clay, you don't use your muscles; you use your skeletal structure. The Power Position To achieve maximum stability, follow these postural guidelines: Sit Close: Pull your stool in close to the wheel. Your thighs should be almost touching the splash pan. If you sit too far back, you will lean forward, losing your balance and your leverage. Feet Flat: Place your feet firmly on the floor or the wheel’s footrest. This creates a grounded base that prevents you from being pushed backward by the clay. The "Tuck": Keep your elbows tucked firmly into your hips or braced against your inner thighs. By locking your elbows into your body, you transfer the pressure from your wrists and shoulders directly into your core. Lean In: Shift your weight forward. Your center of gravity should be over the clay, not behind it. The Golden Rule of Stability: If your arms are floating in the air, you are guessing. If your elbows are braced against your body, you are commanding. Lubrication: The Role of Water In the previous chapters, we discussed the properties of the clay body. Once that clay hits a spinning wheel, friction becomes your primary enemy. Friction creates heat and "drag," which causes the clay to stick to your skin. 4. Opening and Flooring

The Moment of Truth: Breaking the Surface You have spent the last several minutes fighting the clay, pushing it down and bringing it up until it sits perfectly still in the center of the wheel. It looks like a smooth, professional dome. Now comes the part that feels most like "creating": you are going to break that surface and carve out the interior space. For many beginners, this is where the first bit of anxiety sets in. There is a natural fear of pushing too far and poking a hole straight through the bottom—a mistake known as "blowing out the floor." Or, conversely, leaving the base so thick that the pot becomes a heavy, clunky object that is prone to cracking during firing. Opening and flooring is the process of transforming a solid lump of clay into a vessel. It is the foundation upon which everything else is built. If your floor is uneven or too thin, no amount of skill in later stages can fully fix it. Determining the Center Hole Before you touch the clay, you must decide where the "center of gravity" for your piece will be. While it seems intuitive to go exactly in the middle, the "visual center" and the "functional center" are sometimes different. Finding the Dead Center Using your needle tool, gently press into the top of your centered clay body. You aren't trying to carve a hole yet; you are simply marking the spot. The Golden Rule of Placement: Always leave enough clay on the sides to allow for the walls to rise. If you open the hole too wide too early, you will have very little clay left to pull upward, resulting in walls that are dangerously thin or a pot that collapses. The Depth Check Press the needle tool straight down. Stop when you are about half an inch (1.25 cm) from the wheel head. Too Deep: If you go too deep, you risk punching through the bottom. Too Shallow: If you don't go deep enough, you'll struggle to get your fingers into the clay to establish the floor. The Opening Process Opening is the act of creating the initial cavity. This requires a transition from the "aggressive" force used in Centering the Clay to a "controlled" pressure. The Initial Press Once your needle tool has marked the center, place your thumbs (or the index finger of your non-dominant hand, depending on your preferred grip) directly over the mark. Apply steady, downward pressure. Do not "stab" the clay. Instead, imagine your fingers are heavy weights slowly sinking into a sponge. 5. Pulling the Walls

The Magic Moment: From Bowl to Vessel Imagine you have a perfectly centered, opened cylinder of clay. It looks like a thick-walled hockey puck. You want to turn this into a vase or a tall mug, but as you try to push the clay upward, it suddenly wobbles, thins out at the bottom, and collapses into a slumped heap of mud. This is the "Collapse Point," and it is the most frustrating hurdle for every beginner. The collapse doesn't happen because you aren't strong enough; it happens because the clay is fighting physics. To lift the walls, you cannot simply "push" the clay up. You must move the clay through a coordinated dance of compression and synchronization between your two hands. When you master this, you experience the "magic moment" of pottery: the sensation of the clay becoming weightless and gliding upward, transforming from a heavy lump into a delicate, hollow form. The Mechanics of the Pull To move clay upward, you must create a "pinch" between your inner and outer hands. This pinch displaces the clay, forcing it to move in the direction of your travel. The Inner Hand: The Support The inner hand acts as the guide and the primary source of stability. Its job is to tell the clay where the wall should be and how thick it should be. Position: Your fingers should be tucked slightly into the interior of the pot. Role: The inner hand provides the "backstop." If the inner hand is too loose, the outer hand will simply push the clay into the center of the pot, causing it to collapse. Pressure: Apply a steady, firm pressure against the wall, but avoid "digging" into the clay. The Outer Hand: The Driver The outer hand provides the force that moves the clay. Position: Place your fingertips (or the fleshy part of your fingers, depending on the tool or grip) against the exterior base of the wall. Role: The outer hand "pushes" the clay upward. Pressure: The outer hand should generally apply slightly more pressure than the inner hand to effectively move the clay body. The "Pinch" (The Compression Zone) The space between your inner and outer fingers is the compression zone. By squeezing the clay between these two points, you create a concentrated area of pressure. Because the clay has nowhere else to go, it is forced upward. 6. Shaping and Refining

From Cylinder to Form Imagine you have just finished Pulling the Walls. You have a neat, straight-sided cylinder of clay sitting on your wheel. It is technically a success, but it is also a bit boring. A cylinder is the "blank canvas" of pottery; it is the essential foundation, but it isn't yet a bowl, a vase, or a mug. The transition from a cylinder to a finished shape is where the artistry of throwing truly begins. This process is called shaping. Shaping is the act of intentionally manipulating the clay's walls to move them inward or outward while the wheel is spinning. If pulling is about creating height and thickness, shaping is about creating volume and silhouette. Expanding into Bowls To turn a cylinder into a bowl, you must move the clay walls away from the center of the wheel. This is achieved through internal pressure. The Mechanics of Expansion Expansion happens when your internal hand pushes against the clay wall while your external hand provides a stabilizing "guide" or a slight counter-pressure. If you push from the inside without any external support, the clay will simply collapse or warp. 1. Positioning: Place your fingertips (or the palm of your hand, depending on the size of the piece) against the interior wall. 2. The Push: Slowly apply pressure, pushing the clay outward. Start from the bottom (the floor) and work your way up toward the rim. 3. The External Guide: Your outside hand should stay in contact with the clay, moving outward in sync with your internal hand. Think of the external hand as a "fence" that keeps the clay from bulging unevenly. 4. The Spiral: Do not push in one spot. Move your hands in a slow, spiraling motion from the base to the top. This ensures the expansion is even across the entire circumference. Managing the Rim As you expand a cylinder into a bowl, the rim will naturally want to get thinner because you are stretching the same amount of clay over a larger area. To prevent the rim from becoming fragile or "floppy," keep the rim supported with your fingertips as you expand. If the rim begins to wobble, stop expanding and use a light touch to steady it before continuing. Constricting for Necks and Shoulders While expansion moves clay away from the center, constriction (or collaring) moves the clay toward the center. This is how you create the "shoulder" of a vase or the narrow "neck" of a bottle. The Technique of Collaring Constricting clay is more dangerous than expanding it. 7. Trimming and Finishing

Why Trimming Matters: More Than Just a Cleanup Step Imagine this: you’ve just pulled up the walls of your first pot, shaped it carefully, and are ready to call it finished. You lift it off the wheel, set it aside, and admire your work—only to notice that the bottom is uneven, the clay is too thick in places, and the base doesn’t sit flat. It wobbles. It doesn’t look professional. This is where trimming comes in—not as an afterthought, but as the moment when your pot goes from “handmade” to “thrown with intention.” Trimming isn’t just about cleaning up excess clay or making the base look neat. It’s the final refinement that transforms a functional shape into a well-balanced, stable, and aesthetically pleasing piece. A well-trimmed pot will sit flat on a shelf, feel satisfying in the hand, and reveal the care you put into its creation. Without it, even a beautifully thrown form can feel unfinished or unstable. This chapter guides you through the why, when, and how of trimming—starting from identifying the right stage of dryness to securing your pot safely on the wheel, and finally, carving a clean foot ring. Whether you're making a mug, a bowl, or a vase, trimming is your chance to give your pot a professional finish—before it ever sees the kiln. --- The Leather-Hard Stage: Your Best Friend for Trimming Before you can trim, you need to know when your pot is ready. Clay doesn’t go from wet and workable to dry and brittle in one step—it passes through several stages, each defined by how much moisture remains. The stage you want is called leather-hard. What is Leather-Hard? Leather-hard clay is firm enough to hold its shape without collapsing, but still soft enough to be carved, trimmed, or refined with tools. It feels like firm cheese or stiff leather—hence the name. At this stage: - The clay is no longer sticky or plastic. - It can still be carved without cracking. - It won’t sag or distort under gentle pressure. - It’s cool to the touch but not damp. 🔍 Why not trim when it's wetter or drier? - Too wet (greenware stage): Clay is too soft. Trimming tools will tear it, and the pot may collapse or warp under pressure. - Too dry (bone-dry stage): Clay is brittle and will crack or chip easily. It’s nearly ready for firing, not trimming. 8. Drying, Firing, and Glazing

The Moment of Truth: From Fragile Greenware to Lasting Ceramics Imagine this: You’ve just finished trimming a delicate bowl, smoothing its rim with a rubber rib until it’s glass-smooth to the touch. You place it on the shelf to dry, satisfied with your work. Three days later, you reach for it—only to find it cracked in half. One small hairline fracture near the base, and your piece is ruined. The clay didn’t mean to break. It just couldn’t handle the stress of uneven drying. This is the moment when careful preparation meets unforgiving physics. Drying, firing, and glazing aren’t just final steps—they’re the bridge between greenware (your bone-dry pot, still fragile as a dried leaf) and a vitrified ceramic (a fired piece that’s hard, non-porous, and ready for daily use). These stages demand patience and precision. Skip a beat, and your hours on the wheel could end in heartbreak. This chapter will guide you through what happens after you trim your pot, how to prepare it for the kiln, and how to apply glazes that will transform raw clay into a durable, beautiful object. We’ll cover: - How to dry your piece slowly and safely - The difference between a bisque fire and a glaze fire - How to apply glaze evenly without ruining your pot - What the kiln actually does to your clay Let’s begin with the first and most overlooked step: drying. --- Drying: The Quiet Battle Against Cracks Drying is not passive. It’s a controlled race against time, where moisture must leave the clay evenly—or the piece will crack or warp. Freshly trimmed ware is still full of water, even though it looks dry. Greenware refers to any unfired clay object at this stage. It’s strong enough to handle, but only if you let it dry properly. Why Slow Drying Matters When clay dries, water evaporates from the surface first. The outer layers shrink, pulling on the still-wet interior. If the inner clay can’t shrink fast enough, stress builds up. Tiny cracks form—usually invisible until the piece is fired, when they widen into fractures. Scenario: The Rushed Bowl You set a freshly trimmed bowl on a hot windowsill to dry overnight. By morning, the rim is dry to the touch, but the base is still damp. The rim shrinks first, pulling the base inward. By noon, you hear a crack—and your bowl is now two pieces. The damage happened long before the bowl looked dry. How to Dry Your Piece Safely 1.
