1. What is Mahjong? History and Core Concepts

A Game That Began in a Parlor and Now Lives in Living Rooms Worldwide Imagine a rainy Saturday afternoon in 1920s Shanghai. A group of friends gathers around a low wooden table, the air thick with the scent of tea. One of them slides a stack of small, lacquer‑finished tiles across the surface. Laughter erupts as each player draws, discards, and shouts “Mahjong!” The simple act of moving tiles becomes a race to assemble a secret pattern—a “winning hand.” That snapshot captures the essence of Mahjong: a blend of chance, strategy, and social interaction that has traveled from Chinese parlors to cafés in New York, to family gatherings in Tokyo, and to online tables worldwide. By the end of this chapter you will know where Mahjong comes from, why people play it, and how the basic idea of building a winning hand works, plus you’ll recognize the major regional styles that give the game its rich diversity. --- 1. Historical Roots – From Chinese Pastimes to Global Phenomenon 1.1 Early Beginnings Late 19th century China – The most widely accepted theory places the birth of Mahjong in the provinces of Hunan and Anhui during the 1860s–1890s. It evolved from earlier tile‑based games such as “Ma‑Chi” (a game of “horse” or “horse‐chasing”) and the card game “Tien Gow”. Tiles as money – Early tiles were sometimes made from bone, ivory, or paper and carried monetary value, turning the game into a gambling pastime in tea houses and aristocratic salons. 1.2 The Spread Within China Republican era (1912‑1949) – As railways and telegraph lines linked distant cities, Mahjong spread rapidly. Clubs formed in Shanghai, Beijing, and Guangzhou, each adding local twists to the rules. Cultural acceptance – By the 1930s the game was a fixture of urban life, featured in newspapers, illustrated in popular magazines, and even used to teach basic arithmetic to children. 1.3 Crossing Oceans American soldiers in the Pacific – During World II, U.S. servicemen stationed in China encountered Mahjong and brought it home. 1920s‑30s boom in the United States – A wave of Chinese immigrants opened Mahjong parlors in San Francisco and New York. The game was marketed to Western audiences as a “exotic” social activity, leading to the first English‑language rulebooks. Post‑war television and film – Appearances in Hollywood movies (e.g., The Mahjong Girl) cemented the game’s image as a glamorous social ritual. 1.4 The Digital Age 1990s‑2000s – Computer and mobile versions appeared, allowing players to match with opponents across continents. Today – Online platforms host millions of simultaneous matches, and organized tournaments (both amateur and professional) draw participants from every continent. For a newcomer, this kaleidoscope can feel overwhelming—​but the good news is that every tile belongs to one of just four families: three numbered suits, the honor tiles, and the optional bonus tiles. By learning a handful of visual cues, you’ll be able to name every tile in seconds, laying the foundation for building a winning hand. 3. Setting Up the Game and Player Positions

A First‑Time Setup in Action Imagine it’s a rainy Saturday afternoon. Four friends have gathered around a low table, a fresh set of Mahjong tiles glinting in the soft light. One of them, Maya, has never played before, but she’s eager to join. Before anyone can even think about drawing or discarding, the group must build the wall, choose seats, and deal the tiles correctly. This chapter walks you through every step that Maya (and you) will need to follow, turning what can feel like a mysterious ritual into a clear, repeatable process. --- 1. Preparing the Tiles and Building the Wall 1.1 Organizing the Tiles All 144 tiles (as introduced in Mahjong Tiles: Types and Identification) should be placed face‑down on the table. The tiles are usually stored in a rectangular box; shake the box gently to randomize the order, then pour the tiles onto the playing surface. At this point the tiles are a random pool—no tile is yet assigned to a player or a specific location. 1.2 Constructing the Wall The wall is a four‑sided rectangle that mimics a city wall made of tiles. It serves as the source from which players draw tiles throughout the hand. 1. Form four rows of 18 tiles each (18 × 4 = 72 tiles per side). 2. Stack the rows into two layers: each side will have a lower row of 18 tiles and an upper row of another 18 tiles placed directly on top. 3. Push the sides together so the four sides meet, forming a solid square that measures roughly 2 × 2 tiles high. When the wall is complete, you will see a compact, 2‑tile‑high square surrounding the center of the table. It should look tidy, with no gaps between tiles. If any tiles are misaligned, gently slide them into place; a smooth wall makes the later steps (breaking and dealing) much easier. 1.3 Breaking the Wall Breaking determines where the wall will be “cut” to start dealing tiles. The break point is chosen by rolling dice, a tradition that adds a touch of chance to the setup. 1. Roll two six‑sided dice (or a single die twice). 2. Add the two numbers together. The total (2‑12) tells you how many stacks of two tiles you will count clockwise from the East side (the dealer’s side). 3. Count the stacks: each stack consists of two tiles (the upper tile of a column and the lower tile directly beneath it). 4. Mark the break: the stack you land on becomes the break point. 4. Turn Structure: Draw, Discard, and Meld

A Turn in Action Imagine the wind is blowing gently across a mahjong table in a quiet living‑room. The four players have just finished dealing the tiles, and each has a wall of faced‑down tiles in front of them. It’s East’s turn. He reaches for the first tile in his wall, slides it into his hand, and the whole game pivots on what he does next. The entire rhythm of mahjong—its tension, its strategy, its excitement—is contained in the three‑step loop of draw, examine, and discard, punctuated by the occasional opportunity to claim a discarded tile and meld a set. Mastering this loop is the first real step toward playing confidently. Below, we break down every action that occurs from the moment a player draws a tile until the next player’s turn begins. We’ll also show you exactly when you may reach across the table to claim a discard, and how a meld fits into the flow of a turn. A simulated turn with sample tiles at the end lets you practice the concepts in a concrete setting. --- 1. The Three‑Step Turn Cycle A mahjong turn follows a strict order that all players must respect. The cycle repeats clockwise (East → South → West → North) until someone wins. 1. Draw – Take a new tile from the wall. 2. Examine – Look at the tiles now in your hand, decide what you need, and consider any possible melds. 3. Discard – Choose one tile from your hand and place it face‑up in the discard pile. Only after the discard is completed does the next player become eligible to act. This order is immutable; you cannot discard before you have drawn, and you cannot claim a tile that you have already discarded yourself. 1.1 Why the Order Matters - Fairness: Keeping a consistent sequence ensures every player has the same number of opportunities to draw and discard. - Information Flow: The discard tile is the only piece of information you reveal to opponents each turn. If you could discard before drawing, you would be giving away a tile you haven’t even seen yet! - Meld Timing: The rules for claiming a discard (see Section 2) are tied to the moment the tile is placed in the discard pile. If the order were altered, the legal windows for claiming would become ambiguous. --- 2. Claiming a Discard – When and How When a player discards a tile, the three other players each get a brief, simultaneous chance to claim that tile to complete a set. 5. Forming Sets: Pungs, Chows, and Kongs

The Moment the Wall Turns Red You are sitting at the east side of the table. After a few turns you have drawn a 5‑Bamboo from the wall, and the player on your right discards a 6‑Bamboo. In your hand you already hold a 4‑Bamboo and a 7‑Bamboo. Instantly, three possible actions flash through your mind: 1. Form a Chow with 4‑Bamboo, 5‑Bamboo, 6‑Bamboo. 2. Form a Chow with 5‑Bamboo, 6‑Bamboo, 7‑Bamboo. 3. Pass and keep the 5‑Bamboo for a later meld. The decision hinges on the three fundamental meld types you will master in this chapter—Chow, Pung, and Kong—and on the rules that govern how and when each may be exposed. Let’s unpack each meld, see how they are built from the tiles you already know, and learn the exact steps for laying them on the table without breaking the rules. --- 1. Chow – The Straight‑Line Meld 1.1 What a Chow Is A Chow (sometimes called a sequence) is a set of three consecutive numbers from the same suit. The suit can be Dots, Bamboo, or Characters. The numbers must run in order, e.g., 2‑3‑4 or 7‑8‑9. The three tiles do not have to be identical—they are linked only by their numeric progression. Rule reminder: Only the suit (dots, bamboo, characters) matters for a Chow. Honor tiles (winds and dragons) can never form a Chow. 1.2 When a Chow May Be Formed A Chow can be declared only when you are receiving a discard from the player immediately on your left (the “previous” player). This is a direct consequence of the turn order introduced in Turn Structure: Draw, Discard, and Meld. If you draw a tile yourself, you may only use it to create a Chow after you have drawn and before you discard—still considered “your own” turn, but you cannot claim a tile you just drew from the wall to make a Chow with a discard from another player. | Situation | Allowed? | Reason | |-----------|----------|--------| | Discard from left neighbor | ✅ | Chow is a “call” on the preceding player's discard. | | Discard from right neighbor | ❌ | Only the player on your left may be used for a Chow. | | Tile drawn from the wall | ❌ | You must wait until you discard before forming a Chow with your own draw. | 1.3 Forming a Chow: Step‑by‑Step 1. 6. Basic Scoring and Winning Conditions

What Is a Mahjong? Imagine you are the last player to draw a tile. The wall is almost empty, and the tile you just lifted completes a perfect layout on the table. You call out “Mahjong!” and the room quiets. In Mahjong, Mahjong (sometimes written “Mah‑jong”) is the term for a winning hand—the specific arrangement of tiles that satisfies the game’s basic structural rule. A hand is considered a Mahjong when it contains four sets plus one pair. - A set can be a Pung (three identical tiles), a Chow (three consecutive tiles in the same suit), or a Kong (four identical tiles, which counts as a set for the purpose of the hand). - A pair is two identical tiles that are not part of a set. The hand may be completed in two ways: 1. Self‑draw – you draw the winning tile from the wall on your turn. 2. Discard win – another player discards a tile that you can use to complete your hand. Both methods give you a Mahjong, but they affect scoring (see the section on the dealer). Simple Scoring Patterns When you have a Mahjong, the next question is: How many points is it worth? For beginners we focus on a handful of basic scoring patterns that are easy to spot and that add extra points on top of the “base” hand. 1. All Pungs (All Triplets) - Definition: Every one of the four sets is a Pung (or a Kong). No Chow appears in the hand. - Why it matters: Pungs are considered “harder” to form than Chows because they require three identical tiles rather than three sequential ones. - Example hand: | Set | Tiles | Set | Tiles | |-----|-------|-----|-------| | Pung | 5‑5‑5 Dots | Pung | 2‑2‑2 Bamboo | | Pung | Red Dragon ×3 | Pung | 9‑9‑9 Characters | | Pair | 7‑7 Dots | | | This hand scores All Pungs in addition to the base points. 2. Pure Straight (1‑9 Straight) - Definition: The hand contains a Chow of 1‑2‑3, a Chow of 4‑5‑6, and a Chow of 7‑8‑9 all in the same suit. The three Chows together form a complete 1‑9 sequence. - Why it matters: Collecting a full suit from 1 to 9 is relatively rare, especially when the tiles are drawn from a shuffled wall. - Example hand (all Bamboo): | Sets | Tiles | |------|-------| | Chow | 1‑2‑3 Bamboo | | Chow | 4‑5‑6 Bamboo | | Chow | 7‑8‑9 Bamboo | | Pung | East Wind ×3 | | Pair | Red Dragon ×2 | This hand earns the Pure Straight bonus. 3. You announce “Ready” and begin playing defensively, watching the discards for the missing 5 Man. --- Essential Mahjong Vocabulary Even the simplest patterns are described with a handful of recurring terms. Mastering them will let you read score sheets, discuss strategies, and follow tournament commentary. - Dora – A bonus tile indicated by the tile turned up from the dead wall. Each dora in your hand adds one han (a scoring unit). - Ura‑dora – Hidden dora revealed after a win if you had declared Riichi. - Riichi – A declaration made when you are closed‑tenpai; you place a 1000‑point stick on the table and lock your hand. Declaring a Kan draws an extra tile from the dead wall and may reveal a new dora. - Yaku – Any valid pattern that qualifies a hand for scoring. 8. Basic Strategies and Decision Making

Assessing Hand Efficiency When the wall is still full and the game has just begun, the most common question a new player faces is “Which tile should I discard?” The answer depends on how efficiently the remaining tiles can be turned into the required four melds and a pair. Understanding the Shape of Your Hand 1. Count the completed melds – look for any Pungs (three‑of‑a‑kind) or Chows (three consecutive numbers in the same suit) you already have. 2. Identify “potential” melds – tiles that are one or two steps away from forming a meld, such as a 5‑6‑? or a 9‑9‑?. 3. Spot the “dead” tiles – isolated tiles that do not currently fit any potential meld and have few chances to become useful later. A hand that consists mainly of potential melds is efficient; a hand littered with isolated tiles is inefficient. The Discard Decision Tree | Step | Question | Reasoning | |------|----------|-----------| | 1 | Does the tile complete a meld? | Keep it – completing a set brings you closer to a winning hand. | | 2 | Is the tile part of a potential meld (one‑away or two‑away)? | Prefer to keep one‑away tiles; they are more flexible than two‑away tiles. | | 3 | How many copies of this tile remain in the wall? | Tiles with many copies left are safer to discard because the chance of drawing a needed replacement is higher. | | 4 | Is the tile “dangerous” for opponents? | If the tile could complete an opponent’s set, consider discarding a different, less risky tile. | | 5 | Does discarding this tile improve your hand’s shape? | Removing a dead tile often tightens the hand, making the remaining tiles easier to arrange. | Follow the steps in order; the first “yes” you encounter tells you to keep the tile, otherwise you move to the next step. 9. Understanding Regional Variations

A Game Night Across Borders Imagine three friends—Lena from Hong Kong, Mike from the United States, and Aiko from Japan—gather around a Mahjong table for the first time. The tiles are shuffled, the dice roll, and everyone reaches for a tile at the same moment. Lena immediately shouts “Mahjong!” when she completes a hand, Mike flips over a bright red Joker, and Aiko calmly declares Riichi before discarding. Within a handful of turns, the table is buzzing with questions: “Do we need the Joker?” “What does Riichi mean?” “Why does my hand feel worth more after a Dora appears?” The confusion isn’t about the basic mechanics—those were covered in earlier chapters (Turn Structure, Forming Sets, Basic Scoring). It’s about the regional rule variations that change which tiles exist, how points are counted, and even which strategies are most rewarding.
