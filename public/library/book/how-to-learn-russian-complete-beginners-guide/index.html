1. The Cyrillic Alphabet & Reading Basics

You are walking through an airport, and you spot a sign pointing to the gate for a flight to Москва (Moscow). You recognize the shape of the letters, but the word makes no sense. It looks like it should be pronounced "MocKBa," but why is there a capital "B" in the middle? This is the first trap of learning Russian. The Russian alphabet is called the Cyrillic alphabet, and it is named after Saint Cyril, a 9th-century Byzantine monk who, along with his brother Methodius, helped create an early version of this writing system to translate religious texts for Slavic peoples. Because Cyrillic shares a visual ancestor with the Latin alphabet you are reading right now, many letters look identical. However, looks can be deceiving. Some letters look familiar but sound completely different, while others look entirely alien but have straightforward sounds. To read Russian, you must temporarily suspend your instinct to apply English sounds to familiar shapes. We will break the 33 letters of the Russian alphabet into four manageable categories based on how they behave compared to the English alphabet. The "Freebies": Letters That Look and Sound the Same Let’s start with the easiest group. These five letters look exactly like their English counterparts and make the exact same sounds. When you see them, you can trust your instincts. А а: Sounds like the "a" in "father." К к: Sounds like the "k" in "kite." М м: Sounds like the "m" in "man." О о: Sounds like the "o" in "bore" (when stressed). Т т: Sounds like the "t" in "top." If you see the word том (which means "volume" as in a book, or "tomato" in slang), you can read it perfectly: tom. The False Friends: Familiar Shapes, New Sounds This is where most beginners stumble. These letters look like English letters, but they represent entirely different sounds. You will need to actively override your reading instincts when you see them. В в: Looks like English "B", but sounds like the English "v" (as in "van"). Е е: Looks like English "E", but sounds like the "ye" in "yes". Н н: Looks like English "H", but sounds like the English "n" (as in "nose"). Р р: Looks like English "P", but sounds like the English "r" (rolled slightly, like in Spanish). С с: Looks like English "C", but sounds like the English "s" (as in "sun"). У у: Looks like English "Y", but sounds like the "oo" in "boot". Х х: Looks like English "X", but sounds like the "ch" in the Scottish word "loch" or a hard "h" sound. Let’s look at a famous example: Россия (Russia). 2. Pronunciation Rules & Sound Patterns

The Power of the Accent Mark Imagine you are sitting in a Moscow café, trying to order a piece of cake. You learned the word for "cake" in Chapter 1: торт. You confidently tell the waiter you would like a "зáмок" (ZAH-mok). The waiter looks completely baffled, leads you to the door, and points down the street. What went wrong? You just asked for a "castle" instead of a "cake." In English, we usually guess a word's meaning from context, and while we stress certain syllables more than others (like the difference between RE-cord and re-CORD), the vowels themselves don't usually vanish entirely. Russian is different. In Russian, where you place the stress—the emphasis or loudness on a specific syllable—actually dictates what the word means. A stressed syllable is the part of a word that gets the most vocal emphasis. It is pronounced clearly, loudly, and at a slightly higher pitch. An unstressed syllable is softer, shorter, and often weaker. Because Russian stress can fall on practically any syllable in a word, the written language uses an accent mark (´) to show you exactly where the stress is. You will see this mark in textbooks, dictionaries, and language learning apps. In everyday Russian writing (books, signs, text messages), these marks disappear. Native speakers simply memorize the stress patterns. For now, rely heavily on the accent marks. Shifting the stress doesn't just change the nuance; it creates an entirely different word. Here are a few classic examples of how shifting stress changes meaning: зáмок (ZAH-mok) = castle / замóк (za-MOK) = lock мýка (MOO-ka) = flour / мукá (mu-KAH) = torment пéрекусить (PE-re-ku-sit') = to have a snack / перекусúть (pe-re-ku-SIT') = to bite through Stress is the anchor of Russian pronunciation. Once you know where the stress is, you can apply the rules that govern the rest of the language's sound system. Vowel Reduction: The Shape-Shifters Once you know which syllable is stressed, you can predict exactly how the vowels will sound. In Russian, vowels are only pronounced in their pure, textbook form when they are stressed. When they are unstressed, they undergo a process called vowel reduction. Vowel reduction means the vowel shortens, weakens, and often shifts its sound to become closer to a different vowel entirely. Think of how the word "about" is pronounced "uh-bout" in English. The unstressed "a" reduces to an "uh." Russian does this systematically. Let's look at how the most common shape-shifting vowels behave when they are not stressed. 3. Essential Phrases & First Conversations

Breaking the Ice: Your First Russian Words Imagine stepping off a train in Moscow or walking into a Russian bakery in your hometown. You catch the cashier’s eye. What do you say? Languages are not just academic exercises; they are tools for human connection. You have already done the hard work of learning the Cyrillic alphabet and mastering pronunciation rules. Now it is time to use those sounds to communicate. In this chapter, we will assemble your first functional toolkit in Russian. You will learn how to navigate the social nuances of greeting people based on the time of day, introduce yourself, and use the critical polite expressions that will mark you as a respectful guest or conversationalist. A quick note on reading: as you encounter new words, rely on the pronunciation skills you built in previous chapters. Russian writing is highly phonetic compared to English, so if you know the rules, you can read the words. Greetings and the Formality Divide Before we look at specific greetings, you need to understand a fundamental feature of Russian social interaction: the strict divide between formal and informal address. In English, we use the word "you" for everyone—our boss, our grandmother, a stray dog, or the President. Russian, like many European languages, splits "you" into two distinct categories: Вы (Vy): The formal "you" (also the plural "you"). You use this with strangers, people older than you, teachers, bosses, and anyone you have a formal relationship with. It shows respect and distance. Ты (Ty): The informal "you." You use this with friends, family members, children, and peers your own age. Note: You will learn how to conjugate verbs based on "Вы" and "Ты" in Chapter 6. For now, simply be aware that this formality divide governs how Russians interact, and it dictates which greeting you choose. Saying Hello The most common, all-purpose greeting in Russian is Здравствуйте (Zdravstvuyte). Usage: Formal and polite. Use it with strangers, shopkeepers, and elders. It can be used at any time of day. Pronunciation tip: The first "в" (ve) is silent due to rules of consonant clusters you learned in Chapter 2. It is pronounced roughly as ZDRAH-stvooy-teh. If you are speaking to a friend, family member, or a child, you drop the formal ending and simply say Привет (Privet). Usage: Informal. Pronunciation tip: Pronounced pree-VYEHT. 4. Nouns, Gender & the Case System Overview

The Russian Lego Set: Why Endings Matter More Than Order Imagine you are holding a simple set of Lego bricks. In English, if you want to say "The cat sees the dog," you rely on a very specific sequence of words. "The cat" must come first, followed by "sees," and then "the dog." If you shuffle those words around to "The dog sees the cat," the entire meaning of the sentence flips. The English language relies heavily on word order to tell you who is doing the action and who is receiving it. Russian works completely differently. Instead of relying on the order of the words, Russian relies on the endings of the words. Think of a Russian noun as a Lego brick, and its ending as the shape of the connecting part. If you want to show that the cat is doing the seeing, you snap a specific "subject" ending onto the word "cat." If you want to show that the dog is receiving the action, you snap an "object" ending onto the word "dog." Because of this, you can arrange Russian words in almost any order you like. Whether you say "Cat sees dog" or "Dog sees cat" (in Russian), the endings on the words themselves will tell you exactly who is the observer and who is being observed. This system of changing word endings to show their grammatical job in a sentence is called a case system. Before we dive into how this system works, we need to look at the bricks themselves: nouns. And in Russian, every single noun belongs to a specific gender. Russian Nouns and the Three Genders A noun is a word that represents a person, place, thing, or idea (like книга - book, Россия - Russia, or том - volume). In Russian, every noun has a gender. There are no "it" objects in Russian like there are in English. Instead, every noun is either: 1. Masculine 2. Feminine 3. Neuter While some nouns have biological gender (like "mother" or "father"), the vast majority of Russian nouns are assigned a grammatical gender simply based on how they are spelled. The gender of a noun is incredibly important because it dictates how the word changes when you use it in different cases. The Gender Rules: Look at the Ending The best part about Russian gender for beginners is that it is highly predictable. In Chapter 1, you learned the Cyrillic alphabet and how to read basic words. Now, you can use that knowledge to determine the gender of almost any Russian noun simply by looking at the last letter of the word in its dictionary form. 5. Pronouns & Possessives

Stepping In for Nouns Imagine you are at a busy market in Moscow. You point to a stack of books and ask the vendor, "How much is the book?" The vendor replies, "The book is 500 rubles." You hand over the money, and the vendor says, "Here is the book." In just a few seconds, the word "book" was repeated three times. It’s clunky and unnecessary. In English, you would naturally replace the second and third mentions with a shortcut: "How much is the book?" "It is 500 rubles." "Here it is." This linguistic shortcut is called a pronoun. A pronoun is a word that takes the place of a noun so we don’t have to repeat ourselves endlessly. In Russian, pronouns are the heavy lifters of the language. They allow you to track who is doing what, what belongs to whom, and which specific item you are pointing at. Because Russian relies heavily on endings to show how words relate to each other—a concept you encountered in our overview of the case system—pronouns are going to change their shape quite a bit. This chapter will equip you with the three most essential types of pronouns for basic conversations: personal pronouns (I, you, he, she...), possessive pronouns (my, your, his...), and demonstrative pronouns (this, that). We will focus on their base forms while looking ahead to how they will evolve as you learn more about Russian grammar. Personal Pronouns: Who is Doing the Action? Personal pronouns represent specific people or things. In English, they are words like I, you, he, she, it, we, and they. In Russian, these pronouns act as the subject of a sentence—the person or thing performing the action. When a pronoun is the subject, it is in the nominative case, which is the default, dictionary form of a word. Here are the Russian personal pronouns in the nominative case, alongside their English equivalents: Singular Pronouns Я (ya) — I Ты (ty) — You (informal, singular) Он (on) — He Она́ (a-na) — She Оно́ (a-no) — It Plural Pronouns Мы (my) — We Вы (vy) — You (formal, or plural) Они́ (a-ni) — They The Nuances of "You" In English, the word "you" does double duty—it can be used for one person or a crowd. Russian makes a strict distinction here: Ты is used for one person with whom you are on informal terms: friends, family members, children, and pets. Вы is used in two ways. First, it is the plural "you," used when addressing two or more people. Second, it is the formal "you," used when speaking to a stranger, an elder, a boss, or anyone you want to show respect to. 6. Present Tense Verbs & Conjugation

The Engine of the Sentence Imagine you are sitting in a café in Moscow. You want to tell your new Russian friend, "I read books and I drink coffee." You already know the nouns from our previous chapters—книга (book) and кофе (coffee). You even know the pronouns: я (I) and ты (you). But without the verbs—the action words—your sentence is just a pile of vocabulary. You can't express what is happening. Verbs are the engine of any language. They breathe life into static nouns and pronouns. In English, we create the present tense mostly by using the base word (I read, you read, we read) or by adding a simple suffix (she reads, he drinks). Russian works a bit differently. Instead of relying on separate words like "do" or "does," Russian verbs change their endings based on who is performing the action. Changing the ending of a verb to match the subject is called conjugation. By the end of this chapter, you will understand how to look at any basic Russian verb, figure out how it behaves, and conjugate it for all six grammatical persons. The Infinitive: Finding the Starting Line Before you can conjugate a verb, you need to find its starting point. In English, this is the "to" form: to read, to speak, to live. In Russian, this is called the infinitive. Russian infinitives are incredibly easy to spot. They almost always end in -ть. Here are some common Russian infinitives: чита́ть (to read) писа́ть (to write) говори́ть (to speak) жить (to live) рабо́тать (to work) The infinitive is your raw material. To make a sentence, you have to trim off the infinitive ending and attach a new ending that tells the listener who is doing the action. The Two Conjugation Patterns Russian verbs are remarkably organized. Almost every verb in the language belongs to one of two major groups: the First Conjugation or the Second Conjugation. Think of these as two different families of verbs. Each family has its own set of endings that you attach to the verb's stem. The good news is that if you memorize the endings for these two families, you can accurately conjugate thousands of Russian verbs. First Conjugation: The "-ать" Family The First Conjugation is the most common type you will encounter as a beginner. Most verbs that end in -ать belong to this group. Let's use the verb чита́ть (to read) as our model. To conjugate it, we drop the -ть to find the stem. The stem of читать is чита- (pronounced chi-ta-). Then, we add the First Conjugation endings. 7. Forming Questions & Basic Sentence Structure

The Building Blocks of a Russian Sentence Imagine you are walking through a park in Moscow, and you see a friend sitting on a bench. In English, if you want to find out what she is doing, you have to rearrange your sentence. You take "You are reading" and flip it around to create "Are you reading?" Russian does not work this way. To turn a statement into a simple question, you don't need to move words around or add extra helping verbs. You just change how you speak. Before we learn how to ask questions, let's look at the basic statements they are built from. By now, you are familiar with Russian nouns, pronouns, and present tense verbs from previous chapters. In Russian, a standard declarative sentence (a sentence that makes a statement) usually follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) order, much like English. Subject: The person or thing doing the action (e.g., Мама - Mom, Я - I). Verb: The action (e.g., читает - reads, работаю - work). Object: The person or thing receiving the action (e.g., книгу - book). If we put these together, we get: Мама читает книгу. (Mom is reading a book.) While SVO is the most common, neutral way to build a sentence, Russian word order is highly flexible. You can also say Книгу читает мама. Grammatically, this is perfectly correct, but the emphasis has shifted. We will explore exactly how and why Russian word order shifts later in this chapter. First, let's look at the easiest way to turn a statement into a question. Yes/No Questions: The Power of Intonation A "yes/no question" is a question that can be answered with a simple "yes" (да) or "no" (нет). To form a yes/no question in Russian, you take a standard declarative sentence and simply apply a rising intonation to the end of the sentence. You do not change the word order, and you do not add any new words. Statement (falling intonation): Анна работает. (Anna works / Anna is working.) Question (rising intonation): Анна работает? (Does Anna work? / Is Anna working?) Notice that the words are identical. When spoken, your voice drops at the end of the statement, but rises at the end of the question. When written, the only difference is the question mark at the end. Here are a few more examples: Statement: Ты знаешь. (You know.) Question: Ты знаешь? (Do you know?) Statement: Это Россия. (This is Russia.) Question: Это Россия? (Is this Russia?) Statement: Он дома. (He is at home.) Question: Он дома? (Is he at home?) Imagine you are standing in the middle of Red Square in Moscow. You want to buy three matryoshka dolls from a street vendor, figure out what time the Kremlin museums close, and plan a return trip to the city in October. Suddenly, the abstract world of grammar fades, and you are faced with the most practical language challenge of all: numbers, time, and dates. Russian numbers can seem intimidating at first glance because they behave differently than in English. But once you unlock their patterns, a whole new layer of communication opens up. Building on the Cyrillic alphabet, pronunciation rules, and basic sentence structures you have already mastered, this chapter will equip you to count, tell time, and plan your schedule in Russian. Cardinal Numbers: The Foundation of Counting Cardinal numbers are the basic counting words we use to answer the question "How many?" (one, two, three, etc.). In Russian, these numbers act as the building blocks for everything from buying groceries to telling time. Let us start with the basics: 0 through 10. 9. Cases in Practice: Nominative, Accusative & Prepositional

Why Russian Sentences Change Shape Imagine you are reading a simple Russian sentence: Мама читает книгу (Mom is reading a book). If you learned your vocabulary, you might look at the word книга (book) and wonder why it suddenly looks like книгу. The answer lies in the Russian case system. A case is a grammatical category that shows the role a noun plays in a sentence. Instead of relying entirely on word order—like English does—Russian changes the endings of nouns, pronouns, and adjectives to tell you exactly who is doing the action, who is receiving it, and where it is happening. Think of Russian nouns as actors in a play. When an actor changes costumes, you know they are playing a different role. When a Russian noun changes its ending, it is changing its grammatical role. In Chapter 4, we introduced the concept of the case system and met the Nominative case (the dictionary form of a word). In this chapter, we will put the Nominative to work alongside two new cases: the Accusative (used for direct objects and direction) and the Prepositional (used for locations and topics). By combining these three cases, you will unlock the ability to build complex, connected sentences in Russian. The Nominative Case: The Subject in Action The Nominative case (Именительный падеж) is the base form of a noun. When you look up a word in the dictionary, it is always in the Nominative. Its primary job is to act as the subject of a sentence—the person or thing performing the action. Because you have already learned present tense verbs (Chapter 6), you can easily identify the Nominative subject by asking "Who?" or "What?" before the verb. Книга лежит на столе. (The book is lying on the table.) What is lying? The book. Студент читает. (The student is reading.) Who is reading? The student. When a noun is in the Nominative case, you do not change its ending. You simply use the word as it is. The challenge arises when the noun stops being the subject and becomes the object of the action. The Accusative Case: Direct Objects and Motion The Accusative case (Винительный падеж) has two main jobs in Russian: showing the direct object of an action, and showing the destination of a movement. Direct Objects A direct object is the noun that directly receives the action of a verb. In the English sentence "Ivan reads a book," "Ivan" is the subject (Nominative) and "a book" is the direct object (Accusative). 10. Cases in Practice: Genitive, Dative & Instrumental

The Genitive Case: Possession, Absence, and Quantities Imagine you are at a Russian market. You want to buy a bottle of water, you notice a friend’s car parked outside, and you look for a section of books only to find it empty. In English, you might say "a bottle of water", "Anna's car", and "there are no books". Russian handles all three of these situations—quantities, possession, and the negation of existence—by using the genitive case. In Chapter 4, we introduced the case system, and in Chapter 9, you practiced using the nominative (for subjects), accusative (for direct objects), and prepositional (for locations). The genitive case is arguably the most frequently used case in Russian after the nominative. Its primary function is to show that one noun belongs to or is related to another noun. Forming the Genitive Case To use the genitive case, you need to change the ending of the noun. Russian nouns change their endings based on their gender (masculine, feminine, neuter) and whether they are singular or plural. Here are the basic rules for singular nouns: Masculine nouns: If the noun ends in a consonant (like том - volume/book) or -й, add -а. If it ends in -ь (soft sign), add -я. том → тома (of the volume) музей (museum) → музея (of the museum) Feminine nouns: If the noun ends in -а, change it to -ы. If it ends in -я or -ь, change it to -и. Россия (Russia) → России (of Russia) неделя (week) → недели (of the week) Neuter nouns: Change the ending -о to -а, and -е to -я. окно (window) → окна (of the window) For plural nouns, the genitive endings are: Masculine: Add -ов (or -ев if it ends in -й or a soft sign). Feminine and Neuter: Change the ending to -ей (if it ends in a soft sign, -я, or -е) or drop the -а/-о entirely and add a zero ending (nothing) for many hard-stem nouns. Possession and Relationship In English, we use an apostrophe plus "s" ('s) or the preposition "of" to show possession. In Russian, you simply put the possessor in the genitive case and place it after the thing that is possessed. книга брата (the brother's book / the book of the brother) машина Анны (Anna's car) Notice that neither "брат" nor "Анна" is the subject of the sentence; they are just identifying whose things they are. 11. Past & Future Tenses

Time Travel: Speaking in the Past Imagine you want to tell your new Russian friend what you did yesterday. You want to say, "I read a book," "I watched a movie," or "I worked." If you recall from Present Tense Verbs & Conjugation, Russian present tense verbs change their endings based on who is doing the action (I, you, he, she, we, they). But here is the best news you will hear all module: the Russian past tense is incredibly simple. Unlike the present tense, the Russian past tense does not care whether the subject is first person (I/we), second person (you), or third person (he/she/they). Instead, Russian past tense verbs only care about two things: gender and number. To form the past tense, you simply take the verb stem and add one of four endings: - -л (for masculine singular) - -ла (for feminine singular) - -ло (for neuter singular) - -ли (for all plurals, regardless of gender) Finding the Verb Stem How do you find the verb stem? The easiest way is to look at the infinitive (the basic, unconjugated form of a verb ending in -ть or -ти). If you drop the -ть, you are usually left with the stem. Let’s use the verb работать (to work) as an example. 1. Drop the -ть: your stem is работа-. 2. Add your past tense ending: - Я работал (I worked - speaker is male) - Я работала (I worked - speaker is female) Notice that there is no "I" (я) or "he" (он) built into the verb ending anymore. The verb simply acts as an adjective of sorts, matching the gender of the person or thing doing the action. Applying Gender and Number Because Russian nouns, pronouns, and possessives (covered in earlier modules) have gender, the verb must agree with them. Let’s look at how this works with the verb делать (to do/make). The stem is дела-. - Masculine (Он / Я, if male): делал (did/made) - Feminine (Она / Я, if female): делала (did/made) - Neuter (Оно): делало (did/made) - Plural (Они / Мы / Вы): делали (did/made) If a man named Ivan read a book, he says: Иван читал. If a woman named Anna read a book, she says: Анна читала. If a group of friends read a book, they say: Друзья читали. 12. Practical Vocabulary for Daily Life

Dining Out: Food, Menus, and Ordering You have learned how to conjugate verbs, tell time, and navigate the Russian case system. Now, it is time to put those skills to work in the real world. Imagine walking into a bustling café in Moscow or St. Petersburg. The aroma of fresh bread and coffee fills the air, and the waiter approaches your table. You are ready to order. In this section, we will build the specific vocabulary you need to read a menu, order food, and handle a basic restaurant transaction. Common Foods and Drinks Russian menus are usually divided into familiar categories. Knowing these root words will help you recognize what you are ordering. Meats and Proteins курица (koo-REET-sa) - chicken говядина (ga-VYA-dee-na) - beef рыба (RY-ba) - fish яйцо (yay-TSO) - egg Sides and Vegetables картофель (kar-TO-fel') - potato (formal/written) картошка (kar-TOSH-ka) - potato (informal/spoken, extremely common on menus for fried or mashed potatoes) рис (rees) - rice салат (sa-LAT) - salad хлеб (khlep) - bread Drinks вода (va-DA) - water кофе (KO-fe) - coffee чай (chay) - tea сок (sok) - juice пиво (PEE-va) - beer вино (vee-NO) - wine Reading a Menu When reading a Russian menu, look for these common headings: Закуски (za-KOOS-kee) - Appetizers / Snacks Супы (SOO-py) - Soups Горячее (gar-YA-chay-ye) - Hot dishes / Main courses Десерты (de-SER-ty) - Desserts Напитки (na-PEET-kee) - Drinks Ordering in a Restaurant In Essential Phrases & First Conversations, you learned basic greetings. When a waiter (официант - a-fee-TSIANT) approaches, they will typically ask: Что вы будете? (Shto vy BOO-de-ty?) — "What will you have?" To order, you can use the verb взять (vzyat' - to take), which is highly irregular but universally used for ordering food. The complete conjugation isn't necessary for a beginner; simply memorize the first-person form: Я возьму (Ya voz'-moo) — "I will take." Scenario: Ordering at a Café Официант: Здравствуйте! Что вы будете? (Hello! What will you have?) Вы: Здравствуйте! Я возьму курицу и картошку. (Hello! I will take chicken and potatoes.) Официант: Что будете пить? (What will you drink?) Вы: Чай, пожалуйста. (Tea, please.) When you are ready to pay, you can ask for the bill by saying: Счёт, пожалуйста (Shchyot, pa-ZHA-lus-ta). Grammar Note: When you say "Я возьму курицу," you are using the Accusative case (covered in Cases in Practice: Nominative, Accusative & Prepositional). Because курица is an animate, feminine noun, its Accusative form changes the ending from -а to -у.
