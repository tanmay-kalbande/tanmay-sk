1. The Chessboard and Setup

The Battleground: Understanding the 64 Squares Imagine a battlefield where two armies of equal strength face off across a small, checkered plane. There is no hidden information, no luck of the dice, and no terrain advantage given to either side. Yet, from this perfectly symmetrical starting point, billions of possible futures unfold. This battlefield is the chessboard, and before you can command your army, you must understand the ground they stand on. A standard chessboard consists of exactly 64 squares, arranged in an 8-by-8 grid. These squares alternate between two colors, traditionally light and dark (often referred to as "white" and "black" squares, even if they are physically cream and green, or blonde and brown wood). This alternating color pattern is not just for decoration; it is the fundamental geometry of the game. Every piece on the board interacts with this light-and-dark checkerboard in specific ways. Before placing any pieces, we must orient the board correctly and learn the specific names for the lines that run across it. The Golden Rule of Board Orientation If you have ever sat down to play a game of chess and suddenly realized the pieces feel "wrong," you likely had the board oriented incorrectly. There is one absolute, unbreakable rule for setting up the physical board before a single piece is placed: The bottom-right corner square from the player's perspective must always be a light square. If you are sitting behind the white pieces, the square on your extreme right (closest to you) must be light. If you are sitting behind the black pieces, the square on your extreme right must also be light. Because the board is symmetrical, if it is correct for White, it is automatically correct for Black. The Quick Check To quickly verify your board is oriented correctly: 1. Look at the square in your bottom-right corner. 2. Is it dark? Rotate the entire board 90 degrees in either direction. 3. Is it light? You are ready to begin setup. Getting this right is crucial. If the board is oriented backward, the starting positions of the pieces become fundamentally twisted, placing kings and queens on the wrong colored squares and breaking the foundational rules of the game. Ranks, Files, and Diagonals To navigate the 64 squares, chess players use a specific vocabulary to describe the lines that make up the grid. You can think of these like latitude and longitude lines on a map. There are three primary geometric concepts you need to know: ranks, files, and diagonals. 2. Piece Movement and Capturing

The Language of Movement Imagine stepping up to a chessboard for the first time. You know how to set up the 16 pieces, and you know the board is a grid of 64 squares. But right now, those pieces are just static wooden or plastic figures. To play the game, you need to know how to bring them to life. Every piece in chess moves differently. A knight does not glide like a bishop, and a rook does not step like a pawn. These unique movement patterns are the alphabet of chess—once you learn them, you can start forming strategies, launching attacks, and capturing your opponent's forces. Before diving into the specific pieces, we need to define one crucial term: capture. In chess, capturing means moving your piece onto a square occupied by an opponent's piece, removing that piece from the board, and placing your piece on that square. For almost every piece, the way it moves is the exact same way it captures. There is one major exception to this rule, but we will save that surprise for the pawns. Let's start by looking at the pieces that move in straight, predictable lines. The Rook: Master of Ranks and Files In your starting setup, your two rooks sit in the corners of the board. Rooks are heavy, powerful pieces designed for open spaces. A rook moves in straight lines: horizontally along ranks or vertically along files. It can move forward, backward, left, or right. As long as its path is clear, a rook can travel any number of squares in a single turn. Movement Rules for the Rook Direction: Horizontal or vertical only. Distance: Any number of empty squares. Obstructions: A rook cannot jump over any other piece—neither your own nor your opponent's. If an opponent's piece is sitting on a square along the rook's path, the rook can capture it by moving onto that square. However, the rook must stop immediately on that square; it cannot capture a piece and continue moving past it in the same turn. If one of your own pieces is in the way, the rook is blocked and must stop before reaching that square. A Concrete Scenario Imagine your rook is on the bottom-left corner of the board. You want to move it to the right, all the way across the first rank. However, your own knight is sitting halfway across the board on that same rank. Your rook can move up to the square just before the knight, but it cannot jump over it. To move past the knight, you would first need to move the knight out of the way. 3. Special Moves: Castling, En Passant, and Promotion

The Exceptions to the Rules In the previous chapters, you learned how each of the 16 pieces moves and captures. You learned that Rooks control ranks and files, Bishops patrol diagonals, and Pawns march forward one square at a time. But chess has a few surprises. To make the game more dynamic—and to reflect the historical realities of medieval warfare—chess includes three special moves that temporarily break the standard movement rules you just learned. These moves are not optional tricks; they are fundamental parts of the game. Two of them involve the Pawn and the King, the pieces you might initially think of as the simplest on the board. The third allows a Pawn to completely transform into a different piece. Mastering these three exceptions—Castling, En Passant, and Promotion—will dramatically change the way you play. Castling: Safeguarding the King In the opening moves of a game, your King is highly vulnerable sitting in the center of the back rank. Enemy pieces can easily swarm the center files and attack your King before you have time to mobilize your defenses. Castling is a special move that allows you to do two things at once: tuck your King away into a safe corner, and bring a Rook out toward the center where it can be useful. How Castling Works Castling is the only move in chess where you move two pieces in a single turn. The two pieces involved are your King and one of your Rooks. There are two ways to castle, depending on which side of the board you are moving toward: 1. Kingside Castling: You move your King two squares toward the Rook on the right side of the board (the Kingside). The Rook then jumps over the King, landing on the square immediately on the King's other side. 2. Queenside Castling: You move your King two squares toward the Rook on the left side of the board (the Queenside). The Rook then jumps over the King, landing on the square immediately on the King's other side. If you are playing as White, after Kingside castling, your King will rest on the G-file and your Rook on the F-file. After Queenside castling, your King will rest on the C-file and your Rook on the D-file. A Concrete Scenario Imagine you are playing White. Your King is on its starting square (E1), and your Kingside Rook is on its starting square (H1). To castle Kingside, you pick up your King and slide it two squares to the right, landing on G1. 4. Check, Checkmate, and Stalemate

The Ultimate Target Imagine you have fought your way through a long battle. You have captured your opponent's pieces, pushed your pawns, and maneuvered your major pieces into perfect positions. Suddenly, your opponent’s Queen slides down the board and lands directly next to your King. The game is over, right? Not quite. In many games, the goal is to capture a specific piece or flag. Chess is different. The ultimate target in chess is the King, but you never actually capture the King. Instead, the game ends the exact moment the King is trapped with no way out. Every move you make, every piece you capture, and every strategy you employ from the very first move is ultimately designed to protect your own King while hunting down your opponent's. To understand how a chess game is won, lost, or tied, you must understand the three distinct states of the King: Check, Checkmate, and Stalemate. Defining Check In chess, the King is the only piece that cannot be removed from the board. Because of this, the game needs a way to signal that the King is in danger. When an opponent's piece directly attacks your King, your King is in Check. Think of it like an alarm bell ringing. If an enemy Bishop is pointing directly at your King along an empty diagonal, the King is in check. If an enemy Knight jumps to a square where it threatens the King, the King is in check. If an enemy Rook is on the same rank with nothing blocking its path to your King, the King is in check. The Golden Rule of Check There is one absolute, unbreakable rule in chess that overrides all others: You can never make a move that leaves your King in check. Because you cannot leave your King in check, a check is not just a threat—it is a forced action. If your King is in check, you must deal with it immediately on your very next turn. You cannot ignore the check to capture a different piece, push a pawn, or develop a Knight. You cannot build an empire while your own castle is on fire. Furthermore, you are never allowed to move your King into a square that is attacked by an enemy piece. If you do, it is an illegal move, and you must take it back and choose something else. The Three Ways to Escape Check When your King is placed in check, the situation might seem dire, but you always have exactly three legal ways to escape. 5. Basic Chess Notation

The Language of Chess Imagine playing a game of chess without a board. You are sitting at a desk with a friend across the country on the phone. You say, "I'm moving my knight to that square two spaces in front of my rook." Your friend asks, "Which rook? The one on the left or the right? And what do you mean by 'two spaces in front'?" Communicating chess moves verbally without a shared visual reference is incredibly frustrating. Even when you are sitting face-to-face with a board, trying to recall exactly how a game progressed ten moves ago can feel nearly impossible. To solve this problem, chess players developed a universal writing system. Just as musicians use sheet music to record exactly how a song should be played, chess players use algebraic notation to record exactly how a game is played. By learning this system, you gain the ability to read classic games played by masters, replay your own games to find your mistakes, and communicate moves clearly with other players anywhere in the world. The Coordinate System: Mapping the Board In Chapter 1, we introduced the 64 squares of the chessboard and defined the terms rank (the horizontal rows) and file (the vertical columns). To write down moves, we need a way to give every single one of those 64 squares a specific name or "address." We do this by combining the files and ranks into a simple coordinate system, much like the grid on a map. Files: The Letters From White's perspective, the eight vertical files are labeled with lowercase letters from a to h. The file furthest to White's left is the a-file. The file furthest to White's right is the h-file. The letters always run sequentially: a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h. Ranks: The Numbers The eight horizontal ranks are labeled with numbers from 1 to 8. The rank where White's 16 pieces start the game is the 1st rank. The rank where Black's 16 pieces start the game is the 8th rank. The numbers increase as you move up the board from White's side to Black's side. Finding the Square's Address Every square's name is simply the combination of its file letter and its rank number. The letter always comes first, followed by the number. Look at the corners of the board: White's bottom-left corner is a1. White's bottom-right corner is h1. 6. Piece Values and Exchanges

The Price Tag on Every Piece Imagine walking into a grocery store where every item has no listed price. You could trade an apple for a whole watermelon, or give up a steak for a pack of gum, simply because no one told you how to compare their worth. Chess operates on a similar principle. From the moment you learned how the pieces move, you gained the ability to capture your opponent's pieces. But should you? If your opponent offers you their Queen, but in doing so threatens to capture your Rook, should you take the trade? To answer this, chess players use a universal scoring system. By assigning a numerical value to each piece, you can instantly evaluate whether a trade (often called an exchange) is favorable, equal, or a disastrous blunder. Here are the standard point values you need to memorize: Pawn: 1 point Knight: 3 points Bishop: 3 points Rook: 5 points Queen: 9 points Notice that the King is missing from this list. The King is priceless. Because the game ends in checkmate when the King is trapped, you cannot capture a King, and you cannot trade it away. Its value is infinite. Why These Numbers? You might wonder why a Knight is worth 3 and a Rook is worth 5. These numbers weren't handed down by ancient decree; they were developed over centuries of play to reflect a piece's power, speed, and range of motion. Pawns (1): They move slowly, only one square at a time (except on their first move), and can only capture diagonally. They are the infantry of the board. Minor Pieces (3): Knights and Bishops are collectively known as minor pieces. They are roughly equal in value, though their strengths differ. Bishops can sprint across open diagonals, while Knights possess the unique ability to jump over other pieces. Rooks (5): Rooks are major pieces. Because they control entire ranks and files, and can move any number of squares in a straight line, they are far more powerful than minor pieces. Queen (9): The Queen combines the moving power of both a Rook and a Bishop. She is the most devastating attacker on the board, worth slightly less than two Rooks (10 points) but more than any other combination of pieces. Calculating Exchanges Knowing the point values is only half the battle. The real skill lies in calculating the net material—the total point balance after a sequence of captures takes place. When pieces are captured back and forth, you aren't just looking at what you took; you must subtract what you lost in the process. 7. Opening Principles

The First Moves Matter Imagine walking onto a battlefield, and instead of organizing your troops, you immediately send your most powerful soldier on a solo mission deep into enemy territory. In chess, this is exactly what happens when a beginner brings their Queen out on move two. The opponent attacks it with a minor piece, forcing the Queen to retreat. They attack it again, forcing another retreat. Within a few moves, the opponent has developed their army, while you have wasted time moving the same piece around the board. This scenario illustrates a critical chess concept: tempo. A tempo (plural: tempos) is a single move or turn. When you waste a move, you lose a tempo; when you force your opponent to waste a move, you gain a tempo. The player who develops their pieces fastest gains an advantage in time. The opening phase of a chess game is a race to prepare your army for the middlegame. While there are thousands of specific opening sequences memorized by advanced players, you don't need to memorize anything to play a strong opening. Instead, you can rely on three foundational principles: 1. Control the center of the board with pawns and pieces. 2. Develop minor pieces (knights and bishops) before moving the same piece twice. 3. Prioritize king safety by castling within the first several moves. If you apply these three rules during the first ten moves of your games, you will consistently reach the middlegame with a solid, safe, and active position. Controlling the Center In Chapter 1, we identified the 64 squares that make up the chessboard. The most important squares on that board are the four in the very middle: e4, d4, e5, and d5. This 4x4 area is known as the center. Why is the center so important? Geometry. A piece placed in the center controls more of the board than a piece placed on the edge or in the corners. A Knight on e4 attacks eight squares. A Knight on a1 attacks only two. Furthermore, pieces in the center can quickly swing to either side of the board (the queenside or the kingside) to launch attacks or defend. Pieces on the edge are slow to reposition. Using Pawns to Claim Territory Pawns are the infantry of your army. Because they cannot move backward, every pawn push permanently alters the landscape of the board. Moving a pawn into the center (advancing a pawn to e4, d4, e5, or d5) stakes a claim in the middle of the board. Central pawns do two things: They physically occupy central squares, preventing enemy pieces from settling there. 8. Middlegame Fundamentals and Tactics

The Transition to the Middlegame You have developed your pieces, controlled the center, and safely tucked your King away behind a wall of Pawns. The Opening Principles have done their job. But suddenly, the opening is over. There are no more obvious "developing" moves to make, and the board is crowded with pieces from both armies staring each other down. Welcome to the middlegame. The middlegame is the heart of a chess game. It is the phase where your opening preparation transitions into active battle. Because the board is still crowded and Kings have castled to opposite sides, the middlegame is where the vast majority of games are decided. To navigate this chaotic phase, you need two things: strategy (long-term plans) and tactics (short-term sequences of moves to win material or deliver Checkmate). Hanging Pieces and Undefended Squares Before looking at complex attacks, you must learn to spot the simplest errors. In chess, a piece is hanging if it is undefended and can be captured for free. A piece is undefended (or "loose") if no friendly pieces are guarding it. Beginner games are rarely won by brilliant, 10-move-deep combinations. They are won by the player who notices their opponent left a piece undefended. Checking for Safety After your opponent makes a move—and before you make your own—run a quick mental scan of the board: 1. Did my opponent just move a piece to a square where it is undefended? 2. Did my opponent’s move leave another piece undefended? (For example, if a Knight was defending a Rook, and that Knight moves away, the Rook is now loose). 3. Are any of my pieces currently hanging? The "Loose Piece" Combination Tactics become much easier to execute when your opponent has undefended pieces. A common beginner error is leaving two pieces undefended on the same rank, file, or diagonal. If you spot two enemy pieces sitting side-by-side on the same line with no protection, you have the ingredients for a tactical strike. Basic Tactical Motifs A tactic is a sequence of moves that forces a specific, favorable outcome. Usually, this means winning material (capturing a piece for free or gaining a higher-value piece in exchange for a lower-value one) or delivering Checkmate. The opponent can only move one piece out of danger on their turn, allowing you to capture the other. While any piece can execute a fork, the Knight is the most famous fork artist. 9. Basic Endgames

The Final Frontier: Why the Endgame Matters Imagine this: hours of calculation, fierce battles, and sacrificed pieces have left the chessboard nearly empty. You have a King, a single Rook, and one remaining Pawn. Your opponent has only their King and a Pawn. The opening principles and middlegame tactics you used to get here are gone. Now, the board feels vast, and your surviving pieces have immense room to maneuver. This is the endgame—the final phase of a chess game. When few pieces remain, the King transforms from a vulnerable liability that must be hidden away into a powerful, active combatant. For beginners, the endgame often feels like unfamiliar territory. You might know how to start a game and how to launch a middlegame attack, but what happens when the dust settles? Endgames require precision. A single tempo (the chess equivalent of a single move or "turn") can mean the difference between a glorious victory and a frustrating draw. In this chapter, we will focus on the most fundamental endgame scenarios. You will learn how to use your King as an aggressive piece, how to force a checkmate with a lone Queen or Rook, and how to march a Pawn to the end of the board to promote it into a Queen. The King Awakens: Active King Play In the opening and middlegame, you castled your King early to keep it safe from early attacks. But in the endgame, with most of the heavy artillery off the board, the risk of an early checkmate vanishes. Now, your King must leave its fortress. An active King is one that moves toward the center of the board (or toward the action) to support your remaining pieces, attack enemy Pawns, or restrict the enemy King. Because the King can move one square in any direction, it controls up to eight squares around it. In an empty board, an active King is a formidable fighting force. Forcing a Draw: The Concept of Opposition Before we learn how to checkmate, we must understand a critical endgame concept called the opposition. Opposition occurs when two Kings face each other on a file or rank with exactly one square in between them. The player who does not have to move is said to "have the opposition." Because Kings cannot move adjacent to one another, the player forced to move must step aside, allowing the other King to advance past the blockade. Why does this matter? Having the opposition allows you to control the movement of the enemy King. You can use it to force your King deeper into enemy territory, or to defend your own territory. 10. Playing Your First Game and Etiquette

Stepping Into the Arena You sit down across the 64 squares. Your 16 pieces are arranged perfectly, with your Queen on her own color and your Rooks nestled in the corners. You have spent time learning how the pieces move, studied Opening Principles, practiced Middlegame Fundamentals and Tactics, and drilled Basic Endgames. You know how to recognize Check, deliver Checkmate, and avoid Stalemate. You even know how to write down your moves using Basic Chess Notation. But playing a formal, over-the-board game against another human being involves more than just knowing the rules of the pieces. It requires a physical and psychological transition from solving puzzles on a screen to moving physical pieces in a shared space. Formal chess has its own culture, governed by a set of unspoken rules and strict physical protocols designed to ensure fairness. Whether you are playing in a local park, a school club, or your first official tournament, understanding chess etiquette and the mechanics of a formal game is essential. This chapter bridges the gap between knowing how to play chess and knowing how to actually sit down and play a game. The Physical Mechanics of Play When playing informally with a friend at home, you might slide pieces around carelessly or fix a misaligned pawn without a second thought. In a formal game, the physical way you interact with the board is strictly regulated. The Touch-Move Rule The most important rule of over-the-board chess is the Touch-Move rule. It is exactly what it sounds like: 1. If you touch a piece with the intention of moving it, you must move that piece—provided it has at least one legal move. 2. If you touch an opponent's piece with the intention of capturing it, you must capture that piece—provided it is legally possible to do so. 3. If you let go of the piece, the move is final. You cannot take it back or move it to a different square. The rule exists to prevent players from testing out moves on the board, moving pieces back and forth to visualize different scenarios before making a decision. In formal chess, you must calculate the variations in your head. There is one crucial exception: if you accidentally knock over a piece while reaching for another, or if you simply brush against a piece without intending to move it, you are not forced to move it. If you need to move a piece because it is crooked or not centered on its square, you must follow a specific procedure.
