2. How Water Enters and Moves Through a Home

From the Street to Your Faucet Imagine it’s a hot summer afternoon. You turn the kitchen tap on, and instead of a steady stream, only a sputtering trickle comes out. You check the other faucets—same result. The problem isn’t a leaky faucet or a clogged aerator; the whole house has lost pressure. Before calling a plumber, you need to know where the water is coming from, how it travels, and where you can stop it. Understanding the path of water through your home is the foundation for every repair you’ll learn later in this book. --- 1. The Big Picture: Water’s Journey Into the Home 1.1 The municipal supply side 1. Water source – A city or utility draws water from a river, reservoir, or groundwater well. 2. Treatment plant – The water is filtered, disinfected (usually with chlorine), and tested for quality. 3. Distribution mains – Large‑diameter pipes (often 12–24 in.) carry the treated water through the neighborhood. 4. Service line – A smaller pipe (typically ¾‑in. to 1‑in.) branches off the main and runs from the street curb to your house. All of this happens underground and is outside the homeowner’s control. What you can control begins the moment the service line reaches your property. 1.2 The house‑side side When the service line arrives at the foundation, it usually connects to three key components in this order: 1. Water meter – Measures how much water you use (for billing). 2. Pressure regulator (or pressure‑reducing valve, PRV) – Lowers the high city pressure to a safe level for residential plumbing. 3. Backflow preventer – Stops contaminated water from flowing backward into the public supply. From there, water flows into the main shut‑off valve, then branches out through a network of pipes, fittings, and individual shut‑off valves to every fixture (sink, shower, washing machine, etc.). --- 2. Key Components and Their Locations 2.1 Water Meter What it does: A mechanical or digital device that records the volume of water passing through. Typical location: Usually mounted on the outside of the house near the curb or on a concrete pad in the front yard. In some newer homes it may be inside the utility room, but the reading must still be accessible for the utility crew. 2.2 Pressure Regulator What it does: Reduces the incoming pressure (often 80–100 psi) to a safer range (typically 45–55 psi). This protects pipes, fixtures, and appliances from excess stress. Typical location: Directly downstream of the water meter, either outside (exposed to the elements) or inside the house near the main shut‑off valve. 2.3 Backflow Preventer What it does: Allows water to flow only toward the house. A Morning Surprise You’re halfway through your coffee when you hear a steady hiss from the bathroom. The toilet’s water level never drops, and the bowl stays half‑full even after you’ve flushed. A quick glance confirms the problem: the toilet is running constantly. It’s a common symptom that most homeowners can fix without calling a plumber—if you know where to look and what to do. --- How a Toilet Flushes A modern gravity‑fed toilet consists of two main chambers: the bowl (where waste is deposited) and the tank (which stores the water that creates the flushing force). Inside the tank live three essential parts that work together each time you push the handle: | Part | What It Does | Typical Location | |------|--------------|------------------| | Flapper | A rubber (or silicone) valve that lifts to release water from the tank into the bowl. | Hangs from the overflow tube and seals the bottom of the tank. | | Fill Valve (also called a ballcock) | Refills the tank after a flush and controls the water level. | Mounted on the side of the tank, connected to the water supply line. | | Overflow Tube | Prevents the tank from over‑filling; also serves as the anchor for the flapper. | Runs vertically from the bottom of the tank to the bowl’s flush opening. | A float (either a ball on a rod or a cup that slides on the fill valve) rises with the water level and tells the fill valve when to shut off. When you press the flush lever, a lift‑arm pulls the flapper upward, allowing the stored water to surge into the bowl. As the tank empties, the float drops, the fill valve opens, and water refills the tank until the float reaches its preset height, at which point the valve closes and the flapper settles back onto the overflow tube. 6. Installing and Adjusting Shut‑off Valves

Why Shut‑off Valves Matter Imagine you’re about to replace the faucet in your kitchen. You’ve gathered the new fixture, tightened the supply lines, and are ready to turn the water back on—only to discover a steady drip leaking from the pipe under the sink. The whole project stalls, and you’re left scrambling for a bucket. A functional shut‑off valve at each fixture prevents exactly this scenario. It lets you isolate a single line without touching the rest of the house’s plumbing, saving time, water, and frustration. In this chapter you’ll learn how to recognize the three most common valve types, safely turn off the main water supply, install a fresh angle‑stop valve using compression fittings, and verify that everything works leak‑free. --- Three Common Shut‑off Valve Types Shut‑off valves come in three basic designs that you’ll encounter in most homes. Knowing their shape and operation helps you pick the right replacement or decide whether a valve can be adjusted instead of swapped. Ball Valve - Shape & Operation: A hollow sphere (the “ball”) with a hole drilled through its center. Rotating the ball 90° aligns the hole with the pipe, allowing flow; turning it perpendicular blocks the water. - Typical Uses: Main water lines, outdoor irrigation, and any location where quick, full‑on/off control is needed. - Advantages: Durable, easy to operate with a single turn, and resistant to wear. - Limitations: Not ideal for throttling (partially opening) because the flow changes abruptly. Gate Valve - Shape & Operation: A metal “gate” that slides up and down inside the valve body. When the gate is lifted, water passes; when lowered, the gate seals against a seat to stop flow. - Typical Uses: Older homes, water mains, and low‑pressure applications where a gradual opening is useful. - Advantages: Handles high pressure and can be used for throttling. - Limitations: Requires more turns to open/close, and the internal seat can wear over time, leading to leaks. Angle Stop (Angle‑stop Valve) - Shape & Operation: A compact, L‑shaped valve that combines a compression fitting on one end (to attach to the pipe) with a faucet‑type handle on the other. Turning the handle opens or closes the flow. - Typical Uses: Individual fixtures such as sinks, toilets, and washing machines. - Advantages: Small footprint, easy to access, and designed for quick fixture isolation. - Limitations: Usually limited to low‑pressure, indoor lines; not suitable for main‑line service. 8. Minor Pipe Repairs: Patching Leaks and Replacing Sections

You are grabbing a storage bin in the basement when your hand brushes against a cold copper pipe. A tiny jet of water sprays across your knuckles. Upon closer inspection, you spot a pinhole leak actively misting the surrounding drywall. A leaking pipe does not always announce itself with a dramatic flood. Often, it starts as a slow drip or a fine mist caused by internal corrosion. Left alone, that tiny leak can ruin cabinetry, breed mold, and eventually burst. Fortunately, you do not always need to call a professional or replumb your entire home to fix a minor pipe failure. If you can locate the leak and assess the damage, several accessible repair methods can have your plumbing watertight again within an afternoon. Locating and Assessing a Leaking Pipe Segment Water is notoriously deceptive. Because water flows downward and follows the path of least resistance, a water stain on your ceiling rarely means the leak is directly above it. The water might have traveled down a pipe, pooled on a horizontal surface, and dripped several feet away from the actual source. Tracing the Leak If you see water accumulating, start by drying the area completely. Once the floor and surrounding pipes are dry, lay down a piece of paper towel or a dry rag along the suspected pipe run. Watch closely to see which section of the paper towel absorbs water first. Use a flashlight to inspect the underside of pipes, where condensation and leaks often hide. Assessing the Damage Once you locate the source, determine what type of failure you are dealing with: Pinhole Leaks: Tiny, circular holes often found in copper pipe. They usually result from pitting corrosion inside the pipe and spray a fine mist. Hairline Cracks: Thin fractures along the length of a pipe or around a joint. These often weep water rather than spray it. Joint Leaks: Water escaping from where two pipes meet. If you previously completed the Repairing Leaky Faucets or Installing and Adjusting Shut‑off Valves modules, you know that threaded joints rely on Teflon tape and proper tightening. If a threaded joint is weeping, drying it and carefully tightening the fitting might solve the problem. If the joint is a soldered joint, it may need to be re-heated or cut out. Punctures: A physical hole caused by a nail, screw, or drill bit piercing the pipe wall. Before making any repairs, identify your pipe material. Copper is rigid and metallic; PEX is flexible plastic; CPVC is rigid plastic; and galvanized steel is heavy, magnetic metal. 9. Preventive Maintenance and Knowing When to Call a Professional

Imagine lifting a cardboard box stored under your kitchen sink, only to find a black, fuzzy patch of mold creeping up the drywall, and the cabinet floor beneath it soft and spongy. A slow drip from a hidden pipe joint has been silently causing damage for months. This scenario is the exact reason preventive maintenance is the most valuable skill a homeowner can develop. You have already learned how to fix leaks, clear drains, and maintain your water heater; now it is time to learn how to prevent these issues from happening in the first place—and how to recognize when a problem has outgrown your toolbox. The Seasonal Plumbing Checklist Plumbing systems endure constant stress from temperature fluctuations, daily use, and natural wear. By breaking maintenance down into seasonal tasks, you can catch minor vulnerabilities before they become catastrophic failures. Spring: Checking for Winter Damage Winter is notoriously hard on plumbing. As temperatures rise in the spring, it is time to inspect what the cold may have wrought. Check exposed pipes for frost damage: Look closely at pipes in unheated areas like basements, crawlspaces, and garages. Even if a pipe didn't burst, a hard freeze can weaken joints or stretch fittings, leading to slow leaks. Test outdoor hose bibs: Open your outdoor spigots for the first time. If water leaks from the wall or the flow is weak, you may have a cracked pipe inside the wall caused by freezing. Clean faucet aerators: Mineral deposits from hard water build up in the small screens at the end of your faucets, known as aerators. Unscrew them, rinse out the debris, and soak them in vinegar to restore proper water flow. Inspect toilet bases: Give each toilet a gentle nudge. If it rocks, the seal at the base (the wax ring) may be compromised. A rocking toilet will eventually leak sewer gases and water into your floor. Summer: Managing High Usage Summer often means higher water usage from gardening, filling pools, and extra showers. Inspect washing machine hoses: Turn off the water supply and check the rubber or stainless steel hoses for bulging, cracking, or fraying. A burst washing machine hose is a leading cause of indoor flooding. Check for slow drains in floor drains: Pour a gallon of water down basement floor drains. If it drains slowly or the water level rises, you may have a partial blockage in your main sewer line. Examine sprinkler systems: Run your lawn irrigation system and watch the heads. If water is bubbling up from the ground rather than spraying from the heads, you have a broken underground pipe or fitting.
