1. Pronunciation and Basic Greetings

Why German Is Easier Than It Looks You step into a bakery in Berlin. The display is full of pretzels, brötchen, and layered cakes. You want to order, but the words on the signs look like a jumble of impossibly long consonants. You take a deep breath, point, and try your best. The baker smiles, hands you your pastry, and says something that sounds incredibly warm and welcoming. You have just survived your first German interaction. For many travelers, German looks intimidating on paper. Words like Streichholzschächtelchen (little matchbox) seem to require superhuman tongue gymnastics. But here is the secret: German is phonetically consistent. Unlike English, where "read" and "read" can be pronounced two entirely different ways depending on the context, German letters almost always make the exact same sound. Once you learn the rules of the alphabet, you can look at almost any German word and pronounce it correctly on your first try. This chapter gives you the foundational building blocks of the language. We will start with the alphabet and the special sounds created by umlauts, move into the most useful greetings for different times of day, and finish with how to introduce yourself. By the end, you will be equipped to walk into that Berlin bakery and greet the baker with confidence. The German Alphabet and Vowels The German alphabet shares the same 26 letters as the English alphabet, but four of those letters are pronounced quite differently. Additionally, German includes four special characters (ä, ö, ü, and ß) that we will cover shortly. Let us start with the vowels. In English, vowels are notoriously unpredictable. In German, they are strict and consistent. The most important rule to remember is that German vowels are generally "pure." You do not glide from one sound to another within a single vowel like you do in English. Short vs. Long Vowels German vowels are pronounced either short or long. While there are specific spelling rules that dictate whether a vowel is short or long, for a traveler, the best approach is to listen and mimic. However, knowing the difference between the two sounds is vital. Short vowels: These are crisp and clipped. The vowel sound is cut off quickly. Example: The a in Mann (man) sounds like the "a" in the English word "father," but much shorter. Example: The e in Bett (bed) sounds like the "e" in the English word "bed." Example: The i in mit (with) sounds like the "i" in the English word "sit." Example: The o in Sonne (sun) sounds like the "o" in the English word "hot." 2. Polite Phrases and Simple Questions

The Magic Words: Please, Thank You, and Excuse Me You are standing in a busy Berlin train station. You accidentally bump into a stranger, and you need to get their attention to ask for the time. In English, you would instinctively say "excuse me" and "thank you." These words act as social lubricant, smoothing over awkward interactions and showing respect. In German, courtesy is highly valued, and having a handful of polite phrases ready will completely change the way locals interact with you. If you show you are making an effort to be polite, Germans will almost always warm up to you and go out of their way to help. Please and Thank You The most essential polite phrase in German is bitte. It is incredibly versatile and translates to "please," "you're welcome," "here you go," and even "pardon?" Because bitte does so much heavy lifting, Germans often pair it with other words to be more specific: Bitte – Please. Danke – Thank you. Vielen Dank – Many thanks / Thank you very much. Bitte schön – You're very welcome (literally: "please beautiful"). You will also hear this used when handing someone an item, meaning "here you go." Danke schön – Thank you very much (literally: "thank you beautiful"). If someone says Danke schön to you, the natural response is Bitte schön. It is a perfectly balanced exchange. Excuse Me When you need to navigate through a crowd, get a server's attention at a restaurant, or apologize for a minor mistake, you need the phrase for "excuse me." German gives you two main options depending on exactly what you mean: 1. Entschuldigung (en-SHOOL-di-goong): This is the most common, all-purpose "excuse me." Use it when bumping into someone, when trying to pass someone on a narrow sidewalk, or as a direct replacement for "sorry." 2. Verzeihung (fer-TSY-oong): This is a slightly shorter, highly practical alternative. It is often shouted out in crowded places when you are trying to weave through a crowd ("Excuse me, coming through!"). Pronunciation note: Remember from Chapter 1 that the German v is usually pronounced like the English f. Therefore, Verzeihung starts with an "f" sound. Also, the ie in Verzeihung? Wait—that's an ei! Recall that ei is pronounced like the English word "eye." So, Verzeihung sounds like "fer-TSY-oong." Now, we must look at the actual words you use to address people. English has a major convenience: the word "you" works for everyone. 3. Numbers, Money, and Paying

Counting from 1 to 12: The Foundation You are standing in a bustling bakery in Berlin. The display is filled with pretzels, pastries, and loaves of bread. You point to a Schoko-Croissant and hold up two fingers. The baker nods and says, "Das macht vier Euro." To survive and thrive in German-speaking countries, you need to understand numbers. Whether you are buying a snack, paying for a hotel, or figuring out if you got the right change, numbers are the backbone of travel transactions. Let’s start at the very beginning. In German, counting from 1 to 12 is highly logical, but the pronunciation requires a little attention. Here are your first numbers: 1. eins (sounds like "eyens") 2. zwei (sounds like "tsvy") - Remember from Chapter 1 that the German z is pronounced like a sharp English "ts". 3. drei (sounds like "dry") 4. vier (sounds like "feer" - the v in German is usually pronounced like the English "f") 5. fünf (sounds like "fuhnf") 6. sechs (sounds like "zeks") 7. sieben (sounds like "zee-ben") 8. acht (sounds like "ahkt" - the ch here is a harsh, throaty sound, as introduced in Chapter 1) 9. neun (sounds like "noyn") 10. zehn (sounds like "tsayn") 11. elf (sounds like "elf") 12. zwölf (sounds like "tsvelf" - using the ö umlaut sound from Chapter 1) Take a moment to practice these. They are the building blocks for every other number you will learn. The Teens: 13 to 19 Once you hit thirteen, German introduces a pattern that you will see repeated throughout the number system. Instead of saying "thirteen" (three-ten), German says "drei-zehn" (three-ten). To form the teens, you take the numbers 3 through 9 and add -zehn to the end of them. 13: dreizehn 14: vierzehn 15: fünfzehn 16: sechzehn (Notice that the "s" in sechs drops off, making it sech-zehn, not sechs-zehn) 17: siebzehn (Similarly, "sieben" shortens to "sieb-zehn") 18: achtzehn 19: neunzehn The Tens: 20 to 100 Now let’s jump to the tens. Just like the teens, the tens follow a predictable pattern based on the numbers you already know. 20: zwanzig 30: dreißig (Notice the special German letter ß, or "Eszett". It is simply a double "s" sound.) 40: vierzig 50: fünfzig 60: sechzig (Again, the "s" drops from "sechs") 70: siebzig (Again, "sieben" shortens to "sieb-") 80: achtzig 90: neunzig 100: (ein)hundert The Big Reversal: Numbers 21 to 99 Here is where German throws English speakers a curveball. In English, we say "twenty-one" (tens first, then units). In German, you flip it. You say the unit first, followed by the word und (and), followed by the ten. 4. Navigation and Public Transportation

Finding Your Way: Asking for Key Locations You step off the plane or train in Germany, drop your bags at your hotel, and are ready to explore. But first, you need to find the train station, locate a bathroom, and figure out how to get to your next destination. When asking where something is in German, the most important phrase you need is: Wo ist...? (Where is...?) To ask for a specific location, simply add the word for the place to the end of the question. Here are the essential locations you will need to find: der Bahnhof (the train station) - Pronounced: bahn-hohf der Busbahnhof (the bus station) - Pronounced: boos-bahn-hohf die Toilette (the bathroom / toilet) - Pronounced: twa-let-uh. Note: In German, it is perfectly polite to ask for the "Toilette." Do not look for a "bathroom" (Badezimmer), as Germans reserve that word for the room in a house where you take a shower. der Ausgang (the exit) - Pronounced: ows-gahng der Eingang (the entrance) - Pronounced: yn-gahng die Apotheke (the pharmacy) - Pronounced: ah-po-teh-kuh das Hotel (the hotel) - Pronounced: ho-tel Putting It Together If you want to be polite—which, as we learned in Module 2, goes a long way in Germany—you can combine your greeting with your question. Guten Tag. Wo ist der Bahnhof? (Hello. Where is the train station?) Entschuldigung, wo ist die Toilette? (Excuse me, where is the bathroom?) When Germans give you directions, they will use a few key prepositions. You don’t need to memorize every word they say, but listening for these specific keywords will tell you which way to go: links (left) rechts (right) geradeaus (straight ahead) hier (here) dort (there) If someone speaks too fast or gives you a complex set of directions, you can simply smile, say Danke! (Thank you!), and look for the signs. Fortunately, German public transportation signs are highly standardized and use clear international symbols. Buying a Ticket for Trains and Buses Germany has an incredibly robust public transportation network. Whether you are taking a massive intercity train or a local city bus, the process of buying a ticket is quite similar. First, let’s look at the types of tickets you might need to ask for. die Fahrkarte (the ticket) - Pronounced: fahr-kar-tuh einfach (one-way / single) - Pronounced: yn-fakh hin und zurück (round-trip / there and back) - Pronounced: hin oont tsoo-rook der Erwachsene (the adult) - Pronounced: air-vahk-sen-uh das Kind (the child) - Pronounced: kint At the Ticket Machine Most ticket machines in Germany have an English language option (usually marked with a British or American flag, or the word "EN"). 5. Accommodation and Checking In

Arriving at Your Accommodation You’ve navigated the train station, mastered the U-Bahn, and finally found your street. Now you are standing in the lobby of your hotel, a cozy pension, or at the front door of your Airbnb. The front desk clerk greets you. What happens next? Checking into a German-speaking accommodation is usually a straightforward transaction, but it relies on a specific set of vocabulary. The good news is that you already have the foundation. You know how to offer a polite Guten Tag (Good day) and you know your numbers and how to pay. Now, we just need to layer in the words for rooms, keys, and amenities. Let’s start with the essential nouns you will encounter the moment you approach the desk. Types of Accommodation Depending on your travel style, you might be staying in any of the following: das Hotel: The standard hotel. die Jugendherberge: The hostel. Literally translating to "youth hostel," these are budget-friendly and common across Germany. das Gästehaus / die Pension: A guesthouse. These are often family-run, smaller than hotels, and offer a more personal touch. die Ferienwohnung: A vacation rental or apartment. You will use this term when communicating with an Airbnb host. Jargon note: In German, all nouns are capitalized. You might also notice the words der (masculine), die (feminine), and das (neuter) in front of nouns. These are definite articles (the). While memorizing the gender of every word isn't necessary for a quick trip, being aware of them helps you recognize patterns when you see them written down. The Check-In Conversation When you approach the desk, the clerk will likely ask if they can help you or if you have a reservation. Ich habe eine Reservierung. (I have a reservation.) Pronunciation tip: Ich is pronounced with a soft "ch" sound—like the "h" in "huge" combined with a slight hiss. They will usually ask for your name: Auf welchen Namen? (Under what name?) You can respond with: Auf den Namen [Your Last Name]. (Under the name [Your Last Name].) Next, they will need your passport. In Germany, it is a legal requirement for accommodations to register foreign guests. The clerk might say: Ihren Pass, bitte. (Your passport, please.) After processing your registration, they will hand you your key. Hier ist Ihr Schlüssel. (Here is your key.) Note: The ß in Schlüssel is an "ess-tset." It is simply pronounced like a sharp "s." Finally, they will tell you your room number. Because you already studied numbers in Module 3, you will catch this easily! They might say: Sie haben Zimmer eins-null-zwei. (You have room 102.) 6. Dining Out and Ordering Food

Finding Your Table Picture this: You've spent the afternoon exploring a new city, your feet are tired, and the smell of fresh bread and roasting meat drifts from a cozy corner restaurant. You step inside, but suddenly you realize—you don't know how to ask for a table. In Germany, the restaurant experience operates on a few unspoken rules that differ slightly from the English-speaking world. The most important difference? You usually don't wait to be seated. Instead, you find your own table and sit down. If the restaurant is busy or you want to be polite, you should approach the host or a waiter and ask for a table for your group. To do this, you only need two ingredients: the phrase for "a table for" and the numbers you already learned in Chapter 3. Einen Tisch für (Eye-nen tish fewr) = A table for... Personen (per-ZOH-nen) = people Put them together with a number: Einen Tisch für zwei Personen, bitte. (A table for two people, please.) Einen Tisch für vier Personen, bitte. (A table for four people, please.) The Outdoor Seating Question In good weather, outdoor seating (often called a Biergarten or Terrasse) is highly sought after. If you see empty tables outside but want to make sure you are allowed to sit there, you can ask: Drinnen oder draußen? (DRIN-nen OH-der DROW-sen) = Inside or outside? Können wir draußen sitzen? (KER-nen veer DROW-sen ZIT-sen) = Can we sit outside? Once you are seated, the waiter will eventually bring you a menu. In German, the menu is called the Speisekarte (SHY-zeh-kar-teh), which literally translates to "dish card." Decoding the Speisekarte Looking at a German menu for the first time can feel overwhelming, but most menus follow a logical structure. You will typically see the meal categories broken down into sections. Here are the most common headers you will encounter: Vorspeisen (FOR-shpy-zen): Appetizers / Starters Hauptgerichte (HOWPT-geh-rikh-teh): Main dishes Beilagen (BY-lah-gen): Side dishes (like fries or salads) Getränke (geh-TREN-keh): Beverages / Drinks Desserts (deh-ZERT): Desserts (often just the French/English word is used) Common Menu Words You don't need to know the name of every dish, but recognizing a few key preparation words will help you navigate safely: Vegetarisch (vey-tay-AH-rish): Vegetarian Vegan (VAY-gan): Vegan Scharf (sharf): Spicy Gegrillt (geh-GRILT): Grilled Gebraten (geh-BRAH-ten): Roasted or fried Mit (mit): With Ohne (OH-neh): Without If you aren't sure what a dish is, you can simply point to the menu and ask the waiter: Was ist das? (Vas ist das?) = What is that? Ordering Like a Local When the waiter approaches your table, they will usually greet you. 7. Shopping and Buying Souvenirs

Finding Your Way Around a German Store You step into a boutique in Munich to buy a warm sweater, but the racks are dense, and you cannot find the checkout. A shop assistant approaches and asks if you need help. In English, you might say, "I'm just looking for a wool sweater in black." In German, you need a few core phrases to explain what you want and to ask where things are. When you walk into a smaller shop in Germany, the shop assistant will often say: Kann ich Ihnen helfen? – Can I help you? (Formal) Suchen Sie etwas Bestimmtes? – Are you looking for something specific? A polite, standard response, even if you are just browsing, is: Ich schaue nur. – I’m just looking. Ich brauche... – I need... To ask for help finding a specific product, you can say: Wo finde ich...? – Where do I find...? Haben Sie...? – Do you have...? Let’s look at a concrete scenario. You want to buy a souvenir mug for a friend. You: Entschuldigung, ich suche eine Tasse. (Excuse me, I'm looking for a mug.) Assistant: Wir haben Tassen in der zweiten Reihe. (We have mugs in the second aisle/row.) You: Danke. Haben Sie auch T-Shirts? (Thank you. Do you also have T-shirts?) Assistant: Ja, im Erdgeschoss. (Yes, on the ground floor.) A quick note on store layout: In Germany, the Erdgeschoss is the ground floor (what Americans call the first floor). If the assistant sends you to the erste Etage (first floor), you will need to go up one flight of stairs. Types of Stores Depending on what you are shopping for, you might visit different types of stores. Knowing the word on the sign will save you time: Das Kaufhaus – Department store (like Galeria Kaufhof or Karstadt) Die Boutique – Boutique (for clothing) Das Geschäft – Store/shop (a general term) Der Souvenirladen – Souvenir shop Der Supermarkt – Supermarket (for groceries and everyday items) Die Bäckerei – Bakery Der Markt – Market (often outdoor, like a weekly farmers' market) Shopping for Clothes: Sizes and Colors If you are shopping for clothes (Kleidung), you will need to ask for different sizes and colors. Asking for Sizes The German word for size is die Größe. To ask for a specific size, you say: Ich brauche das in Größe... – I need this in size... Haben Sie das in einer anderen Größe? – Do you have this in another size? When stating sizes, it helps to know the basic clothing size systems. Germany uses the European (EU) sizing system. 8. Sightseeing and Telling Time

Asking About Opening Hours You are standing in front of the Brandenburg Gate in Berlin, or perhaps gazing up at the Kölner Dom (Cologne Cathedral). You check your watch and realize it is getting late, and you want to visit the nearby museum before you head back to your hotel. But when does it close? In Chapter 3, you learned how to count and use numbers for paying. In Chapter 2, you learned how to ask basic questions. Now, we are going to combine those skills so you can plan your sightseeing itinerary effectively. To ask when a place opens, you use the phrase: Wann öffnet...? (When does ... open?) To ask when a place closes, you use: Wann schließt...? (When does ... close?) If you want to ask about a specific museum or church, you simply put the name of the place at the end of the sentence. Wann öffnet das Museum? (When does the museum open?) Wann schließt die Kirche? (When does the church close?) The Magic Word: "Geöffnet" When you look at a sign on a door or ask a staff member about hours, you will frequently hear or see the word geöffnet. This is the German equivalent of "open" in the context of business hours. A very common phrase you will see on signs is: Wir haben geöffnet von... bis... (We are open from... to...) Since you already know your numbers from Chapter 3, you just need to slot them into this phrase. Wir haben geöffnet von neun bis fünf. (We are open from nine to five.) Wir haben geöffnet von zehn bis achtzehn. (We are open from ten to eighteen. Note: Germans frequently use the 24-hour clock, which you will learn more about shortly.) If you want to ask a staff member directly, "Are you open today?", you can say: Sind Sie heute geöffnet? Telling Time on the Hour and Half-Hour To understand opening hours and tour schedules, you need to know how to tell time in German. Let's start with the basics: the hour and the half-hour. Asking for the Time To ask a stranger for the time, you use a slightly longer, polite phrase: Wie spät ist es? (How late is it? / What time is it?) On the Hour When the time is exactly on the hour (e.g., 3:00), you use the word Uhr (clock/o'clock). You say the number, followed by Uhr. 8:00 = acht Uhr 12:00 = zwölf Uhr 14:00 = vierzehn Uhr The Half-Hour This is where German differs slightly from English. 9. Emergencies and Health

The Most Important Number: 112 You’re walking through a train station in Munich. Suddenly, the person next to you collapses. People around you start to react, but everyone is speaking rapid German. You need to get help immediately. In a medical emergency or accident, you don't need to build complex sentences. You need three things: your phone, the right number, and a few critical words. In Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, the European-wide emergency number is 112. Dialing 112 connects you to emergency services. It is free, works from any phone (even without a SIM card), and is available 24/7. The dispatcher who answers will speak German, but they are trained to handle calls in English as well. If you are in a non-medical emergency and need the police directly, you can dial 110. However, if you are unsure which one you need, dial 112. The dispatchers can route your call to the police if necessary. What to Say on the Phone When the operator answers, they will say the equivalent of "Emergency services, what is your emergency?" You need to state what happened and where you are. Here are the most critical emergency words. Notice that all German nouns are capitalized, a rule you’ve seen throughout this book. Hilfe! (Help!) Notfall (Emergency) Arzt (Doctor - Note: The 'r' is barely pronounced, making it sound almost like "Ahtst".) Krankenhaus (Hospital) Polizei (Police) Feuer (Fire) To report an accident, you can say: Ein Unfall! (An accident!) Ich brauche einen Arzt. (I need a doctor.) Remember the numbers and navigation vocabulary from Navigation and Public Transportation. The operator will need to know your location. Use the phrase: Ich bin in... (I am in/at...) followed by your location. For example: Ich bin in der Berliner Straße. (I am on Berliner Street.) Asking for English in an Urgent Situation In a high-stress situation, you may not have the bandwidth to speak German. As mentioned, 112 operators generally speak English. But if you are dealing with a bystander, a security guard, or a receptionist, you need to ask them to switch languages. Pulling from the polite phrases you learned in Polite Phrases and Simple Questions, you can ask: Sprechen Sie Englisch? (Do you speak English?) Bitte rufen Sie 112 an. (Please call 112. - Literally: Please call 112 up.) Können Sie mir helfen? (Can you help me?) Ich brauche einen Arzt, der Englisch spricht. (I need a doctor who speaks English.)
