1. Getting Started with French: The Basics

Spoken by over 300 million people on five continents, it’s an official language in 29 countries and the European Union. From the markets of Dakar to the cafés of Montreal, from the diplomatic halls of the United Nations to the classrooms of Vietnam, French connects cultures across continents. Or maybe you’re simply drawn to the beauty of the language—the rolling rs, the soft u, the way "amour" feels like a sigh. Whatever your reason, today marks the first step of a journey. French is also a working language in international organizations such as the United Nations, NATO, UNESCO, and the International Red Cross. That means learning French could open professional doors you haven’t even considered yet. And here’s something surprising: French is the only language taught in every education system worldwide. That makes it one of the most accessible second languages in the world. 2. French Sounds and Pronunciation Mastery

Why French Sounds Are Your Secret Weapon Imagine you’re in a bustling café in Paris, ordering a coffee. You say "Bonjour, je voudrais un café, s’il vous plaît"—and the barista responds with a warm smile and hands you exactly what you ordered. Now imagine the same scenario, but instead of "café", you accidentally pronounced it "cafay" or "ka-feh". The barista hesitates, frowns slightly, and says "Désolé, on n’a pas ça." You’ve just experienced the power—and the pitfalls—of French pronunciation. French isn’t just a language you learn; it’s a symphony of sounds where vowels hum, consonants whisper, and silence plays a starring role. Mispronounce a single sound, and you might find yourself asking for "un pain" (a loaf of bread) when you meant "un pin" (a pine tree). Or worse—you might accidentally call someone "un petit con" (a little jerk) instead of "un petit coin" (a little corner). This chapter isn’t just about memorizing sounds—it’s about rewiring your mouth, ears, and brain to think, hear, and speak like a French speaker. We’ll start with the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), which is like a musical score for French. Then we’ll break down the mouth mechanics behind the most distinctive sounds: nasal vowels, the elusive mute e, and the rhythm of liaisons. Finally, you’ll get tools to train your ear and voice so you can sound not just correct, but natural. Ready to make your French sound like it belongs in a Parisian alleyway? Let’s begin. --- The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA): Your New Best Friend You wouldn’t try to learn music by guessing the notes—you’d use sheet music. Similarly, French pronunciation is best learned with IPA, a standardized system that represents sounds unambiguously. Think of it as a map where each symbol is a sound, not a letter. This is crucial because French spelling is famously inconsistent: "oiseau" (bird) is pronounced /wɑ.zo/, not "oh-ee-zo-ah" as the spelling might suggest. Why IPA Matters for Beginners - Eliminates guesswork: No more confusion between "eau" (/o/) and "eu" (/ø/). - Builds listening skills: You’ll recognize sounds in songs, movies, and conversations. - Improves self-correction: Record yourself and compare your IPA to native speakers’. 3. Essential French Grammar: Nouns and Articles

Understanding Nouns in French A noun is a word that names a person, place, thing, or idea. In French, every noun has two fundamental properties you must learn from the start: grammatical gender and number. Unlike English, where most nouns don’t change based on gender, nearly all French nouns are either masculine or feminine. This affects not only the noun itself but also the words that accompany it—especially the small words called articles (like the or a in English). Imagine you’re in a Parisian café. You point to a cup of coffee: you say un café (a coffee, masculine), but you ask for la crème (the cream, feminine). The gender of these nouns isn’t based on logic—it’s a rule you’ll learn over time. Your ear will gradually recognize patterns, just as it does in English with words like “book” (neuter) and “table” (feminine in French: la table). Let’s begin by understanding how gender works and how it shapes the way we talk about everyday things. --- Why Gender Matters in French In English, gender in nouns is rare. We say the actor and the actress—but the word “actor” doesn’t change gender, only the role does. In French, however, every noun has a fixed gender. There’s no “neutral” option like in German (das), and no word changes form based on gender like “actor” vs. “actress.” This means: - You must memorize the gender of each noun as you learn it. - The gender affects the article (like le or la) and sometimes the adjective that describes it. For example: - un livre (a book, masculine) → le livre (the book) - une table (a table, feminine) → la table (the table) 💡 Tip: Think of gender not as “male” or “female,” but as a grammatical label—like labeling a noun “M” or “F” in your mind. --- Masculine and Feminine Nouns: The Basics Most nouns ending in -e are feminine: - la table (the table) - la chaise (the chair) - la maison (the house) But not all nouns ending in -e are feminine—le café (the coffee) is masculine. Many nouns ending in -ment are masculine: - leiment (the moment) - le gouvernement (the government) Many nouns ending in -tion, -sion, or -té are feminine: - la nation (the nation) - la décision (the decision) - la beauté (the beauty) But again, there are exceptions: - le côté (the side) – masculine - un été (a summer) – masculine ⚠️ There is no reliable 100% rule. You’ll need to learn the gender with each noun. Common Masculine Nouns (to start with): - le garçon (the boy) - l’hôtel (the hotel) - le livre (the book) - le stylo (the pen) - le …

4. French Verbs: Present Tense Basics

What Verbs Do (And Why They’re Not Scary) Imagine you’re in a café in Paris. You’ve just sat down, and the waiter asks: — Qu’est-ce que vous voulez commander ? (What would you like to order?) You pause. Your brain scrambles for the right words. Do you say: - “Je mange un croissant.” (I am eating a croissant.) - “Je mange un café.” (I am eating a coffee.) - “Je bois un café.” (I am drinking a coffee.) Only one makes sense. But how do you know that? The answer lies in the verb: manger (to eat), boire (to drink), and the correct conjugation that matches je (I). Verbs are the engine of every sentence. Without them, you’re just pointing at things and making noises. With them, you can say: - Je parle français. (I speak French.) - Tu habites où ? (Where do you live?) - Il travaille ici. (He works here.) This chapter is about giving that engine a tune-up. You’ll learn how French verbs change shape depending on who’s doing the action, and how to use them in real moments—like ordering a coffee, introducing yourself, or talking about your day. --- How French Verbs Work in the Present Tense A verb is a word that describes an action, a state, or an occurrence. In English, we say: - I eat, you eat, he eats. French does the same thing—but the verb changes form depending on the subject. This change is called conjugation. The Three Verb Families Most French verbs fall into one of three groups based on their endings in the infinitive (the “to” form): 1. -er verbs → parler (to speak), aimer (to like/love) 2. -ir verbs → finir (to finish), choisir (to choose) 3. -re verbs → vendre (to sell), attendre (to wait) Each group follows its own pattern when conjugated. Once you learn the pattern, you can apply it to dozens of common verbs. --- Conjugating Regular -er Verbs Let’s start with -er verbs, the most common group. The infinitive ends in -er. 6. French Sentence Structure: The Basics of Word Order

Why Word Order Matters in French Imagine you’re standing at a café in Paris, trying to order a coffee. You point at the menu and say, "Je café bois." The waiter looks confused. Now try: "Je bois un café." Suddenly, everything makes sense. The difference? Word order. French sentence structure follows specific rules to make meaning clear. Unlike English, where word order is flexible, French relies on a consistent pattern to avoid misunderstandings. This chapter will break down that pattern step by step, so you can build sentences that sound natural and correct. --- The Foundation: Subject + Verb + Object (SVO) Every French sentence, no matter how simple or complex, follows a core structure called Subject-Verb-Object (SVO). This means: 1. Subject (S): Who or what is doing the action (often a noun or pronoun). 2. Verb (V): The action being performed. 3. Object (O): What the action is done to (if anything). Let’s look at examples using verbs you’ve already learned, like manger (to eat) and boire (to drink): | Subject (S) | Verb (V) | Object (O) | English Translation | |-------------|----------|------------|---------------------| | Je | mange | une pomme | I eat an apple | | Tu | bois | du café | You drink coffee | | Il | regarde | la télé | He watches TV | Key Point: In French, the verb always comes right after the subject in a basic sentence. No exceptions. --- Building Affirmative Sentences Affirmative sentences are straightforward—they state something positively. Start with the subject, follow with the verb, and end with the object (if there is one). Step-by-Step Formation 1. Identify the subject: Who is doing the action? - Je (I) - Tu (you, informal) - Il/Elle/On (he/she/one) - Nous (we) - Vous (you, formal/plural) - Ils/Elles (they) 2. Choose the correct verb form: Verbs change based on the subject (this is called conjugation, which you learned in French Verbs: Present Tense Basics). 3. Add the object: What is being acted upon? Remember to use the correct article (like un, une, le, la) before the object. Examples with Common Verbs - Parler (to speak): - Je parle français. (I speak French.) - Tu parles anglais. (You speak English.) - Nous parlons espagnol. (We speak Spanish.) - Aimer (to like): - J’aime le chocolat. (I like chocolate.) - Elle aime la musique. (She likes music.) - Travailler (to work): - Vous travaillez beaucoup. (You work a lot.) - Ils travaillent ici. (They work here.) Tip: Notice how Je becomes J’ before a verb starting with a vowel or silent h (like aime or habite). This is called elision—a small spelling change to make pronunciation smoother. --- Forming Negative Sentences To make …

8. French Adjectives and Agreement Rules

A Bonjour Moment in Paris You’ve just stepped out of the hôtel you booked on Duolingo’s travel forum and are heading toward the rue where your French‑language meetup will start. The sky is bleu, the cafés are petits, and the people you see are a mix of élégants tourists and décontractés locals. As you walk, you can’t help but notice how every detail seems to have a little word attached to it—grande maison, vieille ville, délicieux croissant. Those little words are adjectives, and they do more than just sound pretty; they tell you what something is, how it looks, and even how you feel about it. Understanding how adjectives work—and how they must match the nouns they describe—is the next essential step after mastering nouns, articles, and present‑tense verbs. Let’s dive in. --- 1. The Role of Adjectives in French Sentences - Definition – An adjective is a word that modifies (describes) a noun or pronoun, adding information such as size, color, shape, emotion, or quantity. - Why they matter – In French, adjectives are the primary tool for painting a picture with words. They let you say une grande maison (a big house) instead of just une maison (a house). - Interaction with other parts of speech – Adjectives follow the noun they modify unless special placement rules apply (see §2). They must agree in gender (masculine/feminine) and number (singular/plural) with that noun—think of it as “matching the outfit” of the noun. When combined with a verb, adjectives usually appear after the verb phrase (e.g., Le garçon est intelligent). Because you already know how to identify nouns and articles (Chapter 3) and how to conjugate être (Chapter 4), you can now place adjectives correctly in simple sentences. --- 2. 10. French Negation and Common Negatives

A Moment in a Paris Café You’ve just ordered a croissant and a café au lait. The waiter smiles and asks, « Vous ne voulez pas de sucre ? » (You don’t want any sugar?). You pause. In French, saying “I don’t want sugar” isn’t just “Je veux pas de sucre.” The negation wraps around the verb: ne … pas. Mastering this little “sandwich” is the gateway to sounding natural and avoiding misunderstandings—especially when you need to say what you don’t do, never do, or nothing at all. --- 1. The Negation Sandwich: ne … pas 1.1 How the basic pattern works In French, the standard way to make a sentence negative is to place ne before the verb and pas after it: | English | French (affirmative) | French (negative) | |---------|----------------------|-------------------| | I speak French. | Je parle français. | Je ne parle pas français. | | She likes music. | Elle aime la musique. | Elle n’aime pas la musique. | | They live in Paris. | Ils habitent à Paris. | Ils n’habitent pas à Paris. | Note: When the verb begins with a vowel or a silent h, ne contracts to n’ (e.g., Je n’ai pas). This contraction was introduced in Chapter 4 (Present Tense Basics) when we discussed avoir. 1.2 Why the “ne” sometimes disappears in spoken French Native speakers often drop the ne, especially in informal conversation: Je parle pas français. While you’ll understand it, writing the full ne … pas is required in formal contexts (texts, exams, emails). For beginners, keep both parts; the habit of omitting ne will come naturally later. 1.3 Position of pas with compound tenses When a verb is compounded (e.g., passé composé), pas still follows the auxiliary verb, not the past participle: Affirmative: J’ai mangé (I ate) Negative: Je n’ai pas mangé (I did not eat) The same rule applies to être as an auxiliary: Elle est partie → Elle n’est pas partie. --- 2.
