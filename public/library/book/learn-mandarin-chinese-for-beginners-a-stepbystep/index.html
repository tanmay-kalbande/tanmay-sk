1. The Pinyin System and Tones

Why Mandarin Needs a Sound System Imagine walking into a bakery in Beijing. You want to buy ten buns. You practice the phrase in your head: shí ge bāozi. But when you say it, the baker looks confused, then offended, and finally hands you a wet towel instead of food. What happened? You likely used the wrong tone. In English, changing the pitch of your voice—going up at the end of a sentence—turns a statement into a question. In Mandarin, changing the pitch of your voice changes the actual meaning of the word. Because Mandarin uses a writing system based on characters rather than an alphabet, learners and typists need a way to know how those characters sound. That is where Pinyin comes in. Pinyin (pronounced pin-yin) is the official system used to transliterate Mandarin Chinese sounds into the Roman alphabet (the same letters you are reading right now). The word pinyin literally translates to "spell sound." Before we learn how to read Chinese characters, we must learn how to speak. Pinyin is our bridge. It tells us exactly which consonants, vowels, and tones to use. By mastering Pinyin, you build a solid foundation for speaking and listening. The Anatomy of a Pinyin Syllable Every syllable in Mandarin is made up of three parts: 1. An Initial: The starting consonant sound. 2. A Final: The vowel sound (sometimes ending in a consonant like n or ng). 3. A Tone: The pitch contour applied to the syllable. For example, in the syllable mā (mother), m is the initial, a is the final, and the flat line over the a represents the tone. Let’s break down the initials and finals. The Initials (Consonants) Many Pinyin initials look and sound exactly like they do in English. However, a few are "tricksters"—they look like English letters but sound completely different. The Familiar Initials These sound very similar to their English counterparts: - b, p, m, f (pronounced like the English b, p, m, f) - d, t, n, l (pronounced like the English d, t, n, l) - g, k, h (pronounced like the English g, k, h) The Trickster Initials Pay close attention to these, as getting them wrong is a common beginner pitfall: - c: Sounds like the "ts" in "cats." (Not a hard "k" sound). - z: Sounds like the "ds" in "pads." - zh: Sounds like the "j" in "jungle," but with your tongue curled back. - ch: Sounds like the "ch" in "church," but with your tongue curled back. - sh: Sounds like the "sh" in "ship," but with your tongue curled back. - r: Sounds like the "s" in "measure" or the "r" in "ray" with your tongue …

2. Introduction to Chinese Characters

From Sound to Symbol: The Building Blocks of Chinese In Chapter 1, we mastered the Pinyin system, learning how to produce the sounds of Mandarin using the Roman alphabet. We broke down syllables into an Initial, a Final, and a Tone, and explored how pitch contours change the meaning of a word entirely. But here is the catch: Pinyin is just a bridge. It is a pronunciation guide designed to help you speak. When you walk into a restaurant in Beijing, look at a street sign in Shanghai, or read a menu in Taipei, you will not see Pinyin. You will see Chinese characters. Unlike the English alphabet, where individual letters represent sounds (phonemes), Chinese characters represent meaning and sound simultaneously. A character is a self-contained unit, built from specific physical components. To read and write Mandarin, you must learn this entirely different writing system. Let’s break down how these characters are constructed, starting from the very first pen stroke. The Anatomy of a Character: Basic Strokes If Pinyin is the audio of Mandarin, characters are the video. And just as moving pictures are made of pixels, Chinese characters are made of strokes. A stroke is a single, uninterrupted movement of the pen or brush. When you write in English, you might lift your pen multiple times to cross a "t" or dot an "i". In Chinese, every time your writing instrument leaves the paper, a stroke has ended. There are dozens of variations of strokes, but almost all of them are combinations of eight basic strokes. If you master these eight, you can write almost any character. Here are the fundamental strokes, along with their names and how to write them: 1. 横 (héng) - Horizontal Stroke: A simple line drawn from left to right. 2. 竖 (shù) - Vertical Stroke: A simple line drawn from top to bottom. 3. 撇 (piě) - Left-falling Stroke: A stroke that curves down and to the left. Think of a backward slash (\) that gently curves. 4. 捺 (nà) - Right-falling Stroke: A stroke that curves down and to the right. Think of a forward slash (/) that ends with a slight flair. 5. 点 (diǎn) - Dot Stroke: A tiny, short stroke, usually falling slightly to the right. It is rarely a perfect circle; it looks more like a tiny teardrop. 6. 提 (tí) - Rising Stroke: A short stroke that starts at the bottom left and rises to the upper right. 7. 折 (zhé) - Turning Stroke: This is a compound stroke. It involves a change of direction without lifting the pen. For example, moving horizontally to the right, then abruptly turning downward to make a vertical line. 8. 钩 …

3. Greetings and Basic Sentence Structure

The Universal Icebreaker: "Hello" Imagine you are stepping off a plane in Beijing, walking into a Taipei tea house, or meeting your new coworker in a Singapore office. Before you discuss business, ask for directions, or order a meal, you need to accomplish one simple human task: you need to say hello. In English, we have a multitude of greetings ranging from the formal "Good morning" to the casual "What's up?" Mandarin Chinese is no different. Let us start with the most universally recognized Chinese word on the planet. The Everyday "Hello" The standard, everyday way to say "hello" in Mandarin is: 你好 (nǐ hǎo) If you remember our work in Chapter 1, you know exactly how to read this Pinyin. Let's break it down: 你 (nǐ): This character means "you". It uses the initial n and the final i, spoken with the falling-rising 3rd tone. 好 (hǎo): This character means "good". It uses the initial h and the compound final ao, also spoken with the falling-rising 3rd tone. Literally translated, 你好 means "You good." In Chinese culture, wishing well-being upon the person you are addressing is the standard way to greet them. Tone Changes in Action: The "Two Thirds" Rule Look closely at the Pinyin for 你好: nǐ hǎo. Both characters are naturally in the 3rd tone. However, if you try to drop your voice and raise it for both syllables, you will find it physically awkward and unnatural to speak smoothly. Mandarin handles this with a rhythm rule. When a 3rd tone character is immediately followed by another 3rd tone character, the first one changes to a rising 2nd tone. So, while the Pinyin is written as nǐ hǎo (to preserve the original, underlying tones of the words), it is actually pronounced as ní hǎo. You do not need to memorize a new pitch contour; your brain already knows the 2nd tone from our practice in Chapter 1. Just remember: when two 3rd tones meet, the first one rises. Greetings Throughout the Day While 你好 is a fantastic catch-all that works any time of day, native speakers frequently use time-specific greetings. These greetings follow a highly predictable formula: Time word + 好 (hǎo) 早上好 (zǎo shang hǎo) — Good morning 早上 (zǎo shang): Early / morning. Note that shang uses the neutral tone (no tone mark), meaning it is pronounced lightly and softly, without a defined pitch contour. 下午好 (xià wǔ hǎo) — Good afternoon 下午 (xià wǔ): Afternoon. Both syllables use the falling 4th tone. 晚上好 (wǎn shang hǎo) — Good evening 晚上 (wǎn shang): Evening. Wǎn uses the falling-rising 3rd tone, followed again by a neutral shang. 4. Numbers, Money, and Dates

The Logic of Chinese Numbers You are standing in a bustling market in Beijing, pointing at a beautiful box of tea. The vendor looks at you, smiles, and says a string of sounds you don't quite understand. You hold up three fingers, hoping they mean thirty yuan, not three hundred. To navigate this moment—and to schedule your next visit to the market—you need the Chinese number system. The good news is that Chinese numbers operate on a beautifully strict, logical pattern. Once you learn a few basic building blocks, you don't need to memorize separate words for "seventeen," "fifty," or "ninety-nine." You simply assemble them like Lego bricks. Numbers Zero to Ten Let us start with the absolute foundations. Using the Pinyin system and tones we established in Chapter 1, practice saying these foundational numbers. 0: 零 (líng) - rising tone 1: 一 (yī) - flat tone 2: 二 (èr) - falling tone 3: 三 (sān) - flat tone 4: 四 (sì) - falling tone 5: 五 (wǔ) - dip/rise tone 6: 六 (liù) - falling tone 7: 七 (qī) - flat tone 8: 八 (bā) - flat tone 9: 九 (jiǔ) - dip/rise tone 10: 十 (shí) - flat tone Building Numbers Eleven to Ninety-Nine In English, we have unique words for eleven, twelve, thirteen, fifteen, and twenty. Chinese does away with all of that. To say eleven, you literally say "ten-one" (十一). To say twenty, you say "two-tens" (二十). Here is the simple formula for numbers between eleven and ninety-nine: Multiples of ten (20, 30, 40...): Say the first digit + 十 (shí). 20 = 二十 (èr shí) - literally "two tens" 50 = 五十 (wǔ shí) - literally "five tens" Teens (11-19): Say 十 (shí) + the second digit. 11 = 十一 (shí yī) - literally "ten one" 15 = 十五 (shí wǔ) - literally "ten five" All other numbers (21-99): Say the first digit + 十 (shí) + the second digit. 23 = 二十三 (èr shí sān) - literally "two tens three" 89 = 八十九 (bā shí jiǔ) - literally "eight tens nine" The Big Milestones: Hundreds and Thousands When we hit one hundred, we encounter our first major new vocabulary word: 百 (bǎi). 100: 一百 (yī bǎi) - literally "one hundred" 200: 两百 (liǎng bǎi) - Note: The number two (二, èr) changes to 两 (liǎng) when counting hundreds, thousands, or measuring objects. We will explore measure words in a later chapter, but for now, remember to use 两 when saying 200 or 2,000. 5. Time, Days, and Daily Routines

A Day in the Life: Telling Time in Mandarin Imagine you are meeting a new friend in Beijing. You agree to meet for coffee, and your friend asks when you are free. If you can’t tell time, you might end up waiting at the café all afternoon! In Chapter 4, you learned how to count, use money, and state dates using the numbers 1 through 99. Now, we will take those exact same numbers and apply them to the clock. Chinese time-telling is incredibly logical and relies entirely on the math you already know. There are no special words like "o'clock," "past," or "to" to memorize. Instead, you simply state the hour, followed by the minutes. By the end of this chapter, you will be able to read a clock, schedule your week, and describe your daily routine from the moment you wake up to the moment you go to sleep. Telling Time: Hours, Halves, and Minutes To tell time in Mandarin, you only need a few foundational vocabulary words and the numbers from Chapter 4. The Basics of the Clock Let's start with the word for "hour" or "o'clock." 点 (diǎn): hour / o'clock. (Pinyin note: The initial d and the compound final ian combine to make the sound "dyen." Remember to apply the falling-rising third tone to this syllable). To say what time it is right now, you use the sentence structure introduced in Chapter 3: Subject + 是 (to be) + Time. However, for telling time, it is very common and natural to drop the word "to be" entirely and simply state the time. 现在两点。 (Xiànzài liǎng diǎn.) - It is 2 o'clock now. (Note: We use 两 (liǎng) for the number 2 when telling time, rather than 二 (èr), just as we did when counting money in Chapter 4). Halves and Quarters When telling time, you will frequently need to express halves. 半 (bàn): half. To say "half past" an hour, you simply put "half" directly after the hour. 两点半 (liǎng diǎn bàn) - 2:30 (Two o'clock half) 三点半 (sān diǎn bàn) - 3:30 You will also hear the word 刻 (kè), which means "a quarter" (15 minutes). While less common in casual, everyday speech than simply saying "fifteen minutes," it is useful to recognize. 两点一刻 (liǎng diǎn yī kè) - 2:15 (Two o'clock one quarter) 两点三刻 (liǎng diǎn sān kè) - 2:45 (Two o'clock three quarters) Expressing Minutes To express exact minutes, you use the word for "minute." 分 (fēn): minute. The formula is always: [Hour] 点 [Minute] 分. 两点十分 (liǎng diǎn shí fēn) - 2:10 两点二十分 (liǎng diǎn èrshí fēn) - 2:20 If the minute is a single digit (like 2:05), you do not …

6. Family Members and Possession

The Uniquely Chinese Family Tree In English, we use words like "aunt," "uncle," and "cousin" as catch-all terms. If you say "my aunt is coming to visit," the listener usually has to ask follow-up questions to figure out exactly who this person is. Is she your mother’s sister? Your father’s brother’s wife? In Mandarin Chinese, the family tree is built into the vocabulary itself. There is no single word for "aunt." Instead, Mandarin uses highly specific terms that tell you the exact lineage: which side of the family they belong to, whether they are older or younger than your parent, and whether they are related by blood or by marriage. While this means you have more vocabulary to learn, it also means you can convey incredibly precise information in a single word. Before we dive into the specific terms, we first need to learn how to say "my," "your," and "his," because in Chinese, you rarely talk about family members without stating exactly whose family they belong to. Personal Pronouns In Chapter 3 (Greetings and Basic Sentence Structure), you learned the foundational pronoun for "I" or "me": wǒ (我). To talk about family, we need to expand this set of pronouns to include "you," "he," "she," and the plural forms ("we," "they"). Here is the complete list of personal pronouns in Mandarin: wǒ (我) = I / me nǐ (你) = you tā (他) = he / him tā (她) = she / her wǒmen (我们) = we / us nǐmen (你们) = you (plural) tāmen (他们) = they / them (male or mixed group) tāmen (她们) = they / them (exclusively female) The Plural Suffix: men (们) Notice the pattern? To make a personal pronoun plural, you simply add the suffix men (们) to the end of the singular pronoun. wǒ (I) + men = wǒmen (we) nǐ (you) + men = nǐmen (you all) A Note on Spoken Chinese If you look closely at the pinyin for "he" and "she," you will notice they are both pronounced tā. In spoken Mandarin, there is absolutely no difference in sound between he, she, and it (它). The distinction only exists in the written characters. 他 (he) uses the "person" radical (亻) 她 (she) uses the "woman" radical (女) 它 (it) uses the "roof" radical (宀) When you listen to a native speaker, you will have to rely entirely on context to know whether they are talking about a man or a woman. 7. Hobbies, Likes, and Dislikes

The Verb "To Like": Expressing Preferences Imagine a colleague asks you about your weekend plans, or a new friend wants to know what you do for fun. To navigate these conversations, you need the ability to express your personal tastes. In Mandarin Chinese, the cornerstone of expressing preferences is the verb 喜欢 (xǐhuān). Let’s break down this essential word using the Pinyin system we established in Chapter 1: xǐ (third tone): Sounds like "shee" but with your voice dropping in pitch and then rising. huān (first tone): Starts with the initial h, uses the compound final uān, and stays high and flat. Unlike English, where we use the auxiliary verb "do" to talk about hobbies (e.g., "I do like reading" or "Do you like tea?"), Mandarin keeps things beautifully direct. You simply state the subject, followed by 喜欢 (xǐhuān), followed by the object or activity. 我喜欢书。 (Wǒ xǐhuān shū.) — I like books. 他喜欢狗。 (Tā xǐhuān gǒu.) — He likes dogs. Liking Activities: The Magic of the Verb "To Go" When talking about hobbies, we are usually talking about actions—reading, swimming, watching movies—rather than just physical objects. In English, we put an "-ing" on the verb (e.g., "I like reading"). In Mandarin, you can place a verb directly after 喜欢 (xǐhuān) to mean you like doing that activity. However, for leisure activities, native speakers frequently use a specific structure: 喜欢 (xǐhuān) + 去 (qù) + [Activity Verb]. You already know 去 (qù) from Chapter 5 as the verb "to go." When combined with an activity, it implies going out to do that hobby. 看电影 (kàn diànyǐng): to watch a movie 喜欢看电影 (xǐhuān kàn diànyǐng): to like watching movies 喜欢去看电影 (xǐhuān qù kàn diànyǐng): to like going to watch movies (going to the movies) Both are correct, but adding 去 (qù) makes it sound more like an active, out-of-the-house hobby. Here are the basic activity verbs we will use in this chapter: 1. 听 (tīng): to listen 2. 看 (kàn): to look / to watch / to read 3. 做 (zuò): to do (often used for sports and exercise) Building Your Hobby Vocabulary To have a meaningful conversation about your weekend or free time, you need the words for those activities. Let's group them by the action verbs we just learned. Listening and Watching We consume a lot of media in our free time. 8. Food, Drinks, and Measure Words

The Restaurant Scenario Imagine you are sitting in a busy restaurant in Beijing. The aroma of sizzling garlic and toasted sesame fills the air. A server walks over to your table, hands you a menu, and asks what you would like to order. In previous chapters, you learned how to greet people, talk about your family, and express your likes and dislikes. You also learned how to count and use money. Now, it is time to combine those skills. Ordering food and drinks is one of the most highly anticipated milestones for anyone learning Mandarin. It is your chance to interact directly with Chinese culture through taste, politeness, and grammar. To successfully navigate this scenario, you need two things: the vocabulary for what you want to eat and drink, and the grammatical structure to tell the server exactly how much you want. This brings us to one of the most fascinating—and essential—aspects of the Chinese language: measure words. Naming Common Foods and Dishes Let’s start by building your culinary vocabulary. Chinese cuisine is incredibly diverse, but every restaurant will feature variations of the following staples. Staple Foods (主食 - zhǔ shí) In China, a meal is not usually complete without a staple carbohydrate. 米饭 (mǐ fàn): Cooked white rice 面条 (miàn tiáo): Noodles 包子 (bāo zi): Steamed stuffed buns 饺子 (jiǎo zi): Dumplings (usually boiled or pan-fried) 炒饭 (chǎo fàn): Fried rice Main Dishes (菜 - cài) The word 菜 (cài) generally refers to cooked dishes, vegetables, or cuisine as a whole. 鸡肉 (jī ròu): Chicken 牛肉 (niú ròu): Beef 猪肉 (zhū ròu): Pork 鱼 (yú): Fish 青菜 (qīng cài): Green vegetables (a generic term for leafy greens like bok choy) 炒菜 (chǎo cài): Stir-fried vegetables or a stir-fried dish Fruits (水果 - shuǐ guǒ) Fruits are often eaten as a dessert or a refreshing snack. 苹果 (píng guǒ): Apple 香蕉 (xiāng jiāo): Banana 西瓜 (xī guā): Watermelon 葡萄 (pú tao): Grape 橙子 (chéng zi): Orange Beverages (饮料 - yǐn liào) Whether you need a caffeine boost or a refreshing drink to cool down the spices, these are the essentials. 水 (shuǐ): Water 茶 (chá): Tea 咖啡 (kā fēi): Coffee 果汁 (guǒ zhī): Fruit juice 可乐 (kě lè): Cola 啤酒 (pí jiǔ): Beer The Rule of Measure Words In English, if you want to order two coffees, you simply say "two coffees." 9. Locations, Directions, and Transportation

Finding Your Way: Asking "Where Is...?" You have just stepped out of your hotel in Beijing. The air is crisp, the streets are buzzing with electric scooters, and you are ready to explore. You want to find the nearest subway station, but your phone’s data connection has suddenly dropped. You look up and see a friendly-looking coffee shop owner setting up his outdoor sign. You walk over and need to ask him one simple question: "Where is the subway?" In Mandarin, the word for "where" is 哪里 (nǎ lǐ). To ask "Where is...?", you simply place the thing you are looking for right in front of it. Let’s look at the most important question you will need to know: 地铁站在哪里？ Literal translation: Subway station at where? Meaning: Where is the subway station? In Chapter 3, we learned that Mandarin sentences often follow a Subject-Verb-Object order. However, when talking about locations, the verb is usually a preposition like "at" or "in." The structure is simply: [Thing] + [Preposition] + [Location]. When asking a question, you just replace the location with 哪里 (nǎ lǐ). Here are a few more examples of things you might need to find: 洗手间 (xǐ shǒu jiān) - Restroom 洗手间在哪里？ 银行 (yín háng) - Bank 银行在哪里？ 餐厅 (cān tīng) - Restaurant 餐厅在哪里？ Sometimes, you might want to ask where a specific object is, like your phone or your bag. You can use the exact same structure. If you want to ask "Where is my phone?", you just say: 我的手机在哪里？ Literal translation: My phone at where? The Magic Word: 在 To answer the question "Where is...?", you need to use the preposition 在 . In English, we use words like "in," "on," "at," and "to" depending on the context. Mandarin is much simpler. 在 covers all of these. It generally means "to be located at" or "in/on/at." If someone asks you where the restroom is, and it is on the second floor, you would say: 洗手间在二楼。 Literal translation: Restroom located at second floor. Meaning: The restroom is on the second floor. Notice that you do not need a separate word for "is" (like 是) when talking about where something is located. 在 acts as both the "is" and the "at." In Chapter 8, we learned about measure words like 个 used to count items. Location words in Mandarin often function similarly, but instead of counting, they tell us the spatial relationship between objects. 10. Forming Questions and Basic Tenses

Asking Yes/No Questions with 'ma' You have spent nine chapters building a vocabulary, mastering the Pinyin system, and learning how to navigate daily life in Mandarin—from ordering food to discussing your family and finding your way around a city. But conversing is a two-way street. To truly interact with native speakers, you need to do more than make statements; you need to ask questions. In English, when we want to turn a statement into a yes/no question, we often rearrange the sentence structure or add a "helping" verb. "You like tea" becomes "Do you like tea?" Mandarin takes a much simpler, more streamlined approach. To ask a yes/no question, you keep your entire statement exactly as it is and simply attach the particle 吗 (ma) to the very end of the sentence. 吗 (ma) is a question particle. It carries no meaning on its own; its only job is to signal to the listener, "Please answer this statement with a yes or a no." The Formula Take any standard declarative sentence and add 吗 to the end. Statement: 你喝咖啡。 Literal translation: You drink coffee. Question: 你喝咖啡吗？ Translation: Do you drink coffee? Notice that the Chinese sentence structure did not change at all. No extra verbs were added, and no words were swapped. Answering 'ma' Questions Because Mandarin does not have dedicated words for "yes" and "no" that work in every situation, you answer a 吗 question by repeating the main verb of the question. To say "no," you simply put the negation word 不 before that verb. Question: 你喝咖啡吗？ Answer (Yes): 喝。 Answer (No): 不喝。 If the question uses the verb 是 —which you learned in Greetings and Basic Sentence Structure as the verb "to be"—your answers will naturally use that same verb. Question: 这是你的书吗？ Translation: Is this your book? Answer (Yes): 是。 Answer (No): 不是。 A Quick Note on Tones In The Pinyin System and Tones, you learned that Mandarin uses four distinct tones to change the meaning of words. You might be wondering what tone ma uses. When 吗 is used as a question particle at the end of a sentence, it is technically written in the neutral tone . This means it is pronounced lightly and softly, without a specific pitch contour. It naturally takes a lower, relaxed pitch compared to the word right before it. Gathering Specific Information with Question Words Yes/no questions are highly useful, but they only get you so far. Often, you need specific details. To ask for information like "what," "where," "who," "when," and "how," Mandarin uses specific question words.
