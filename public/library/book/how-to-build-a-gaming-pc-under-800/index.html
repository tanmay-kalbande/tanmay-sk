1. Understanding PC Components and Budget Allocation

Imagine unboxing a brand-new, pre-built gaming computer from a major retailer. You peel back the protective foam, plug it in, and fire up the latest blockbuster game—only to watch the screen stutter and freeze. You check the price tag: $1,200. Now, imagine a different scenario. You sit at a desk covered in individual boxes. You spend two hours carefully snapping parts together, plug in the power cord, and boot up that exact same game. It runs flawlessly on high settings. You check your receipts: $780. The difference between these two scenarios is the knowledge of what is actually inside the box. Computer manufacturers often charge a massive premium for assembly, disguising cheap internal parts with flashy exterior lights. By building it yourself, you take control of the budget, ensuring every dollar goes toward performance. To build a machine capable of smooth, high-quality gaming for under $800, you first need to understand what the primary parts do, how they interact, and where you should spend your money to get the most bang for your buck. The Core Components and Their Functions A computer is essentially a highly organized team of electronic parts, each with a specific job. Before figuring out how to divide your $800, you need to know who is on the roster. The CPU (Central Processing Unit) The CPU, often called the "processor," is the brain of the computer. It is responsible for executing instructions, doing math, and managing the flow of information through the system. In a game, the CPU calculates the physical rules of the world—like the trajectory of a thrown grenade, the artificial intelligence of enemy characters, and the position of your character. The GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) The GPU, or "graphics card," is a specialized processor designed solely to render images, video, and 2D/3D graphics. If the CPU is the brain calculating the rules of the game, the GPU is the artist painting the visual world on your screen at lightning speed. It figures out the lighting, the textures on the walls, and the reflections in the water. For gaming, the GPU is the most important component in your PC. RAM (Random Access Memory) RAM is the computer’s short-term memory. It temporarily stores data that the CPU needs to access right this second. When you launch a game, the game's active data moves from your permanent storage into the RAM. This is because RAM is incredibly fast—much faster than a hard drive—allowing the CPU to grab information without any delay. However, RAM "forgets" everything the moment you turn off the computer. 2. Selecting the CPU and Motherboard

The Foundation of Your Build Imagine your computer is a busy restaurant. The CPU (Central Processing Unit) is the head chef, preparing orders as fast as possible. The motherboard is the kitchen floor plan—the physical foundation that connects the chef to the pantry (storage), the waitstaff (RAM), and the serving window (GPU). If you pair a world-class chef with a kitchen where the doorways are too narrow to wheel in ingredients, the chef’s speed is completely wasted. In PC building, this happens when you buy a fast processor but pair it with a cheap motherboard that lacks the features to let that processor stretch its legs. In Chapter 1, we allocated a strict budget for our $800 gaming PC. We set aside $100 to $130 for the CPU and $80 to $100 for the motherboard. In this chapter, we will figure out exactly how to spend that money. You will learn how to read the "spec sheets" of these components to ensure your head chef and your kitchen floor plan are perfectly matched. The CPU: AMD vs. Intel in the Budget Tier When you walk into a computer hardware store (or browse online), you will notice the CPU aisle is largely dominated by two brands: AMD and Intel. Both companies make excellent processors, but they name and categorize them differently. To compare them, we first need to define a few terms that dictate how a CPU performs: Cores: Think of a core as an individual chef. A CPU with 6 cores has six chefs working simultaneously. For gaming, 6 cores is generally the sweet spot for budget builds today. Threads: A thread is a chef's ability to juggle two tasks at once (a technique called simultaneous multithreading). A 6-core CPU with 12 threads can handle 12 streams of data at once, making it much better at multitasking. Clock Speed (GHz): This is how fast the chef is chopping. A higher GHz means the CPU completes more cycles per second. Understanding the Naming Conventions Both manufacturers use numbering systems that look like alphabet soup, but they follow logical patterns. Intel’s Lineup: Intel’s consumer processors are part of the Core family. For budget gaming, you will be looking at the Core i3 or Core i5 tiers. An example of a current budget Intel CPU is the Intel Core i5-12400F. i5: The tier (mid-range). 12: The generation (12th generation). 400: The specific model number. F: A suffix. For Intel, "F" means the processor requires a dedicated graphics card. Since we are building a gaming PC with a dedicated GPU (which we will cover in Chapter 3), an "F" chip is exactly what we want. 3. Choosing the Right Graphics Card (GPU)

The Heart of Your Gaming Experience Imagine this: You’ve just finished assembling your brand-new $800 gaming PC. You double-check all the cables, press the power button, and load up the latest blockbuster game. The opening cinematic plays smoothly, building your excitement. But the moment the actual gameplay starts, the screen stutters, the movements turn choppy, and your character feels like they are running through molasses. What went wrong? You likely overspent on your processor and severely underspent on your graphics card. In Chapter 1, we established that a gaming PC is a system of working parts, and in Chapter 2, we chose the "brain" of our operation—the CPU. But while the CPU calculates the physics of a jumping character and tracks where enemies are located, it relies on another component to actually paint those visuals onto your screen. That component is the GPU (Graphics Processing Unit). For a gaming PC, the GPU is the absolute most important component for determining how good your games look and how smoothly they run. If you want high frame rates (smooth motion) and crisp visuals, you need a capable graphics card. Let’s look at how to choose the right one without blowing your $800 budget. Understanding the Jargon: What Makes a GPU Tick? Walking into a computer store or browsing online retailers will bombard you with a wall of graphics cards covered in numbers and acronyms. To make a smart choice, we need to break down the two most important specifications: VRAM and Core Clock Speeds. VRAM (Video Random Access Memory) In Chapter 1, we briefly introduced system RAM. System RAM acts as your CPU’s short-term memory, holding data that the processor needs right this second. VRAM works the exact same way, but it is built directly onto the graphics card and serves the GPU instead of the CPU. When you play a game, the game loads textures (the visual skins applied to 3D models, like brick walls or character clothing), shadows, and lighting data into the VRAM so the GPU can access it instantly. If your GPU has too little VRAM, it cannot hold all the visual data a game requires. When this happens, the GPU has to constantly swap data back and forth with your slower system storage (your SSD or HDD), causing massive lag spikes and stuttering. For a budget gaming PC today, 8GB of VRAM is the sweet spot. It is enough to handle modern games at 1080p resolution without forcing you to turn all your visual settings down to "low." 4. Memory, Storage, and Power Supply

You finally saved up enough for the core of your $800 gaming PC. You picked out a CPU that fits your budget, found a motherboard to host it, and managed to score a great dedicated graphics card. You are almost ready to play. But if you skimp on the remaining three components—memory, storage, and the power supply—your powerful CPU and GPU will either starve for data, leave you staring at loading screens, or worst-case scenario, literally go up in smoke. In Chapter 1, we allocated $40 to $50 for RAM, $50 to $60 for storage, and $50 to $60 for the power supply (PSU). These might seem like minor expenses compared to a $300 graphics card, but they form the critical bridge between raw processing power and a smooth, reliable gaming experience. Let’s look at how to spend that budget wisely. Memory (RAM): Your PC’s Short-Term Workspace In earlier chapters, we briefly touched on RAM as your system’s short-term memory. Unlike your hard drive, which stores data permanently, RAM is a temporary workspace. When you launch a game, the CPU and GPU need instant access to game assets—like textures, character models, and level geometry. RAM holds this data so the processor doesn't have to wait for the slower storage drive to fetch it frame by frame. If your storage drive is a filing cabinet, RAM is your workbench. The larger the workbench, the more assets you can have open and ready at a single time. Capacity: How Much RAM Do You Need? For a modern gaming PC, RAM capacity is your first major decision. - 8GB: The bare minimum. You can play many games with 8GB, but modern titles are increasingly demanding. You will likely experience stuttering in heavy open-world games, and multitasking (like running a web browser or Discord while gaming) will be difficult. - 16GB: The sweet spot. For an $800 build, 16GB provides enough breathing room for the operating system, background applications, and any modern game without breaking a sweat. - 32GB: Overkill for most gamers right now, though heavily modded games (like Skyrim or Minecraft) or running a Minecraft server in the background can benefit from it. Recommendation for an $800 build: Stick to 16GB. It perfectly fits our $40 to $50 budget allocation and ensures you won't run into memory bottlenecks. Higher numbers mean the RAM can transfer data to the CPU faster. Currently, there are two main generations of RAM relevant to PC builders: DDR4 and DDR5. 5. Choosing a Case and Cooling System

The Invisible Component: Why Airflow Dictates Performance Imagine saving up for weeks to buy a high-performance sports car, only to tape the front grille shut and block the exhaust pipe. The engine might start, but within minutes, it would overheat, lose power, and grind to a halt. A gaming PC works on the exact same principle. Every component you learned about in previous chapters—the CPU processing calculations, the GPU rendering graphics, the RAM shuttling data—generates heat as a byproduct of electrical resistance. If that heat has nowhere to go, the temperature inside your case will skyrocket. Modern computer parts are smart; they have built-in defense mechanisms. When a CPU or GPU gets too hot, it automatically slows itself down to prevent physical damage. This is called thermal throttling. If your system overheats, you will experience frame drops, stuttering, and severely reduced performance, effectively wasting the money you spent on good parts. In Chapter 1, we allocated a modest $50 to $60 for your case. In the grand scheme of an $800 build, this might seem like an afterthought, but choosing the wrong enclosure can choke your hardware. This module will teach you how to select a case that physically fits your components and provides the lifeblood of a gaming PC: consistent, cool airflow. Understanding Motherboard Form Factors Before you can understand airflow, you need to understand the physical space inside the case. The size of your motherboard dictates the overall footprint of your build. These standardized sizes are called form factors. When you were selecting your CPU and motherboard in Chapter 2, you likely encountered terms like ATX, Micro-ATX, and Mini-ITX. Here is what they mean in practical terms: ATX (Advanced Technology eXtended): The largest of the common consumer boards. It offers the most expansion slots for add-in cards and typically measures 12 x 9.6 inches. Micro-ATX (mATX): A slightly shorter board, usually 9.6 x 9.6 inches. It fits into smaller cases and usually provides four RAM slots and two PCIe slots, which is more than enough for a budget gaming build. Mini-ITX: The smallest standard form factor, measuring 6.7 x 6.7 inches. These are designed for ultra-compact builds and usually have only two RAM slots and a single PCIe slot. Every PC case is designed to accept a specific range of form factors. A large ATX case can usually hold an ATX, Micro-ATX, or Mini-ITX board. However, a small Mini-ITX case can only hold a Mini-ITX board. For a budget gaming build, a Micro-ATX motherboard is incredibly common, meaning you can shop for either Micro-ATX or ATX cases. You have spent the last five modules carefully balancing a budget. You know exactly which $100–$130 CPU to pair with a $250–$300 GPU. You have mapped out your $80–$100 motherboard and decided on a case that won’t suffocate your components. But after spending roughly $600 to $700 on the internal hardware, you are suddenly faced with a realization: a pile of computer parts cannot play a game by themselves. You still need a screen to look at, devices to control the action, and a brain to make the software run. If you aren't careful, a shiny new monitor and a fancy keyboard can easily blow your $800 budget before you even buy an operating system. This module bridges the gap between buying parts and actually playing games. We will explore how to select budget-friendly peripherals that match the performance of the machine you just designed, understand the software that will run it all, and prepare the tools needed to bring your system to life. Selecting Budget-Friendly Peripherals Peripherals are the external devices you connect to your computer to interact with it. For a gamer, the three essential peripherals are the monitor, the keyboard, and the mouse. When building an $800 PC, the golden rule of peripherals is balance. It makes no sense to pair a budget-friendly graphics card (GPU) with a $500 monitor that the GPU isn't powerful enough to run properly. Conversely, a top-tier monitor won't look good if you buy a $5 mouse that constantly misclicks. The Monitor: Your Window to the Game The monitor is arguably the most important peripheral, because it dictates the visual experience your GPU can provide. When shopping for a budget monitor, you will encounter three main specifications: resolution, refresh rate, and panel type. Resolution refers to the number of pixels on the screen. More pixels mean a sharper image. The most common resolutions for budget gaming are: 1080p (Full HD): 1920 x 1080 pixels. This is the sweet spot for an $800 PC. Your chosen GPU (in the $250–$300 range) is built to excel at 1080p. 1440p (QHD): 2560 x 1440 pixels. This offers stunning clarity, but requires significantly more graphical horsepower. An $800 PC will struggle to run modern games smoothly at this resolution. Refresh rate measures how many times per second the monitor updates its image, measured in Hertz (Hz). A standard office monitor is 60Hz, updating 60 times a second. A gaming monitor might be 144Hz or 165Hz. A higher refresh rate makes fast-moving games look incredibly smooth. 7. Pre-Build Preparation and Safety

The Million-Dollar Mistake of Rushing In Imagine spending weeks researching the perfect $800 gaming PC. You carefully balanced your budget: $120 for a CPU, $280 for a GPU, $90 for a motherboard, and carefully selected your RAM, storage, PSU, and case. Your boxes finally arrive. Swept up in the excitement, you grab a kitchen butter knife, sit on a wool carpet in your living room, and pry open the motherboard box. As you lift the board out, it slips, scraping against the metal edge of the case sitting on the floor. You try to power it on later, and nothing happens. That scenario plays out more often than beginners realize. The physical assembly of a computer—which we will cover in the next module—usually only takes a couple of hours. However, the preparation before you touch a single screw is what dictates whether your build succeeds on the first try or ends in fried components and frustrating troubleshooting. Taking the time to gather the right tools, establish a safe environment, and read the manuals is the least glamorous but most critical part of building a PC. Let’s set up your workshop. Gathering Your Tools You do not need a professional electronics toolkit to build a modern gaming PC, but you do need a few specific items. Using the wrong tools can strip screws, scratch components, or physically damage your hardware. Before you begin, collect the following: A 2 Phillips-head screwdriver: This is the most important tool you will need. Nearly every screw in a modern PC case and component is a standard Phillips-head. A 2 size fits perfectly. Avoid using a 1 (too small, will strip the screw head) or a 3 (too large). Magnetic tips are incredibly helpful for picking up dropped screws, but standard non-magnetic works fine. A clean, well-lit workspace: You need a solid table or desk. Do not build on a bed, a couch, or the floor. Soft surfaces generate static electricity, and building on the floor invites dust, pet hair, and lost screws. A small flashlight or headlamp: Even in a well-lit room, the interior of a PC case can be shadowy. You will often need to see tiny printed labels on the motherboard or find a tiny screw dropped into the chassis. A small bowl or magnetic tray: You will end up with a surprising number of spare screws. Keeping them in a designated container prevents them from rolling off the table or getting lost in the case. Thermal paste (optional): If you bought a CPU that comes with a stock cooler, it will have thermal paste pre-applied. 8. Step-by-Step Physical Assembly

You have your components spread out on a clean table. Your anti-static wrist strap is secured. Your screwdrivers are laid out and ready. The $800 budget you carefully balanced across your CPU, GPU, motherboard, RAM, storage, PSU, and case has materialized into a collection of boxes. Now comes the moment that intimidates every first-time builder: turning a pile of silicon, metal, and plastic into a functioning computer. The secret to a stress-free build is sequence. If you try to install everything directly into the case right away, you will fight a losing battle against tight spaces and tangled cables. Instead, we build from the "inside out," starting with the motherboard on your table. Preparing the Motherboard on the Table Place your motherboard on top of the cardboard box it came in. The box provides a soft, non-conductive surface that protects the delicate circuitry on the underside of the board. Ensure you are working in the static-safe environment you prepared in the previous module. Unboxing and Installing the CPU The CPU (Central Processing Unit) is the brain of your computer. It is a delicate square of silicon with hundreds of tiny pins that must align perfectly with the motherboard. 1. Locate the CPU socket: Find the square socket on the motherboard. Next to it, you will see a metal lever. Gently push the lever slightly outward to clear the retention hook, then lift it up. The metal bracket protecting the socket will lift open. 2. Orient the CPU: Look closely at the corners of the CPU and the socket. You will notice a small gold triangle on one corner of the CPU, and a matching triangle or missing pinhole on one corner of the socket. 3. Place the CPU: Hold the CPU by its edges—never touch the bottom where the pins are. Gently lay it into the socket, aligning the triangles. It should drop in effortlessly. Never force the CPU. If it doesn't fall into place, check your orientation. 4. Lock it down: Once seated, lower the metal bracket back over the CPU. Push the lever back down and hook it under the retention tab. You will feel some resistance; this is normal, as the pressure is required to ensure proper electrical contact. Installing the RAM RAM (Random Access Memory) provides short-term memory for your CPU. Your motherboard slots will likely be color-coded in pairs (e.g., two gray slots and two black slots). If you are installing two sticks of RAM (which is standard for a budget build to enable "dual-channel" performance), you must place them in the correct slots to get that speed boost. 9. First Boot and BIOS Configuration

The Moment of Truth: Connecting Power and Display You’ve spent hours carefully assembling your budget gaming PC. You installed the CPU onto the motherboard, slotted in your RAM, secured the GPU, and managed the cables from your PSU. Now, you're sitting in front of a completed chassis, a power cable, and a monitor. This is the moment of truth. Before you flip the power switch, let’s make sure your connections are dialed in. Powering the System Grab the heavy-duty power cable that came with your Power Supply (PSU). Plug the female end (the side with three prongs inside a shielded housing) into the back of your PSU. Then, plug the male end directly into a wall outlet. Pro tip for beginners: Avoid plugging your new PC into an old, cheap power strip. A direct wall outlet is the safest bet for your first boot. If you have a surge protector with a built-in joule rating, that is perfectly fine, but daisy-chaining extension cords can introduce unstable power delivery. Connecting the Display Next, you need to get an image from your PC to your monitor. This is a step where first-time builders frequently stumble. Locate your monitor’s video cable. For a modern gaming build, this will almost certainly be an HDMI or DisplayPort cable. You need to plug one end into the monitor, but the crucial part is where you plug the other end into your PC case. Look at the back of your case. You will see a cluster of small ports (USB, Ethernet, audio) grouped together. Above or below that cluster, you might see a metal bracket with video ports (HDMI, DisplayPort, VGA). Do not plug your monitor into these ports. These ports are connected directly to your motherboard, which relies on Integrated graphics (the graphics capability built into some CPUs). Instead, look further down the back of the case. You should see a separate, distinct set of video ports horizontally aligned. These are the ports on your Dedicated graphics card (GPU). To get the gaming performance you paid for, plug your monitor directly into the GPU. If you plug into the motherboard by mistake, the PC might boot, but it will run on the slower integrated graphics, completely bypassing the powerful GPU you just installed. Once the power and display cables are securely connected, flip the power switch on the back of the PSU to the "I" (On) position. Hitting the Power Button Take a deep breath and press the power button on the front of your case. 10. OS Installation, Drivers, and Stress Testing

You’ve spent hours carefully snapping together the motherboard, routing power supply cables, and seating your graphics card. In Chapter 9, you pressed the power button for the first time, navigated the BIOS, and verified that all your hardware was recognized. But right now, your custom-built machine is essentially a brilliant, empty shell. It has the physical brain (CPU), the muscle (GPU), and the memory (RAM), but no instructions on how to use them. It’s time to breathe life into your $800 build by installing the operating system, teaching your components how to communicate with each other, and proving that the system can run safely under pressure. Installing the Operating System In Chapter 6, you chose your operating system (OS)—likely Windows 11, as it is the most widely supported platform for PC gaming—and in Chapter 7, you created a bootable USB drive. The OS is the master software that manages your hardware and allows you to run games, browsers, and other applications. Now, you will use that USB drive to install the OS onto your newly built PC. Preparing for the Installation Before you begin the installation process, make sure you have a few things ready: 1. The Bootable USB Drive: The flash drive you prepared with the OS installation files. 2. Keyboard and Mouse: Connected to your PC via USB. 3. Monitor: Connected directly to your dedicated graphics card (GPU), not the motherboard. (If you connect to the motherboard, you might not get a display, as the system will try to route video through the GPU you installed in Chapter 8). 4. A Stable Power Source: Ensure your PC is plugged into a reliable wall outlet or surge protector. A power outage during an OS installation can corrupt your storage drive. Booting from the USB Drive When you turn on a computer, it looks for an OS on its storage drives. Since your SSD or HDD is currently blank, it won't find one. You need to tell the computer to look at the USB drive instead. This is called changing the boot order. 1. Turn on your PC and immediately begin tapping the designated key to enter the Boot Menu or BIOS (often F8, F11, F12, or Delete, depending on your motherboard). 2. If you enter the BIOS (which you visited in Chapter 9), look for a "Boot" or "Boot Priority" tab. 3. Select your USB drive from the list of devices and move it to the number one position. 4. Save and exit (usually by pressing F10). The PC will restart. Instead of booting to a blank screen, it will now read from your USB drive and launch the Windows Setup program.
