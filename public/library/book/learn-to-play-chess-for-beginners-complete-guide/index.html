1. The Chessboard and Setup

The Battlefield: Understanding the 8x8 Grid Imagine walking into a quiet room. Two players sit across from each other, staring intently at a wooden board. Between them lies an army of 32 carved figures, locked in a silent battle of pure intellect. There is no dice rolling to rely on luck, no hidden cards to bluff with. The only things that matter are the 64 squares on that board and the minds of the players. This is chess—a game of absolute clarity where every piece of information you need to win is sitting right in front of you. But before you can command an army, you must understand the ground they stand on. Every great general studies the terrain before the battle begins, and chess is no different. The battlefield is the chessboard, a perfect square divided into a grid. Squares, Ranks, and Files A standard chessboard consists of 64 individual squares arranged in an 8x8 grid. This means there are eight rows of squares running horizontally and eight columns of squares running vertically. To communicate effectively about chess—whether reading a book, following a tournament broadcast, or analyzing your own games—we use a specific vocabulary to navigate this grid: Ranks: The horizontal rows of the board. Ranks are numbered from 1 to 8. The rank closest to the player with the white pieces is Rank 1, and the rank closest to the player with the black pieces is Rank 8. Files: The vertical columns of the board. Files are labeled with lowercase letters from a to h, starting from the left side of the board from White's perspective. Diagonals: Straight lines of squares running at a 45-degree angle, connecting squares of the same color. While they don't have numbers or letters, understanding diagonals is crucial because certain pieces travel exclusively along them. By combining a file letter and a rank number, every single square on the board has a unique "address." For example, the very bottom-left square from White's perspective is a1. The top-right square is h8. This system is called algebraic notation, and it is the universal language of chess. You don't need to memorize every square today, but knowing that the letters go left-to-right and the numbers go bottom-to-top (from White's view) is the first step to speaking the language. The Golden Rule of Board Orientation Take a close look at any chessboard. You will notice that the squares alternate in color—one light, one dark, one light, one dark, all the way across. This alternating pattern is not just for aesthetic appeal; it is mathematically vital to how the pieces interact. 2. How the Pieces Move and Capture

The Language of the Battlefield Imagine stepping into a bustling foreign city where every vehicle follows a completely different set of traffic laws. A taxi might be allowed to travel straight down a highway but forbidden from making a left turn. A delivery truck might be permitted to jump over a congested roundabout, while a commuter bus must stop the moment a pedestrian steps into its path. To a beginner, the chessboard can feel exactly like this. The Six Pieces—King, Queen, Rook, Bishop, Knight, and Pawn—are your vehicles. They all share the same 64-square grid, but each obeys a unique set of traffic laws dictating how it travels and how it eliminates enemy forces. In the previous chapter, you learned how to set up the grid, orient the board ("Right is light"), and place the pieces on their correct starting positions. Now, it is time to learn how to drive them. Understanding Captures and "Under Attack" Before diving into the movement patterns of individual pieces, we must define the two most important concepts in chess: movement and capture. Most pieces in chess move and capture in the exact same pattern. If a piece has the legal right to move to a square, it generally has the legal right to capture an enemy piece sitting on that square. A capture occurs when you move your piece onto a square occupied by an enemy piece. When this happens, the enemy piece is permanently removed from the board for the rest of the game. The capturing piece takes its place on that square. When an enemy piece has the ability to capture one of your pieces on its very next turn, we say that your piece is under attack. If your Queen is sitting on a square that an enemy Bishop can legally move to, your Queen is under attack. You must either move the Queen out of the way, block the attack, or capture the attacking Bishop. How to Execute a Capture 1. Identify an enemy piece you want to remove. 2. Ensure your piece's movement rules allow it to travel to that enemy's square. 3. Pick up your piece, physically remove the enemy piece from the board, and place your piece down on the newly vacated square. The Pawns: The Infantry Pawns are the soul of chess. They are the only pieces that move and capture in entirely different ways. Because there are eight of them per side, and they begin the game forming a protective wall on Rank 2 (for White) and Rank 7 (for Black), they are the first pieces to engage in battle. 3. Special Moves and Rules

The Exceptions to the Rules In the previous chapter, you learned how every piece moves and captures. You learned that the King moves one square at a time, that Rooks glide along ranks and files, and that pawns march forward but capture diagonally. If you play a few games using only those rules, you will eventually run into three confusing situations: 1. Your King feels exposed in the center of the board, but it will take several turns to walk it to safety. 2. Your opponent moves a pawn two squares forward, landing right next to yours, completely dodging a capture you felt you deserved. 3. Your pawn reaches the far end of the board, but it has nowhere left to go. Chess would be a deeply flawed game if these situations had no solutions. To fix them, chess includes three special moves that are exceptions to the standard movement rules: Castling, En Passant, and Pawn Promotion. These moves are not obscure tricks; they are fundamental rules of the game. Let’s break down exactly how and when to use them. Castling: Safeguarding the King In the starting position, the King rests on the e-file, surrounded by its army. But as pieces move out to fight, the center of the board becomes a dangerous battleground. The most secure place for a King is usually tucked away in the corner, behind a wall of unmoved pawns. Castling is a special move that allows you to accomplish two things at once: move your King to safety and bring your Rook into the active center of the board. It is the only move in chess where you get to move two of your own pieces in a single turn. How to Castle When you castle, the King and one of your Rooks work together. The King moves two squares toward the Rook, and the Rook jumps over the King, landing on the square directly on the King's other side. There are two ways to castle, depending on which side of the board you want to hide your King: Kingside Castling: This happens on the right side of the board (the e1 to h1 side for White, e8 to h8 for Black). The King moves from e1 to g1, and the Rook jumps from h1 to f1. Because the King ends up closer to the corner, this is also known as a short castle. Queenside Castling: This happens on the left side of the board (the e1 to a1 side for White, e8 to a8 for Black). The King moves from e1 to c1, and the Rook jumps from a1 to d1. 4. Check, Checkmate, and Draws

The Ultimate Objective: Capturing the King Imagine playing a game of tag where no one ever actually gets tagged. You run, you dodge, you strategize—but the game just goes on forever. Chess needs a finish line. Every move you make, from the very first push of a pawn on Rank 2 or Rank 7 to the sweeping attacks of your Queen, is ultimately aimed at one specific target: the enemy King. You might assume that to win a game of chess, you must physically capture the opponent’s King, removing it from the 64 individual squares just like you would a captured pawn or Knight. But if you look closely at the rules of capture, you will notice something peculiar: there is no prescribed way for a piece to "capture" a King. This is because the King is never actually captured. Instead, the game ends the exact moment the King is trapped with no possible escape. To signal this, players use a specific vocabulary: check, checkmate, and draw. Understanding these three concepts is the key to understanding how a game of chess begins, develops, and ultimately concludes. Understanding Check In chess, the King is the heart of your army. If the King is under direct attack—meaning an enemy piece could capture it on the very next move if it were allowed to do so—the King is said to be in check. When a King is in check, it is in immediate, mortal danger. The rules of chess dictate that a player can never make a move that leaves their own King in check, nor can they ignore a check when it is delivered. If your opponent places your King in check, you must deal with it right then and there. You cannot say, "I will move my King next turn," and proceed to move a pawn instead. Because of this rule, a check is a forcing move. It compels the opponent to drop whatever their previous plan was and address the immediate threat to their King. How to Announce a Check While not strictly required by the official rules of tournament chess, it is a time-honored tradition in casual play to verbally announce a check by saying "check" when you attack the enemy King. This acts as a courtesy, ensuring your opponent realizes their King is in danger. However, even if no one says the word, the condition of being in check applies. If you leave your King in check, you have made an illegal move and must take it back. 5. Piece Values and Exchanges

The Hidden Economy of Chess Imagine walking into a marketplace where every item has a price, but the price tags are hidden. You wouldn't trade a luxury watch for a pocket calculator, yet beginner chess players make equivalent trades every single game. They sacrifice a powerful piece to capture a weak one, simply because both pieces look similar on the board. To play chess well, you need to understand that the pieces are not just wooden or digital figures; they are a currency. Every piece has a specific numerical value, and every capture is a transaction. If you trade $5 for $1, you are losing the game economically, even if you haven't been checkmated yet. By learning the standard point values of the pieces, you transform from someone just pushing wood into an investor evaluating the board for profitable trades. The Standard Point Values In chess, we assign numerical values to the pieces to help us evaluate trades. The King is the only piece without a numerical price tag because you cannot capture the King—the game ends in checkmate before that happens. Here are the standard point values you need to memorize: Pawn: 1 point Knight: 3 points Bishop: 3 points Rook: 5 points Queen: 9 points These numbers are not arbitrary. They are based on the mobility and power of each piece relative to the Pawn. A Pawn, being the slowest and most limited piece, is our baseline unit of 1. A Knight or a Bishop is generally considered three times as powerful as a single Pawn. Why the Queen is Worth 9 Points The Queen is the most powerful piece because it combines the moving abilities of the Rook and the Bishop. It can travel along ranks, files, and diagonals for any distance. Because it controls so many squares simultaneously, it is incredibly valuable—worth roughly three minor pieces (for example, a Knight and a Bishop equal 6 points, which is less than a Queen's 9 points). The Bishop and the Knight: A Tale of 3 Points You might wonder why the Bishop and the Knight are both worth 3 points when they move so differently. While they share the same numerical value, they have distinct strengths. We often refer to the Bishop and the Knight as minor pieces, while Rooks and Queens are major pieces. Bishops travel diagonally and can cover long distances across the board in a single move. However, they are permanently restricted to the color of the square they start on. 6. Basic Chess Tactics

The Power of a Single Move Imagine moving a single piece, and suddenly your opponent realizes they are about to lose their Queen. They stare at the board, searching for an escape, but there is none. In a fraction of a second, the entire game has tilted in your favor. This is the power of chess tactics. While strategy dictates your long-term plan—where you want to place your pieces and what areas of the board you want to control—tactics are the short-term sequences of moves used to gain an immediate advantage. Most often, this advantage means winning material from your opponent. As we learned in Chapter 5, every piece has a relative value. A Knight is worth 3 points, a Rook is worth 5, and a Queen is worth 9. Tactics are the tools you use to trick your opponent into giving up those points. By recognizing specific visual patterns, you can spot opportunities to capture pieces for free or win a higher-value piece in exchange for a lower-value one. In this chapter, we will explore four of the most fundamental and powerful tactical patterns in chess: the fork, the pin, the skewer, and the discovered attack. The Fork: Attacking Two Things at Once A fork is a tactic where a single piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at the exact same time. The opponent is placed in a difficult position: they can usually only move one piece per turn, meaning they can only save one of the attacked pieces. The other is often left to be captured on your next move. Almost any piece can execute a fork, but Knights and pawns are particularly famous for it. The Knight Fork The Knight is a unique piece. As you recall from Chapter 2, it moves in an "L" shape—two squares in one direction (horizontal or vertical), and then one square perpendicular. Because of this strange movement, the Knight is the only piece that can jump over other pieces. It also attacks in a way that feels unintuitive to the human eye, making Knight forks incredibly dangerous and easy to miss. Scenario: Imagine White’s Knight is sitting on square e5. 7. Common Checkmating Patterns

The Power of Pattern Recognition Imagine staring at a chessboard, trying to calculate every single possible move for yourself and your opponent. You would quickly exhaust your mental energy. Chess is far too complex to play purely by calculating from scratch on every turn. So, how do experienced players make the game look so effortless? The secret is pattern recognition. By studying common setups that frequently appear in games, players can instantly recognize familiar formations without having to calculate every variation. In earlier chapters, you learned how the pieces move, the rules of special moves, and the difference between a check and a checkmate. You also learned basic tactics like pins, forks, and skewers. Now, it is time to combine those skills. This chapter focuses on recognizable sequences and formations that lead directly to the ultimate goal: trapping the enemy king in a checkmate. By memorizing these specific visual patterns, you will not only learn how to deliver checkmate yourself, but you will also develop a defensive radar to spot threats before they crash down on your king. The Back-Rank Checkmate The most common—and often most painful—checkmate a beginner will experience is the back-rank mate. It relies on a simple geometric reality: the king is trapped by its own defenders. The Mechanics of the Back Rank At the start of a game, White’s king sits on rank 1, and Black’s king sits on rank 8. To keep the king safe, players usually build a protective wall of pawns directly in front of it. For example, White might place pawns on g2 and h2, locking the king on g1. While this "castle wall" protects the king from frontal attacks by knights and bishops, it creates a hidden weakness. Because the pawns are on the second rank, the first rank (the back rank) is left completely empty. If an enemy heavy piece—a Rook or a Queen—can slip onto that back rank, it will attack the king along a horizontal line. Because the king is blocked from moving forward by its own pawns, and cannot move sideways to escape the check, the king is instantly trapped. A Concrete Scenario Let’s look at a classic example. Imagine White has castled kingside. White’s king is on g1, protected by pawns on f2, g2, and h2. White has a strong position but has neglected to create an "escape hatch" for the king. 8. Opening Principles

The First Moves Set the Stage Imagine construction workers trying to build a house by starting with the roof. They hoist tiles and beams into the empty air, expecting them to stay put. Of course, without a foundation, the roof collapses. Chess works the same way. Beginners often rush into the game trying to deliver an early checkmate—swinging their Queen into the action on move two or three—only to find their attack collapsing because they have no foundation to support it. The opening phase of a chess game comprises the first 10 to 15 moves. It is the time when you transition your army from its starting positions into battle-ready formations. You are not trying to win the game in the opening; you are trying to win the battle to start the game. To build a strong foundation, grandmasters and beginners alike rely on three foundational principles: 1. Control the center of the board. 2. Develop your minor pieces quickly. 3. Ensure king safety and early castling. If you follow these three guidelines, you will almost always survive the opening and reach the middlegame with a fighting chance. Controlling the Center Take another look at The Grid. The chessboard is 64 individual squares divided into ranks 1 to 8 and files a to h. The four most important squares on the entire board sit right in the middle: e4, d4, e5, and d5. This 2x2 square in the dead center of the board is the high ground. Why the Center Matters Controlling the center is the most important principle in chess strategy. When your pieces occupy or command the central squares, they gain maximum mobility and influence. Consider the Knight. From a corner square like a1, a Knight can only move to two squares. From a central square like d4 or e5, a Knight can move to eight different squares. The same logic applies to your Bishops, Rooks, and Queen: pieces in the center radiate power across the board. Furthermore, if you control the center, you create a barrier that makes it difficult for your opponent to move their pieces from one side of the board to the other. A Knight in the center can quickly hop to either flank to attack or defend. A Knight trapped on the edge of the board is essentially out of the game. 9. Middlegame Fundamentals

Stepping Out of the Opening You have developed your pieces, controlled the center, and castled your king to safety. The opening phase is over. But what happens next? Many beginners breathe a sigh of relief after surviving the first ten moves, only to freeze. The opening principles gave you a clear set of instructions: move pawns, bring out knights and bishops, castle. But now that your army is developed, the map is blank. This is the middlegame. The middlegame is the heart of a chess game. It is the phase where plans are formed, weaknesses are targeted, and the board becomes a complex battlefield. Because there are no strict mathematical formulas to memorize here, middlegame play relies heavily on understanding strategic concepts. When you first enter the middlegame, the sheer number of possible moves can feel overwhelming. To cut through the noise, you need a mental checklist. Three of the most important questions to ask yourself on every turn are: 1. Are any of my opponent’s pieces unguarded? 2. Are any of my pieces unguarded? 3. How can I create an immediate threat? Let’s break down how to answer these questions. Spotting Unguarded Pieces and Creating Threats In earlier chapters, we discussed the material values of the pieces and how to capture them. But your opponent will rarely leave a piece in the open for you to take without a good reason. However, they will frequently leave pieces unguarded. An unguarded piece—often called a hanging piece—is a piece that is not protected by another friendly piece. If your opponent’s bishop is sitting on a square, and none of their pawns, knights, rooks, or queens can recapture if you attack it, that piece is hanging. Scanning the Board To find unguarded pieces, get into the habit of doing a quick visual scan of the board. Look at every single one of your opponent’s pieces. Ask yourself: "If I could magically place one of my pieces next to that knight, could I capture it for free?" Beginners often leave knights, bishops, and rooks on squares where they have no protection. This frequently happens when a piece is moved to an aggressive square to attack one of your pieces, but the attacker forgets to bring along support. Creating Immediate Threats Once you spot an unguarded piece, you don't always have to capture it immediately. Instead, you can threaten it. A threat is a move that creates a danger your opponent must respond to. Creating a threat is powerful because it seizes the initiative. 10. Elementary Endgames

Imagine you have played a long, hard game. You navigated the Opening Principles, survived the chaotic clashes of the Middlegame Fundamentals, and finally captured your opponent's last defending piece. You look at the board: you have your King and a Queen, while your opponent has only a bare, defenseless King. You are completely winning! But the game isn't over yet. The opponent’s King is running around the board, and you only have 50 moves to deliver Checkmate before the game is declared a draw. If you don't know the exact technique to trap that lone King, your perfectly played game could end in a frustrating tie. This final phase of the game, where most pieces have been traded off and only a few remain, is called the endgame. In the endgame, Kings transform from hiding behind their pawns into active, fighting pieces. Here is how to use your King and your remaining pieces to hunt down the enemy and deliver Checkmate. The Golden Rule of the Endgame: King Activation In the opening and middlegame, your King is a liability. It hides in the corner behind a wall of pawns to stay safe from enemy attacks. But in the endgame, when there are very few attackers left on the board, the King is no longer in constant danger. Instead of hiding, your King must become an active participant. In the endgame, your King is a fighting piece. Because the ultimate goal is to trap the enemy King against the edge of the board, your own King must help push them there. A lone Queen or a lone Rook cannot force the enemy King to the edge by themselves; they need your King to walk up the board and act as a wall, taking away the opponent's escape squares. Checkmate with a King and Queen The Queen is the most powerful piece on the board. Checkmating a lone King with a King and Queen is the most common endgame you will encounter, so mastering it is essential. The goal is to force the enemy King to the edge of the board (the 1st or 8th rank, or the a- or h-files). Once the King is trapped on the edge, you will bring your own King close to deliver the final blow. If you just keep checking, the enemy King will simply dance around the center of the board forever. Instead of checking, you must use your Queen to take away squares. 11. Playing Your First Games

The Universal Language of Chess Imagine sitting across from someone who speaks a language you do not understand. You cannot communicate with words, but suddenly, they slide a pawn forward, taps a clock, and writes something down on a scoresheet. You look at the paper and see "e4." You nod, slide your own pawn to e5, tap the clock, and write your move beneath theirs. Without uttering a single syllable, you are having a fluent conversation. This is the power of algebraic notation. It is the universal language of chess, allowing players across the globe to record, read, and replay games exactly as they happened. Every book you read, every lesson you study, and every game you analyze will rely on this system. Mapping the Board You already know the geography of the board from setting it up: there are 64 individual squares, with files running vertically (labeled a through h) and ranks running horizontally (labeled 1 through 8). To read and write notation, you simply combine the file letter and the rank number to name any square. The square in the bottom-left corner is a1. The square in the top-right corner is h8. If you move your White pawn two squares forward on the first turn, it goes from e2 to e4. That is written simply as e4. Writing Piece Moves When a pawn moves, you just write the destination square. But when a piece moves, you must identify which piece is moving. Every piece has a capital letter symbol: K for King Q for Queen R for Rook B for Bishop N for Knight (We use N because K is already taken by the King) To write a piece move, put the piece letter immediately followed by the destination square. If you move your Knight to the square f3, you write Nf3. If you move your Bishop to e7, you write Be7. Captures When a piece captures an enemy piece, you use an "x" to show that a capture occurred. If a pawn captures a piece, you write the file the pawn started on, an "x", and the destination square. If a pawn on the e-file captures a piece on d5, you write exd5. If a piece captures, you write the piece letter, an "x", and the destination square. If a Bishop captures a piece on g7, you write Bxg7.
