That's the topic-comment structure, the real power move of ISL. Still with me, or you zoning out already? Good. 'Cause this chapter separates the tourists from the actual communicators. Core Carnage (Rip Apart the Essentials) Here's the garbage you need to unlearn right now. In English, you live and die by subject-predicate. "The man eats the burger." Subject. Verb. Object. Done. The verb is the king. Without it, the sentence collapses. You've been brainwashed to think every language works this way. It doesn't. ISL operates on a completely different axis. It's not about who did what to whom in some linear verb chain. It's about what are we talking about followed immediately by what about it. That's it. That's the whole game. Topic-Comment: The Skeleton Key The topic is the thing you're putting on the table. The comment is what you're saying about that thing. Simple. Brutal. Effective. You want to say "The conference was exhausting"? You don't start with some verb conjugation nonsense. You establish CONFERENCE as your topic. You put it out there in space. Then you hit 'em with the comment: EXHAUSTING. Maybe you add a facial expression that screams "I barely survived." Done. Meaning delivered. No verb required. No subject-predicate gymnastics. Just raw, efficient communication. 🎯 Key Insight: Topic-comment isn't just "a way" to organize meaning in ISL. It's the primary organizational principle. You're not building sentences — you're staging information. Topic first. Comment second. Every. Single. Time. Instead, it bends space and time like a street magician with a grudge. You don't conjugate nothing. You MAP time onto the physical world around you and let your hands do the talking. The most common mapping? Past is behind you. Future is in front of you. Present is right at your body. You want to talk about yesterday? You don't just sign "YESTERDAY" and call it a day. You can point backward over your shoulder or gesture behind you. Tomorrow? Your hand moves forward, out in front of you. Last year? Way back. Next year? Way forward. The further out the action is in time, the further your hand travels from your body. But here's where it gets nasty — the timeline isn't always a straight forward-back line. In spoken languages, you've got pronouns and names scattered through sentences like breadcrumbs. In ISL, you've got your signing space doing heavy lifting. Remember Point With Purpose: Pronouns & Indexing? That's your foundation. But now we're playing the long game. Here's the move. You introduce a referent — say, your boy Marcus. You sign MARCUS, then you assign him a location in your signing space. Left side. Boom. Marcus now lives there. Every time you talk about Marcus, you index that spot. You don't need to re-sign his name every three seconds. The space IS his name now. 🎯 Key Insight: Referential cohesion in ISL means the signing space becomes a map of your narrative. People, places, and objects get spatial locations that persist throughout the discourse. Lose the location, lose your audience. But here's where it gets nasty. You introduce a second person. Sarah. She goes to the right side. Now you've got Marcus on the left, Sarah on the right. When you narrate their interaction, you orient your signs accordingly. A directional verb from left to right means Marcus did something to Sarah. Right to left? Sarah hit back. The disaster? Beginners forget their own spatial assignments mid-narrative. Marcus was on the left. Then suddenly he's center. Then he's on the right where Sarah was. Now nobody knows who's who. You just blew up your own story. Still with me, or you zoning out already? Pay attention. This is where bodies get buried. The fix is brutal in its simplicity. Commit. Once you place a referent in space, that spot is sacred. You don't move it unless you're deliberately relocating. Your body remembers. Your eyes remember. …
