1. Mastering Tool Geometry and Edge Preparation

The Unseen Battle: How Edge Geometry Determines Your Carving Success The chisel bites deep, the gouge slices cleanly, and the shavings curl like ribbons—until suddenly, the tool chatter vibrates up your arm, the edge digs in too aggressively, and the wood tears out in jagged chunks. You’ve adjusted your stance, checked your sharpening angle, and confirmed the bevel is consistent. So why does the same tool behave differently in oak versus pine, or when carving against the grain versus with it? The answer lies not in your muscle memory, but in the invisible geometry where steel meets wood. This chapter isn’t about how to sharpen—you already know that. It’s about why the angles behind the sharpening matter, how the subtlest tweaks can turn a tool from a reluctant plow into a whispering scalpel, and how to diagnose edge failure before the steel even touches the wood. We’re going to dissect bevels like surgeons, analyze force vectors like engineers, and troubleshoot failure modes like detectives. Because in advanced hand-carving, the difference between frustration and flow often comes down to a degree—or less. --- The Physics of the Cut: Bevel Angles and Cutting Resistance Every carving action is a negotiation between tool geometry and wood resistance. The bevel angle isn’t just a sharpening habit—it’s a lever that controls how the tool engages the grain, how much force you need to apply, and how much control you retain over the cut. Misjudge it, and you’re fighting the wood. Nail it, and the wood seems to carve itself. Primary Bevel vs. Secondary Bevel: The Dual Nature of Edge Geometry The primary bevel is the ground face of your tool, the one you maintain through sharpening. It sets the foundation for everything that follows. But the secondary bevel—the micro-bevel you add just behind the edge—is where precision lives. - Primary Bevel Angle Range: - 15°–25°: For fine detail work and softwoods. Minimal cutting resistance but fragile edge. - 25°–35°: The universal sweet spot for hardwoods and general carving. Balances durability and control. - 35°–45°: For aggressive stock removal in dense or interlocked grain. High resistance, but edge longevity increases. - Secondary Bevel Angle Range: - 5°–10°: Adds a razor-thin apex for slicing cuts in end grain or highly figured woods. - 10°–15°: The most common secondary bevel. Provides a stable edge for most carving tasks. - 15°–20°: For tools used in high-impact scenarios, like roughing out green wood or carving across strong grain. 2. Advanced Grain Reading and Directional Carving

The Hidden Language of Wood A master carver once told me that wood isn’t just a material—it’s a conversation. Every piece speaks in grain patterns, knots, and hidden weaknesses, and the best carvers learn to listen before they speak back. The problem? Most grain is deceptive. What looks like a straightforward board can turn into a minefield of chip-out and surface tearing the moment the chisel meets the wood. Worse, the wrong cut in the wrong place can turn a subtle flaw into a structural failure that ruins weeks of work. Consider the case of a walnut sideboard I was carving last year. The customer wanted a deep relief of oak leaves along the grain, but the wood had a subtle spiral grain—just enough to make the veins of the leaves tear when I carved against the dominant direction. I spent two days repairing the damage, blending the torn areas with fine abrasives and a steady hand. Had I recognized the spiral grain earlier and adjusted my cutting strategy, I could have saved myself the trouble. This chapter isn’t about identifying grain patterns you already know. It’s about decoding the ones that defy easy categorization, understanding their hidden quirks, and developing cutting strategies that work with the wood, not against it. If you’ve ever carved a detail only to watch it tear apart at the last pass, or struggled with a board that seemed fine until the chisel screamed in protest, this is where you’ll find the answers. --- Reading Beyond the Surface: Decoding Complex Grain Grain isn’t just a direction—it’s a dynamic system of forces, moisture gradients, and growth anomalies. The patterns you see on the surface are often just the visible tip of a much larger story. To carve effectively, you need to read between the lines. 3. Three-Dimensional Carving and Relief Depth Management

Planning Multi-Plane Reliefs: From Concept to Carved Reality Consider the last time you ran your fingers over a finely carved relief and felt the deliberate undulations beneath your fingertips—how the deeper planes receded smoothly into shallower ones, how the transitions didn’t betray a single chisel stroke. Now imagine trying to replicate that intuitively, without a plan, on a piece where the depth shifts seven times across a single 12-inch panel. That’s the difference between decoration and design in three-dimensional carving: precision isn’t optional, it’s foundational. This chapter assumes you’ve already mastered tool geometry and grain directionality, so we’re cutting straight to the art of orchestrating depth—where to begin, how to think in layers, and how to make every plane feel inevitable. Depth isn’t just a measurement; it’s a narrative. In a classically styled frieze, the foreground might sit at 3/16" below the surface, the midground at 5/32", and the background at 1/8"—each level serving a visual purpose. But wood doesn’t carve evenly. Different species respond uniquely to pressure, and even within one board, grain density shifts like weather. The secret isn’t in perfecting a single depth, but in controlling how depth transitions across those variations without losing cohesion. Today, we’re focusing not on chiseling out a single plane, but on engineering an entire topography where the eye follows light and shadow as if guided by an unseen hand. --- Designing for Depth: Mapping Your Relief Before You Carve Before the mallet touches wood, the relief must exist in your mind as a set of nested planes. That mental model starts with a depth map—a visual plan that assigns numerical depth values to every region of your design. At the advanced level, this map isn’t just about aesthetics; it’s about compensating for wood movement, tool geometry, and carver fatigue. The Four-Layer Depth Hierarchy Every multi-plane relief can be reduced to four functional layers, regardless of subject matter: 1. Background Plane (Level 0) – The flat or minimally carved surface that anchors the piece. Often the highest point in relief depth (i.e., least recessed). 2. Midground Plane (Level 1–2) – Where secondary elements reside. Depth here is typically 1/16" to 3/32" below background. 3. Foreground Plane (Level 3–4) – Primary subjects or focal points. Depth ranges from 1/8" to 5/32". 4. Detail Plane (Level 5+) – Ultra-shallow areas (1/32" or less) reserved for fine textures, edges, or inscriptions. Trade-off Alert: Deeper isn’t always better. A 1/4" deep recess in basswood may collapse under tool pressure, while the same depth in walnut may require excessive mallet force, risking tear-out. Always test on a scrap piece first. 4. Textural and Surface Detail Carving

Mimicking Life’s Minute Fractures A raven’s wing under the winter sun: the feathers catch the light in bands of gloss and shadow, each barbule catching the slightest breeze. Yet if you press a fingertip to the shaft, you feel only the faintest ridge—the rest is illusion. How do you translate that into wood without carving a thousand individual lines? The difference between a feather that reads as soft and one that reads as brittle hinges on two things: the angle of your tool and the depth of your apex. This chapter assumes you already know how to keep an edge sharp and how to read grain direction. What changes when the grain itself becomes the surface you’re carving—not a background to cut away from, but the texture you’re building into? The answer lies in specialized bevels, tool selection, and a counterintuitive approach to pressure: the lighter the touch, the more organic the result. --- Crafting Organic Textures: From Bark to Feathers Reversing the Logic of Relief In Three-Dimensional Carving and Relief Depth Management, we treated depth as a gradient that recedes from foreground to background. For textural work, depth becomes a tactile language—a rhythm of peaks and valleys that your fingers (and the viewer’s eye) will interpret as fur, scales, or weathered bark. Trade-off Alert: Every micro-valley you carve removes mass. If you over-carve a feather shaft to deepen the groove, you risk creating a stress riser that could propagate a crack under seasonal movement. The solution is not to skip detail but to thin the apex while keeping the primary bevel intact. - Narrow apex (0.001"–0.003") — ideal for feather barbs, fine fur textures. Requires a secondary bevel of 10°–15° to prevent the edge from rolling. - Wide apex (0.004"–0.008") — better for bark or scales where you need durability and less risk of tear-out on reversing grain. Scenario: Carving a great horned owl’s breast feathers. The primary bevel rides at 30° for strength, while the secondary is honed to 12° and only 0.002" wide. The difference in tactile feedback between the sharp edge and the slightly blunter apex creates the illusion of softness without sacrificing edge life. 5. Compound Angle and Compound Curve Carving

The Hidden Geometry of Spiral Staircases Picture the spiral staircase in a medieval tower. From above, it’s a perfect circle, but its steps twist upward like a helix. Now imagine carving that staircase from a single block of oak—no glue, no joints, just the curve of the grain and the angle of your gouge. The tool never moves in a straight line; it follows a path that’s simultaneously rotating and rising. This is compound angle carving: the moment where two or three axes of motion intersect in the stroke of a single tool. Most carvers never attempt it. They stop at flat panels, simple curves, or shallow reliefs. But the moment you accept that wood can be guided along a path where the bevel angle, the cutting direction, and the depth all change simultaneously, your work lifts off the bench and into the realm of sculpture. It’s not about brute force—it’s about choreography. The tool, the wood, and the hand must move in concert. Misalign any one of them by a degree, and the cut snaps off, the surface tears, or the shape collapses. This chapter isn’t about making it easier—it’s about making it possible. We’re going to dissect the mechanics of spirals, coves, and ogees not as decorative shapes, but as dynamic systems where forces, angles, and grain interact in real time. You’ll learn how to set up a carving station where a multi-axis path can be controlled by hand, how to verify a compound curve before it’s cut, and how to recover when the apex of a cove disappears under a shaving the size of a fingernail. We’ll work through the edge cases: what happens when the secondary bevel is too wide on a spiral, or when the primary bevel angle collides with the grain direction at 45 degrees. And we’ll talk about power tools—not as a shortcut, but as a way to stabilize motion so your hands can focus on the nuance of the curve. This isn’t theory. It’s the kind of carving that separates museum pieces from the kind you hang in the basement. So sharpen your gouges, and let’s begin. --- The Three Axes of Compound Motion Compound angle carving isn’t just “curvy.” It’s the intersection of three distinct axes of movement: 1. 6. Structural Integrity in Carved Artworks

The Paradox of the Thin Edge Imagine a sculpture of a draped figure, where the wood has been carved to a translucent 2mm thickness to simulate silk. For months, the piece is a triumph of Three-Dimensional Carving and Relief Depth Management. Then, during a seasonal shift in humidity, a hairline fracture appears—not in the thick base, but precisely where the thin "fabric" meets the dense "body." This is the fundamental challenge of structural integrity: the more we push wood to mimic non-wooden materials or defy gravity, the more we fight the cellular memory of the organic medium. At an advanced level, structural integrity is not about making a piece "strong" in a general sense, but about managing the internal stresses and hygroscopic movement of the wood so that the artwork does not destroy itself. Advanced Material Selection for Stability While basic wood selection focuses on grain pattern, structural selection focuses on dimensional stability coefficients. For large-scale or high-detail carvings, the goal is to minimize the differential movement between the radial and tangential planes. Evaluating the Stability Ratio When selecting stock for complex carvings, prioritize woods with a low ratio between tangential shrinkage (T) and radial shrinkage (R). A high T/R ratio indicates a wood prone to cupping and splitting as it dries or reacts to the environment. Low-Ratio Woods (Stable): Species like Mahogany or Teak exhibit more uniform shrinkage, making them ideal for thin-walled vessels or intricate relief work. High-Ratio Woods (Volatile): Species like White Oak or Beech have a higher differential, meaning they are more likely to develop "checks" (small cracks) when the exterior dries faster than the core. The Risk of "Internal Tension" in Large Billets Large blocks of wood contain latent stresses from the growth process and the initial milling. When you remove significant mass—as is common in 3D carving—you are effectively "releasing" these stresses. Scenario: You are carving a life-sized bust from a single block of walnut. As you remove the bulk of the cheeks and neck, the piece suddenly develops a longitudinal crack along the midline. This happened because the internal tension of the wood was held in check by the surrounding mass; once that mass was removed, the wood "sprung" to find a new equilibrium. To mitigate this, employ Stress-Relief Roughing: 1. Remove 60-70% of the waste material. 2. Pause the carving process for 2–4 weeks. 3. Allow the wood to "move" and settle into its new shape before executing the final, delicate details. 7. Advanced Tool Control and Ergonomic Efficiency

The Paradox of the "Death Grip" Imagine you are deep into a high-detail section of a project—perhaps a complex set of overlapping scales or a deep undercut. You feel the resistance of a stubborn knot. Your instinct is to squeeze the tool harder, lock your wrist, and lean your shoulder into the cut. For a few seconds, you get the result you want. But an hour later, you notice a dull ache in your forearm, a tremor in your fingers, and a sudden loss of the microscopic precision required for Textural and Surface Detail Carving. This is the "Death Grip" paradox: the more we consciously try to "control" the tool through force, the more we degrade the neurological feedback loop between the wood and the hand. At an advanced level, tool control is not about the application of force, but the management of resistance. True efficiency is found when the body becomes a series of linked pivots rather than a rigid lever. Kinetic Chain Integration: Beyond the Wrist Advanced carving requires moving the source of power from the small muscles of the hand to the large muscles of the core. When you rely solely on the wrist and fingers, you encounter rapid fatigue and a limited range of motion, which inevitably leads to the "stutter" in long, sweeping cuts. The Pivot Hierarchy To maintain precision over extended sessions, distribute the movement across these three zones: 1. The Core (The Anchor): Your power originates in your stance. For standing work, a staggered stance (one foot slightly ahead of the other) allows you to shift your center of gravity forward into the cut without leaning your neck. For seated work, the pelvis should be neutral, with the lower back supported to prevent the "slump" that restricts diaphragmatic breathing and oxygen flow to the muscles. 2. The Shoulder and Elbow (The Gross Adjustment): Use the shoulder for the primary trajectory of the cut and the elbow for the depth and angle. By locking the wrist and moving from the shoulder, you create a consistent arc that is far more repeatable than the erratic flick of a wrist. 3. The Hand and Fingers (The Micro-Adjustment): The hand should act as a guidance system, not a motor. Its role is to maintain the tool's orientation relative to the Secondary Bevel and to make minute corrections in the tool's path. Developing Muscle Memory for Repetitive Motions Complex, repetitive motions—such as creating a series of uniform flutes or repetitive hatching—should be treated as a "dance" rather than a series of individual tasks. 8. Dynamic Carving: Working with Green Wood and Freshly Cut Stock

The Paradox of the Green State Imagine a piece of seasoned black walnut. It is stable, predictable, and resists the tool with a consistent, crystalline hardness. Now, imagine that same species freshly felled. The wood is "plastic"; it yields to the gouge with a buttery consistency, allowing for massive material removal and sweeping, fluid curves that would be exhausting—if not impossible—to achieve in kiln-dried stock. However, this ease comes with a volatile price. The moment you remove a chip of green wood, you are not just altering the shape; you are altering the rate of evaporation. Every deep gouge creates a new surface area for moisture to escape, turning your carving into a complex map of differential drying tensions. For the advanced carver, green wood is not simply "easier to cut"—it is a dynamic medium where the carving process and the drying process are in a constant, often conflicting, dialogue. The Mechanics of Moisture and Movement To carve green wood effectively, you must shift your perspective from static geometry to predictive deformation. While you have already mastered Advanced Grain Reading and Directional Carving, green wood introduces a temporal element: the grain will shift and shrink as the cellular structure collapses during drying. Differential Shrinkage and Checking Wood does not shrink uniformly. Tangential shrinkage (parallel to the growth rings) is typically significantly greater than radial shrinkage (perpendicular to the rings). In a complex 3D carving, this creates internal shear stress. The Surface-Core Conflict: The exterior of a green carving dries faster than the core. This creates a "skin" of shrunk wood that attempts to compress the still-swollen interior, leading to checking (small surface cracks) or splitting (deep structural failures). The Geometry of Stress: Sharp interior angles and deep, narrow recesses act as stress concentrators. Where you might have applied Three-Dimensional Carving and Relief Depth Management to achieve a specific aesthetic in dry wood, in green wood, those same deep recesses can become the origin points for catastrophic splits. The "Plastic" Window There is a critical window between "freshly cut" and "seasoned" where the wood is at its most malleable but has begun to lose its initial volatility. Carving in this window allows for the use of a Narrow Apex (0.001"–0.003") to slice through the saturated fibers without crushing the cell walls, resulting in a surface finish that requires far less abrasion later. 9. Digital-Assisted Carving: Bridging Traditional and Modern Workflows

The Paradox of Precision: Why Digitalize the Hand? Imagine you are commissioned to carve a series of four identical, anatomically correct human hands emerging from a block of burl walnut. Each hand must be mirrored perfectly, and the proportions must be exact to a millimeter. In a purely traditional workflow, you would spend dozens of hours on clay maquettes, calipers, and "eyeballing" the symmetry—only to potentially discover a structural flaw halfway through the final piece, risking the expensive stock. The goal of digital assistance is not to replace the chisel, but to eliminate the "guesswork phase" of complex geometry. By shifting the burden of proportion and symmetry to a digital environment, you reclaim your mental bandwidth for what truly matters: the nuance of Advanced Grain Reading and Directional Carving and the tactile execution of Textural and Surface Detail Carving. 3D Scanning for Template Generation For the advanced carver, a 3D scan is not a final product, but a sophisticated reference map. Whether using a professional-grade structured light scanner or a high-resolution photogrammetry array, the objective is to capture a "digital twin" of a physical object or a refined clay model. From Mesh to Physical Guide Once a 3D model is acquired, the most efficient way to bridge the gap to the workbench is through Physical Sectional Templates. Rather than attempting to carve "by eye" against a screen, use the software to create 2D slices of the 3D model at specific intervals (e.g., every 20mm). 1. Slicing: Use a Boolean operation or a slicing tool to create cross-sections of the model. 2. Exporting: Export these slices as DXF or SVG files. 3. Fabrication: Laser-cut or print these slices on thin MDF or acrylic. 4. Application: Glue these templates directly to the workpiece or use them as "gauges" to check depth and profile during the roughing phase. This method transforms a complex, organic shape into a series of manageable, two-dimensional checkpoints, ensuring that your Three-Dimensional Carving and Relief Depth Management remains mathematically accurate before you commit to final cuts. The "Ghost Model" Technique For projects requiring extreme repeatability, 3D print a low-resolution "ghost model" in PLA. 10. Finish Carving: Final Touches and Surface Refinement

The Paradox of the Perfect Surface Imagine a piece of high-relief carving where every anatomical detail is surgically precise, but the surface is sanded to a plastic-like uniformity. The result is often a loss of "soul"—the light no longer dances across the facets, and the organic vitality of the wood is smothered. Conversely, a piece left with raw tool marks may feel unfinished or amateurish if those marks lack intentionality. The advanced carver does not seek to "erase" the process of carving, but to curate it. Surface refinement is the act of deciding which tool marks serve the form and which distract from it. It is the transition from shaping (the focus of previous modules) to refining, where the goal shifts from subtractive volume management to the manipulation of light and tactile response. Strategic Scraping: Precision Leveling without Abrasion While sanding is the default for many, the advanced carver relies on the cabinet scraper or the card scraper for final refinement. Unlike sanding, which pushes dust into the pores and rounds over crisp edges, scraping shears the wood fibers cleanly. The Mechanics of the Burr To achieve a surface that requires no further sanding, you must master the hook (the microscopic curl of steel turned over the edge of the scraper). The Burnished Edge: Using a burnishing tool to turn the edge of the scraper creates a hook that acts as a thousand tiny plane blades. The Depth of Cut: A steep hook removes material quickly but can leave "chatter" marks. A shallow, refined hook is essential for the final pass on compound curves, as discussed in Compound Angle and Compound Curve Carving. 11. Carving Large-Scale Projects: Logistics and Execution

The Gravity of Scale: The Shift from Art to Engineering Imagine you have secured a 14-foot black walnut log, destined to become a monumental figurative piece. In a small-scale project, a mistake in grain reading is a nuisance; here, a failure to account for the internal stresses of a massive trunk can result in a catastrophic check that splits the piece in half six months into the process. When you move from tabletop carving to large-scale execution, the primary challenge shifts. You are no longer just managing Advanced Grain Reading and Directional Carving; you are managing mass, momentum, and the physics of stability. At this scale, the "workpiece" becomes an environment. You do not move around the carving; you navigate it. The logistics of how the piece is supported, how you access the "far side" of a three-ton slab, and how the piece will eventually be transported are not secondary concerns—they are the primary constraints that dictate the design. Managing Irregular Large-Scale Stock Working with logs or massive slabs requires a departure from the controlled environment of a workbench. The unpredictability of large-scale stock introduces variables that can compromise the Structural Integrity in Carved Artworks if not mitigated early. Stock Selection and Log Geometry When selecting oversized stock, the "visual" center of the log is rarely the structural center. You must identify the heart-center and the sapwood transition to predict how the piece will move during the drying process. Taper Analysis: Large logs are rarely perfect cylinders. Map the taper to ensure your primary forms fit within the narrowest diameter of the intended section. Internal Void Detection: Use a sounding hammer or a professional probe to check for heart rot or shake. A hidden void in a large-scale project can lead to structural collapse once significant material is removed. Stress Mapping: Identify existing checks (cracks). Rather than fighting them, integrate them into the design or use them as markers for where the wood is most likely to move. 12. Damage Control and Repair in Carved Pieces

The Psychology of the Mistake: Assessment and Triage Imagine you are finishing a high-relief carving of a complex botanical subject. As you execute a final, deep undercut to create a shadow gap, a sudden shift in grain—an overlooked anomaly despite your application of Advanced Grain Reading and Directional Carving—causes a three-inch section of the petal to shear off. The immediate instinct for many advanced carvers is "aggressive correction": carving away surrounding material to "blend" the mistake into a new shape. This is often a trap. In advanced carving, the goal of repair is not to redesign the piece, but to restore its intent with the least amount of invasive intervention. Structural vs. Cosmetic Failure Before a tool touches the wood for repair, you must categorize the damage. The repair strategy for a structural failure is fundamentally different from that of a cosmetic flaw. Structural Damage: These are failures that compromise the Structural Integrity in Carved Artworks. Examples include deep checks (cracks) that threaten to split the piece, sheared-off load-bearing elements, or instability caused by internal rot or knots. These require mechanical reinforcement. Cosmetic Damage: These are surface-level issues—small chips, "tear-out" from a dull edge, or surface degradation (compression) where the wood fibers were crushed rather than sliced. These require additive or blending techniques. The Triage Protocol When faced with damage, apply the following hierarchy of intervention: 1. Stabilization: Stop the damage from spreading (e.g., pinning a crack). 2. Subtraction: Removing the damaged area only to the point where clean, healthy wood is exposed. 3. Addition: Introducing new material (Dutchman patches, epoxy, or splines). 4. Integration: Blending the texture and patina to hide the transition. --- Mechanical Interventions for Structural Failure When a piece suffers a structural break or a significant crack, the priority is to stop the movement and restore the tension of the wood. Precision Splining and Pinning For a clean break or a deep check, a simple glue-up is rarely sufficient due to the internal stresses of the wood. Splines provide the necessary shear strength. The Blind Spline: For repairs where the joint must remain invisible, use a blind spline. 13. Designing for Carvability: Planning Projects Around Hand-Carving Limitations

The Paradox of the "Impossible" Detail Imagine you have designed a hyper-realistic sculpture of a clenched fist holding a delicate piece of lace. On paper, the composition is flawless. However, as you move from sketch to block, you realize the "lace" requires undercuts that are physically inaccessible to any gouge in your kit, and the knuckles of the fist create a grain confluence that guarantees tear-out regardless of your Advanced Grain Reading and Directional Carving skills. The most common failure in advanced carving is not a lack of technical skill, but a failure of spatial empathy—the ability to visualize not just the finished form, but the physical path the tool must take to arrive there. Designing for carvability is the process of negotiating between your artistic vision and the physical realities of tool geometry, wood anatomy, and ergonomic reach. Evaluating Design for Carvability An advanced design is not merely a drawing; it is a map of potential resistances. When evaluating a concept, you must look for "friction points" where the design fights the medium. The Conflict of Undercuts and Tool Reach The most significant limitation in hand-carving is the "blind spot." Every tool has a minimum radius of curvature and a maximum reach before the handle or the tool's shoulder interferes with the workpiece. The Shadow Zone: Identify areas where a tool cannot enter without removing supporting material. If a design requires a deep, narrow channel that then curves 90 degrees, you are creating a shadow zone. The Clearance Conflict: Consider the handle. A deep relief carving may be easy to start, but as the depth increases, the handle of your gouge may collide with the higher relief areas surrounding the cut. The Reach-to-Depth Ratio: The deeper the cut, the less leverage you have to maintain a precise Secondary Bevel Angle. Design your depths to allow for a natural swing of the arm and wrist, as discussed in Advanced Tool Control and Ergonomic Efficiency. Grain Integration and Stress Points While you are already proficient in Advanced Grain Reading and Directional Carving, designing for the grain is different from reacting to it. Grain Orientation Mapping: Before cutting, map the primary grain flow across the entire design. If a thin, protruding element (like a finger or a leaf) runs perpendicular to the grain, it becomes a structural liability. The "Short Grain" Trap: Avoid designing thin sections where the grain "runs out." These areas are prone to snapping during the final stages of Finish Carving or failing over time due to wood movement.
