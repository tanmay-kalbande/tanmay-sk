1. Motherboards, CPUs, and RAM

A help desk ticket lands in your queue: "User's PC won't boot. Fans spin, but the screen is completely black." You walk over to the workstation, open the side panel, and are immediately confronted by a dense grid of circuits, heatsinks, and cables. Where do you even begin? To the uninitiated, a motherboard looks like a chaotic map of a microchip city. To a CompTIA A+ certified technician, it is a highly organized ecosystem with distinct districts: processing, memory, power, and expansion. Understanding how these districts communicate—and what happens when they don't—is the foundation of hardware troubleshooting. This chapter dissects the core trio of internal PC components: the motherboard, the CPU, and RAM. We will move past basic definitions and focus on form factors, compatibility requirements, and the configuration techniques necessary to pass the CompTIA A+ exam and keep real-world machines running. Motherboard Form Factors and Chipsets The motherboard is the backbone of the computer. Every component, from the CPU to the storage drives, connects directly to it. When evaluating or troubleshooting a system, you must first understand its physical size (form factor) and its core logic (chipset). Form Factors A motherboard's form factor dictates its physical dimensions, mounting points, and power supply connections. Matching the form factor to the computer case (chassis) is a strict compatibility requirement. - ATX (Advanced Technology eXtended): The standard for desktop PCs. Measuring 12 × 9.6 inches, it offers ample room for expansion cards, multiple RAM slots, and robust power delivery. - Micro-ATX (mATX): Measuring 9.6 × 9.6 inches, this is a square, scaled-down version of ATX. It fits in standard ATX cases but offers fewer expansion slots, making it a popular choice for budget and mid-range office PCs. - Mini-ITX: Designed for small form factor (SFF) builds, Mini-ITX boards are just 6.7 × 6.7 inches. They typically feature only two RAM slots and a single expansion slot, requiring careful component selection to avoid heat issues. - ITX vs. Mini-ITX: The CompTIA A+ exam often distinguishes between these. ITX is a slightly older, smaller standard (often used in embedded systems), while Mini-ITX is the modern standard for consumer small-form-factor PCs. The Chipset The physical board is just the wiring; the chipset is the traffic controller. The chipset determines what CPUs a motherboard can support, the maximum amount and speed of RAM, and the number of USB and PCIe lanes available. Historically, chipsets were divided into two physical chips: 1. Northbridge: Handled high-speed communication between the CPU, RAM, and PCIe slots. 2. Southbridge: Handled slower peripheral connections like USB, SATA, and audio. Modern architectures have evolved. 2. Storage, Power, and Peripherals

Power Supplies: Sizing, Selection, and Configuration A client requests a high-end gaming PC build. You select an ATX motherboard, a top-tier CPU, and a high-end PCIe x16 graphics card. You assemble everything, plug it into the wall, press the power button, and nothing happens—or worse, it powers up for a split second before dying. Nine times out of ten, the culprit is an inadequate or improperly connected power supply unit (PSU). The PSU is the foundation of system stability. Selecting the right one requires calculating total system wattage and understanding the physical and electrical connections required by modern components. Calculating Power Supply Wattage To pass the CompTIA A+ exam, you must know how to calculate the wattage required for a system build. You cannot simply buy a 1000W PSU for every machine; you must right-size the component for efficiency and cost. Wattage (W) is calculated by multiplying Voltage (V) by Amperage (A) (W = V x A). While motherboards distribute power to RAM and low-profile PCIe cards, the primary power draws in a modern system come from the CPU, the graphics card, the storage drives, and the cooling fans. Scenario: Calculating System Wattage You are building a workstation with the following components: CPU: 95W High-end GPU (PCIe x16): 250W Motherboard: 50W RAM (2 sticks): 8W Storage (1 NVMe, 1 SSD): 10W Cooling Fans (3): 15W Total calculated draw: 428W If you buy a 450W power supply, the system will run at near 100% capacity under load. This generates excess heat, reduces the PSU's lifespan, and leaves no headroom for power spikes or future upgrades. Best practice dictates adding a 30% to 50% buffer to your total calculation. For a 428W draw, a 600W power supply provides the ideal buffer, allowing the PSU to operate in its most efficient power band (typically 50-80% load). PSU Cables and Connectors The motherboard and components require specific physical connectors. As an intermediate technician, you should already know the basic 24-pin and 4/8-pin CPU connectors, but the CompTIA A+ exam expects you to know the nuances of PCIe and storage power. SATA Power: 15-pin connector providing 3.3V, 5V, and 12V power. Used for HDDs, SSDs, and optical drives. Molex: 4-pin legacy connector (12V, 5V) used for older IDE drives and some chassis fans. Rarely used in modern builds but still tested. PCIe Power Connectors: High-end graphics cards draw more power than the PCIe x16 slot (which provides up to 75W) can supply. PCIe power connectors come in 6-pin (provides 75W) and 8-pin (provides 150W) configurations. A high-end GPU might require one 8-pin, two 8-pins, or a combination of both. 3. Mobile Devices and Wearables

Replacing and Upgrading Laptop Hardware Components Unlike desktop systems where standard form factors like ATX (Advanced Technology eXtended) and Mini-ITX dictate motherboard dimensions, laptops are masterpieces of spatial engineering. Every cubic millimeter matters. This means laptop components are highly proprietary, and upgrading or replacing them requires precision, patience, and an understanding of how these miniaturized parts fit together. While the CPU and RAM in modern desktops are easily swapped via sockets and DIMM slots, laptop architectures are vastly different. Hardware You Can Typically Upgrade: Memory (RAM): Many modern laptops solder their RAM directly to the motherboard to save space. However, some models still feature SODIMM (Small Outline Dual In-line Memory Module) slots. If your laptop has a removable back panel or a specific RAM access door, you can typically upgrade this. Always verify the supported RAM speed and maximum capacity via the manufacturer's documentation before purchasing. Storage: This is the most common upgrade. Older laptops use 2.5-inch SATA drives, but modern machines rely heavily on M.2 NVMe SSDs. When upgrading storage, you must verify the exact M.2 key supported (typically M-key for NVMe) and the maximum allowable length (codes like 2280, 2260, or 2242, where the first two numbers represent width in mm and the second two represent length). Wireless Cards: The Wi-Fi/Bluetooth combo card is often a replaceable M.2 2230 module. If you are upgrading to a newer Wi-Fi standard, ensure the laptop's internal antennas are compatible and properly routed. Hardware That Requires Full Board Replacement: CPUs: In almost all modern laptops, the CPU is soldered directly to the motherboard (BGA packaging). You cannot upgrade or replace it independently. If the CPU fails, the entire motherboard must be replaced. GPUs: Dedicated mobile graphics are similarly soldered to the board. Specialized Components: DC Jacks: The power input port is a frequent point of failure. On some laptops, the DC jack is a discrete, cable-connected component that is cheap and easy to replace. On others, it is soldered directly to the motherboard, requiring advanced micro-soldering to fix. Keyboards: Replacing a laptop keyboard varies wildly. Some are held by a few screws accessible from the bottom of the chassis. Others (like modern MacBooks) are riveted into the top case assembly, requiring the replacement of the entire upper chassis. When replacing any internal component, always disconnect the battery from the motherboard first. Even if the laptop is powered off, the battery maintains a live current to the motherboard. Dropping a screw onto a powered motherboard will instantly short circuit the chipset or Platform Controller Hub (PCH), destroying the system. 4. Networking Technologies and Cables

Cabling Standards and Physical Media A technician receives a ticket: a newly installed workstation in a corporate office cannot connect to the network, while the older machine sitting right next to it works perfectly. The new computer was just unboxed, plugged into the wall jack, and powered on. The operating system shows "Network cable unplugged." The technician checks the cable, swaps ports on the switch, and updates the network interface card (NIC) drivers via a USB flash drive, but the issue persists. The problem isn't the computer, the switch, or the OS. The issue is a physical mismatch: the wall jack is wired for a legacy T568A standard, but the newly crimped patch cable was wired using T568B, and poorly at that. Understanding the physical layer of networking is non-negotiable for the CompTIA A+ exam. A network is only as reliable as the copper, glass, and connectors tying it together. As an intermediate technician, you need to move beyond simply plugging things in and understand the exact standards, speeds, and limitations of various media types. Twisted-Pair Cabling Twisted-pair cabling is the backbone of most local area networks (LANs). It consists of eight individual copper wires twisted into four pairs to reduce electromagnetic interference (EMI) and crosstalk. There are two primary wiring standards for the RJ45 connectors at the ends of these cables: T568A: The older standard, still common in government and older enterprise installations. The pinout pairs are white/green, green, white/orange, blue, white/blue, orange, white/brown, brown. T568B: The most common standard in modern commercial and residential networks. The pinout swaps the orange and green pairs: white/orange, orange, white/green, blue, white/blue, green, white/brown, brown. A cable with the same standard on both ends is a straight-through cable, used to connect dissimilar devices (e.g., a PC to a switch). A cable with T568A on one end and T568B on the other is a crossover cable, historically used to connect similar devices (e.g., PC to PC, or switch to switch). Today, modern NICs feature Auto-MDIX, which automatically detects the cable type and adjusts the signal internally, making crossover cables largely obsolete for standard connections. Twisted-pair cables are categorized by performance standards: Cat 5e (Category 5e): Supports up to 1 Gbps at 100 MHz over 100 meters. Cat 6: Supports up to 10 Gbps at 250 MHz, though 10 Gbps is only reliable up to 55 meters. Cat 6a (Augmented): Supports 10 Gbps at 500 MHz over the full 100 meters. Cat 7: Supports 10 Gbps at 600 MHz, but uses proprietary connectors (like GG45 or TERA) rather than standard RJ45 to achieve this, making it less common in standard SOHO environments. 5. Virtualization and Cloud Computing

The Virtualization Edge: Running Multiple Systems on One Machine Your company develops a cross-platform application, but the IT budget doesn't allow for separate physical hardware to test Windows, Linux, and macOS environments. Or perhaps a critical legacy application only runs on an outdated operating system, but migrating the user to a modern workstation is mandatory for security. These are classic scenarios where virtualization saves the day. By abstracting the physical hardware—the motherboard, CPU, RAM, and storage devices we covered in earlier chapters—you can run multiple, isolated operating systems simultaneously on a single physical machine. For the CompTIA A+ exam, you need to understand not just what virtualization is, but how to deploy it, allocate its resources, and differentiate it from cloud computing. Client-Side Hypervisors and VM Deployment At the heart of virtualization is the hypervisor (also known as a Virtual Machine Monitor, or VMM). The hypervisor is the software layer that intercepts operating system calls to the hardware and manages the execution of the virtual machines (VMs). For client-side virtualization—running VMs locally on a user's workstation—you will typically encounter two types of hypervisors: - Type 1 (Bare-Metal): The hypervisor installs directly on the physical hardware. It does not require a host operating system. While more common in enterprise data centers, examples include VMware ESXi and Microsoft Hyper-V (when enabled as a server role). - Type 2 (Hosted): The hypervisor installs as a software application on top of a pre-existing host operating system (like Windows, macOS, or Linux). This is the standard for client-side deployment. Examples include Oracle VirtualBox, VMware Workstation, and Parallels Desktop (for macOS). Enabling Virtualization in Hardware Before you can install a Type 2 hypervisor, the host machine's physical CPU must support hardware-assisted virtualization. On AMD processors, this is called AMD-V. On Intel processors, it is called VT-x. If you try to launch a VM and receive an error stating that hardware virtualization is not enabled, you must reboot the host machine, enter the BIOS/UEFI firmware settings, and enable the VT-x/AMD-V feature. On modern systems, this is often enabled by default, but legacy or corporate-imaged machines may have it disabled. Deploying a Virtual Machine Once the hypervisor is installed, deploying a VM is a straightforward process, but it requires careful configuration to ensure system stability. When you create a new VM, the hypervisor will prompt you to define several parameters: 1. Operating System Selection: You must specify the guest OS (e.g., Windows 10, Ubuntu Linux). The hypervisor uses this to optimize its internal settings. 2. Virtual Hardware Allocation: You must assign a portion of the host's physical hardware to the VM. 3. 6. Core 1 Hardware and Network Troubleshooting

The CompTIA Six-Step Troubleshooting Process A user submits a help desk ticket stating, "My computer won't turn on." As a technician, this statement tells you almost nothing. Does the machine receive power? Is the motherboard dead? Is the CPU malfunctioning? Without a structured methodology, you might start swapping out the power supply, then the RAM, then the motherboard, wasting hours and potentially causing further damage. To prevent chaotic, guesswork-based repairs, CompTIA expects you to execute a strict six-step troubleshooting process on the exam and in the field. 1. Identify the problem: Gather information, duplicate the issue, and identify symptoms. Do not attempt a fix yet. Ask the user what changed recently, check for error logs, and isolate the exact scope of the failure. 2. Establish a theory of probable cause: Look at the symptoms and formulate a logical hypothesis. If the computer won't power on, a probable cause is a failed Power Supply Unit (PSU) or a disconnected power cable. 3. Test the theory to determine the cause: Verify your hypothesis. If you suspect the PSU, test it with a multimeter or swap in a known-good power cable. If the theory is incorrect, return to step 2 and form a new theory. 4. Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem: Once the cause is confirmed, determine the steps required to fix it. If the PSU is dead, the plan is to purchase a compatible replacement, install it safely, and verify system functionality. 5. Implement the solution: Execute your plan. Swap the hardware, apply the setting change, or replace the faulty cable. 6. Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures: Boot the system, confirm the user can log in, and check that the original issue is gone. If a failing UPS caused a power surge that killed the PSU, implement a preventive measure by replacing the UPS to protect the new hardware. Finally, document your findings, actions, and outcomes. On the CompTIA A+ exam, you will frequently encounter questions that ask, "What is the next step?" Knowing the exact order of these six steps is critical to answering them correctly. Because we have already covered the physical characteristics of these components in earlier chapters, we will focus strictly on isolating and resolving their failures. 7. Windows Operating Systems

Navigating the Windows Interface A user complains that their laptop battery dies halfway through their morning commute, and their display is too dim to read in a brightly lit office. In Windows 7, you would have headed straight for the Control Panel. In Windows 10 or 11, you might find yourself toggling between the Control Panel, the Settings app, and the Windows Mobility Center. Knowing exactly where to find these configurations—and how to use legacy tools when modern interfaces fall short—is a daily reality for IT support. Since Windows 8, Microsoft has been migrating configuration settings from the classic Control Panel to the modern Settings app. For the CompTIA A+ exam, you must be comfortable navigating both, as many troubleshooting scenarios still require legacy tools. The Settings App Accessed by pressing Win + I or clicking the Start menu gear icon, the Settings app provides a streamlined, categorized interface. Accounts: Manages user accounts, sign-in options (like Windows Hello), and sync settings. Update & Security: Houses Windows Update, Windows Security (antivirus), backup, and recovery options. The Control Panel Despite the migration, the Control Panel remains essential. Accessed by typing control in the Run dialog (Win + R) or the Start menu search, it defaults to Category view. Switching the "View by" dropdown to Large icons or Small icons exposes all available tools individually. Critical Control Panel applets you will encounter on the exam include: Network and Sharing Center: Used to change adapter settings, configure IP addressing (as covered in Networking Technologies and Cables), and manage advanced sharing settings. Device Manager: Though also accessible via the Win + X menu, this is where you manage hardware drivers—a direct continuation of the hardware troubleshooting covered in Core 1. 8. macOS, Linux, and Mobile OS

A marketing agency calls you in because their lead designer cannot boot their primary workstation—a 27-inch iMac. The screen displays a flashing folder with a question mark. Meanwhile, the development team is complaining that their Linux build server is rejecting file uploads due to a permissions error, and the CEO cannot figure out why a newly installed mobile app won't connect to the corporate network. As an IT support technician, you must be fluent in more than just Windows. You need to navigate the distinct file systems, configuration hubs, and command-line interfaces of macOS, Linux, and mobile operating systems to resolve these diverse issues efficiently. Navigating macOS: File Systems and System Settings While macOS shares some conceptual DNA with Linux under the hood, Apple has heavily modified the operating system to create a distinct ecosystem. Understanding how macOS structures its storage and settings is critical for troubleshooting and configuring Mac endpoints. The macOS File System Modern Macs use the Apple File System (APFS). If you encounter older Macs, they might use Mac OS Extended (HFS+), but APFS is the standard for drives formatted with macOS High Sierra (10.13) and later. APFS was designed specifically for flash and solid-state storage, offering strong encryption, space sharing, and fast directory sizing. Unlike Windows, which uses drive letters (C:, D:), macOS uses a unified file tree rooted at the root directory, represented by a forward slash (/). When you open Finder, you are presented with a user-friendly view of this file system, but the underlying structure is strictly hierarchical. Key directories to know for the CompTIA A+ exam include: /Applications: The default location for all installed user applications. (Equivalent to C:\Program Files in Windows). /System: Contains files required by macOS to run. Since macOS Catalina, this volume is read-only and cryptographically sealed to prevent system integrity compromise. /Library: Contains system-wide settings, preferences, and support files for applications. /Users: Contains the home directories for all users on the machine. (Equivalent to C:\Users). /Users/[username]/Library: A hidden folder containing the specific user's application preferences, caches, and keychains. IT technicians frequently access this hidden folder to delete corrupted preference files to resolve application crashes. /Volumes: The mount point for all attached storage devices, including external drives, USB flash drives, and mounted disk images. 9. Security Concepts and Threat Mitigation

Access Control and Authentication A user clicks a link in a convincing email, enters their credentials into a fake portal, and within minutes, an attacker is inside the corporate network. The firewall never blocked it because, to the network, it looked exactly like legitimate user traffic. This scenario plays out constantly because the perimeter of a network is no longer just the router; it is the user's identity. Access control is the gatekeeper that determines who is allowed in and what they are allowed to do. Authentication verifies who a user is, while authorization determines what they can access. Authentication Factors Authentication is typically broken down into three core factors: - Something you know: A password or PIN. - Something you have: A smartphone, a smart card, or a hardware token. - Something you are: Biometrics, such as a fingerprint or facial recognition. Relying on a single factor—especially "something you know"—is a massive liability. Passwords can be guessed, phished, or stolen in database breaches. Multifactor Authentication (MFA) Multifactor Authentication (MFA) requires the user to present at least two different types of authentication factors. The key word is different. Requiring a password and a security question is still single-factor authentication (two things you know). Requiring a password and a code from an authenticator app is true MFA (something you know + something you have). Common MFA implementations include: - TOTP (Time-based One-Time Password): Apps like Google Authenticator or Microsoft Authenticator generate a 6-digit code that changes every 30 seconds. - Push notifications: The authentication server sends a prompt to the user's registered mobile device asking them to approve or deny the login. - Hardware tokens: Physical devices (like a YubiKey) that must be plugged into a USB port or tapped against an NFC reader to prove possession. Authentication Technologies Beyond simple passwords and MFA, CompTIA A+ expects you to understand specific authentication protocols and hardware: - Biometrics: Windows Hello and Apple's Face ID use facial recognition or fingerprint scanning. Biometrics are highly convenient but can suffer from false positives (accepting the wrong person) or false negatives (rejecting the right person). - Kerberos: The default authentication protocol in Active Directory environments. It uses a system of "tickets" granted by a Key Distribution Center (KDC). Once a user authenticates, they receive a Ticket-Granting Ticket (TGT), which they use to request access to network resources without re-entering their password. - SSO (Single Sign-On): Allows a user to authenticate once and access multiple independent software systems. SSO is often implemented using SAML (Security Assertion Markup Language) in enterprise environments. - RADIUS: A centralized authentication, authorization, and accounting (AAA) protocol. 10. Software Troubleshooting

Resolving Windows Boot Failures and BSODs A user powers on their workstation, the BIOS POST completes successfully, but instead of loading the Windows login screen, the system halts with a dreaded Blue Screen of Death (BSOD). As an IT technician, your ability to rapidly diagnose whether this is a corrupt boot sector, a faulty driver, or failing hardware is critical. In Chapter 7: Windows Operating Systems, we explored the architecture of the Windows boot process. Now, we apply that knowledge to troubleshooting. When Windows fails to boot or presents a BSOD, your first step is to determine if the issue is hardware or software. As covered in Core 1 Hardware and Network Troubleshooting, you should always check physical connections and run hardware diagnostics first. Once hardware is ruled out, focus on the OS and its boot components. Tackling the Blue Screen of Death (BSOD) A BSOD is a protective measure. When the Windows kernel detects a condition that compromises system integrity—such as an illegal memory access or an unhandled exception—it halts the system to prevent data loss. To troubleshoot a BSOD, you need the Stop Code (e.g., IRQLNOTLESSOREQUAL) and the failing module name (e.g., ntoskrnl.exe or a specific .sys driver file). Common BSOD scenarios and their resolutions include: - INACCESSIBLEBOOTDEVICE: Windows cannot access the system partition during startup. This often points to a missing storage controller driver or a failing storage drive. Boot into the Windows Recovery Environment (WinRE) and use the Command Prompt to run chkdsk C: /f /r to fix file system errors. - DRIVERIRQLNOTLESSOREQUAL: Typically caused by a buggy or incompatible kernel-mode driver. If you can boot into Safe Mode, use Event Viewer and Driver Verifier to identify the culprit, then roll back or update the driver via Device Manager. - PAGEFAULTINNONPAGEDAREA: Frequently indicates a memory issue. While this could be a software bug, it is a classic symptom of failing RAM. Use the Windows Memory Diagnostic tool to test the physical memory modules. 11. Operational Procedures and Professionalism

Safety and Environmental Procedures When you are replacing a swollen battery in a laptop or servicing an aging server rack, the technical steps are only half the battle. The other half is ensuring you do not harm yourself, the equipment, or the environment. IT hardware contains hazardous materials, carries high voltages, and relies on sensitive components that a stray static shock can destroy. Personal Safety and Electrical Hazards Before opening a computer case or networking rack, you must disconnect power. However, removing the power cord is not always enough. Modern motherboards, even standard ATX form factors, retain a small standby voltage whenever they are plugged into the wall. To ensure personal safety and protect the hardware: - Remove jewelry: Rings, watches, and metal necklaces are excellent conductors. If a loose necklace drops onto a live motherboard, it can short circuit the board and cause severe burns to you. - Disconnect power: Unplug the device. For laptops and mobile devices, remove the battery if possible. - Beware of capacitors: Power supplies (PSUs) and CRT monitors (though rare today) contain capacitors that can hold lethal charges long after being unplugged. Never open a power supply casing. If a PSU fails, replace the entire unit. - Lift properly: Servers, UPS batteries, and large laser printers are heavy. Lift with your legs, keep your back straight, and use a team lift for anything over 50 pounds. Electrostatic Discharge (ESD) ESD occurs when two objects with different electrical potentials come into contact. The discharge is often invisible to the human eye but carries enough voltage to fry a CPU or RAM module instantly. To prevent ESD damage: - Use an ESD wrist strap: Clip the strap to your bare skin and attach the alligator clip to an unpainted, grounded metal surface on the computer chassis. - Use an ESD mat: Place the computer and components on a rubberized ESD mat that is grounded to the same common ground as your wrist strap. - Control humidity: ESD is far more prevalent in dry environments. Maintaining a relative humidity between 40% and 60% reduces static buildup. - Store components properly: Spare motherboards, PCIe cards, and RAM must be stored in antistatic bags (silver or pink) when not in use. Environmental Disposal Electronic waste (e-waste) contains toxic heavy metals like lead, mercury, and cadmium. Throwing a failed motherboard or depleted UPS battery into a standard trash bin violates environmental regulations in most jurisdictions. Follow these disposal best practices: - Batteries: Lithium-ion (mobile devices), NiMH, and lead-acid batteries (UPS units) must be taken to designated recycling centers or returned to the vendor. - Toner and Ink: Printer consumables contain fine plastic and chemical dust.
