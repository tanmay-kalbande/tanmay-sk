2. Core Pitch Correction Concepts

A Real‑World Call‑And‑Response Imagine you’re mixing a pop vocal that just recorded a powerful chorus. The singer hits every lyric with emotion, but a few notes sit just a half‑step flat, and a couple of sustained vowels sound thin. You have two choices: fix the off‑pitch notes so the melody sits perfectly in the key, or re‑imagine the line by turning those same pitch quirks into a stylized, melodic hook. The decision hinges on understanding what “pitch” really is, how it interacts with a singer’s intonation and formants, and how Melodyne translates those acoustic details into editable data. Below we unpack the musical and technical foundations that let you make that choice confidently, and we walk through how Melodyne detects pitch and visualizes it as the blobs you already saw on the Edit Grid. --- 1. Pitch, Intonation, and Formants in Vocal Performance 1.1 What Is Pitch? Pitch is the perceptual correlate of a sound’s fundamental frequency (F₀). In the human voice, F₀ is produced by the vibration of the vocal folds. Musically, pitch is mapped onto a musical scale (e.g., C‑major) and quantified in cents—100 cents = one semitone. On the Edit Grid, the Vertical Axis (Pitch) shows each blob’s nominal pitch (e.g., C4 85 cents). 1.2 Intonation: The Human Factor Intonation describes how accurately a singer’s produced pitches align with the intended musical pitch. Perfect intonation = 0 cents deviation. Small deviations are normal and can be expressive (e.g., a slight rise on a phrase’s end). Large deviations ( 50 cents) often sound out‑of‑key. Intonation is influenced by: 1. Physiological factors – tension of the vocal folds, breath support. 2. Acoustic context – interaction with accompaniment, room resonance. 3. Artistic intent – stylistic bends, vibrato, or genre‑specific pitch slides. 1.3 Formants: The Voice’s Timbre Skeleton Formants are resonant peaks in the vocal tract’s frequency response that shape vowel quality. The first two formants (F₁, F₂) determine the perceived vowel (e.g., “ah” vs. “ee”). Formants are independent of pitch; you can raise the fundamental while the vowel sounds unchanged—think of a singer singing a high “ah” without sounding “ee”. In Melodyne, the Formant attribute of a blob lets you adjust timbre without affecting pitch, preserving natural vowel shape when correcting intonation. Quick Reference - Pitch = fundamental frequency (F₀) → musical note. - Intonation = pitch accuracy relative to target note. - Formants = resonant frequencies shaping vowel quality, separate from pitch. --- 2. If the audio is imported into Melodyne with the wrong sample rate, an inappropriate detection mode, or without proper gain‑staging, even the most skilled editor will waste time fighting artifacts rather than shaping the performance. This chapter walks you through the exact workflow that prepares a vocal track for flawless pitch editing, so you can spend more time on musical decisions and less on technical clean‑ups. --- 1. 4. Basic Pitch Editing in Melodic Mode

Getting Started: A Real‑World Example Imagine you’ve just finished tracking the lead vocal for a pop ballad. The performance is emotionally convincing, but a few phrases sit a half‑step flat, and the climactic high note drifts sharp by about 30 cents. The rest of the arrangement is already mixed, so you need a quick, transparent fix that keeps the singer’s natural timbre intact. In Melodic mode, the solution is as simple as selecting the offending pitch blobs, snapping them to the correct scale degree, and nudging the formant just enough to preserve the vocal’s character. The steps below walk you through that exact workflow, using the tools you’ve already seen in Introduction to Melodyne Interface and Workflow and Core Pitch Correction Concepts. --- Selecting and Moving Pitch Blobs 1. Identify the target notes - Switch to the Edit Grid (if it isn’t already visible) via the Tool Bar. - Zoom in on the phrase in question; the Horizontal Axis (Time) lets you see the exact location of each note, while the Vertical Axis (Pitch) displays the blobs. - Use the Amplitude view (toggle in the Inspector) to differentiate voiced sections from breathy or silent parts—this helps avoid moving stray, low‑amplitude blobs that could introduce artifacts. 2. Make a selection | Method | When to use it | |--------|----------------| | Click‑drag a rectangle around a group of blobs | Quick selection of a phrase or a series of notes | | Shift‑click individual blobs | Fine‑tuning when only a few notes need adjustment | | Lasso (Ctrl + drag) | Irregular shapes, e.g., when a vibrato tail extends beyond the main note | Tip: The Select tool (shortcut S) stays active until you click another tool, so you can freely move between selection and editing without re‑clicking the toolbar. 3. Move the blobs - Mouse drag: Click any selected blob and drag vertically toward the desired pitch. - Arrow keys: With a blob selected, press ↑ or ↓ to move in 1‑cent increments; hold Shift for 10‑cent steps. - Snap to nearest note: Enable Snap to Scale (Inspector → Snap → Scale). When active, any vertical movement automatically snaps to the nearest note in the chosen scale, eliminating the need to guess the exact pitch value. You reach for Melody DNA, not to yank the notes into a robotic grid, but to nudge each pitch at the micro‑tonal level while keeping the singer’s unique timbre intact. This is the sweet spot where advanced DNA editing meets independent formant control, and it’s the difference between a polished performance and a synthetic‑sounding impersonation. --- Activating DNA Mode 1. Open the Edit Grid – If you’ve followed Preparing and Importing Vocal Tracks, your track should already be displayed in the Edit Grid with the usual horizontal (time) and vertical (pitch) axes. 2. Switch to DNA – In the Tool Bar, click the DNA button (or press Shift + D). The interface will change from the “blob” view to a series of note‑level markers that can be expanded. 3. Expand the DNA View – Hover over a note and press Ctrl + ↑ (or click the small “+” beside the note name) to reveal the underlying pitch‑curve points. You’ll now see a dense set of draggable dots representing the raw pitch data extracted from the audio. Tip: If the DNA markers appear “frozen,” make sure “Show DNA” is enabled in the Inspector panel. This option was introduced in Core Pitch Correction Concepts and must be toggled on for detailed editing. --- Micro‑tonal Pitch Editing Why Micro‑tonal Matters Even after quantizing a vocal to a scale, the natural intonation of a singer often contains subtle bends that give a performance its character. 6. Polyphonic Mode for Complex Vocal Arrangements

When a Four‑Part Harmony Collides with a Mic Imagine you’re mixing a studio recording of a pop‑rock anthem. The lead vocalist has recorded a tight double‑track, while the backing choir was captured in a single take. In the chorus, the choir’s Soprano, Alto, Tenor, and Bass parts overlap so heavily that the waveform looks like a single, dense wall of sound. When you load the track into Melodyne, the Edit Grid shows a tangled forest of blobs—some clearly belonging to the lead, others to the choir, and a few that are a blend of two or three voices. Your goal is simple: pull each individual note out, fix any pitch drift, and keep the lush blend intact. This is exactly what Polyphonic mode was built for. In the sections that follow we’ll walk through a practical workflow that meets the three learning objectives: 1. Detect and separate overlapping notes within a polyphonic vocal segment. 2. Edit individual note blobs without affecting adjacent pitches. 3. Correct the tuning of stacked harmonies while preserving the overall blend. All of the tools you need—Edit Grid, Tool Bar, Inspector, Pitch‑Draw, Quantize, and the Polyphonic algorithm—have already been introduced in earlier chapters. We’ll now focus on how they work together in a real‑world, multi‑voice scenario. --- 1. Setting Up Polyphonic Mode 1.1 Choose the Right Algorithm When you first drag the vocal file onto a new track, Melodyne defaults to Monophonic detection. Switch to Polyphonic by clicking the Algorithm selector in the Inspector (see Chapter 1). Tip: If you’re unsure whether a segment is truly polyphonic, enable “Show Detection Confidence” in the Inspector. Low confidence values usually indicate overlapping notes that need manual refinement. 1.2 Define the Analysis Window Polyphonic detection works best when the analysis window matches the density of the material: 1. Zoom in on the Edit Grid until you can see individual note blobs clearly (use the Horizontal Axis zoom). 2. In the Inspector, set “Maximum Polyphony” to a number slightly higher than the expected number of simultaneous voices (e.g., 5 for a four‑part choir plus lead double‑track). 3. Click Analyze. Melodyne will now parse the audio, creating a separate blob for each detected pitch. Caution: Over‑estimating polyphony can cause the algorithm to split a single note into multiple blobs, creating “ghost” notes that must be cleaned up later. --- 2. Detecting Overlapping Notes 2.1 Visual Clues in the Edit Grid In Polyphonic mode, overlapping notes appear as stacked blobs that share the same time axis but occupy different vertical positions. 7. Using Scale and Chord Detection for Musical Accuracy

Setting the Harmonic Context in Melodyne Before any pitch‑correction can be musically sensible, Melodyne needs to know what it is correcting. The Inspector panel houses the Key, Scale, and Chord Progression controls that define the harmonic backdrop for every blob on the Edit Grid. 1. Open the Inspector – If it’s hidden, click the “i” icon on the Tool Bar. 2. Select the correct Key – Click the key signature dropdown and choose the tonic (e.g., C‑major). 3. Choose a Scale – Melodyne offers diatonic scales (major, minor, melodic minor, etc.) and a Custom option for exotic or modal material. 4. Enter the Chord Progression – Use the “Chord” field to type the sequence (e.g., “I–V–vi–IV”). Melodyne will display the chord symbols above the grid, and the Chord Detection algorithm will align its internal model to those chords. Tip: When you set the key and scale, Melodyne automatically updates the Quantize snap grid to match the chosen tonalities, but you can still override it per note if artistic intent demands it. --- Applying Scale Detection What Scale Detection Does When you enable Scale Detection, Melodyne scans the entire vocal region and highlights notes that fall outside the selected scale. Out‑of‑scale notes appear in a different colour (by default orange) while in‑scale notes stay green. This visual cue is a quick audit of the vocal’s harmonic conformity. Enabling and Configuring Scale Detection | Step | Action | |------|--------| | 1 | In the Inspector, locate the Scale Detection toggle – it sits just below the Scale dropdown. | | 2 | Click to turn it On. | | 3 | Adjust the Sensitivity slider (if available) to control how aggressively Melodyne flags borderline notes. Lower sensitivity = only clear violations; higher = more permissive, useful for jazz or micro‑tonal passages. | Using the Information - Green blobs → Already conform to the chosen scale. No action needed unless you want to enhance them. - Orange blobs → Potentially dissonant. Decide whether to snap, re‑draw, or leave them based on artistic intent. Instead of snapping it to E, you might keep it for expressive effect, simply lowering its Amplitude to reduce harshness. --- Leveraging Chord Detection for Context‑Sensitive Corrections How Chord Detection Works Melodyne’s Chord Detection algorithm analyses the harmonic content of the accompaniment (or a reference track) and maps each measure to a chord label. 8. Timing and Rhythm Corrections

A Tight Groove Without Losing the Soul Imagine you’ve just finished a vocal take that’s pitch‑perfect—every C4 lands at 85 cents, the formant is intact, and the DNA analysis shows a clean, natural tone. The only problem? The singer’s phrasing drifts just a few milliseconds off the drum groove, and a couple of “yeahs” sit a beat early. The track sounds great, but the timing mismatch creates a subtle tension that pulls listeners out of the moment. Melodyne’s rhythmic editing tools let you pull those moments back into the pocket without sacrificing the emotional nuance you worked so hard to capture. In this chapter we’ll walk through three core techniques: 1. Quantizing note start times and durations to a grid or groove template. 2. Manually nudging individual notes for a natural, human feel. 3. Applying the Time Stretch tool to reshape phrasing while keeping pitch intact. By the end, you’ll be able to tighten a vocal performance to the beat, preserve its expressive timing, and fix phrasing issues with surgical precision. --- 1. Quantizing to a Grid or Groove Template Quantization is the fastest way to bring a vocal line into alignment with the underlying rhythm. Melodyne gives you two main avenues: a simple grid‑based quantize and a groove‑template quantize that mirrors the timing of another track (e.g., a drum loop). 1.1 Preparing the Grid 1. Open the Transport and set the project tempo. The tempo should match the session you imported the vocal into (see Preparing and Importing Vocal Tracks). 2. In the Inspector, locate the Quantize section. 3. Choose the Note Grid dropdown: 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, etc., for straight‑grid quantization. Custom if you need triplets or odd‑meter subdivisions. Tip: Use the same grid you used for the drum track to keep everything locked to the same rhythmic framework. 1.2 Applying Straight Quantize 1. Select all notes (Ctrl + A / Cmd + A) or a specific phrase you want to tighten. 2. Click the Quantize button in the Tool Bar. 3. Adjust the Strength slider: 100 % forces every note to the exact grid line—great for electronic styles. 50‑70 % retains some human feel while still tightening the groove. The Quantize operation moves the start times and, if you enable Duration Quantize, also snaps the note lengths to the selected grid. With Melody Ne y’s edit‑grid workflow, you can generate those harmonies, craft convincing doubles, and sculpt unique vocal textures without ever re‑recording. 10. Integration, Export, and Best Practices

1. Bringing the Edited Vocal Back into the DAW When the pitch‑correction pass in Melodyne is complete, the most common question is how to get the polished track back into the DAW without losing any of the nuance you just sculpted. 1.1. Two Paths: “Transfer” vs “Render” | Method | When to use it | What it does | |--------|----------------|--------------| | Transfer to DAW (or “Play‑through”) | You need a live sync for further editing (e.g., automation, additional comping). | Sends the edited audio stream back to the DAW in real‑time, keeping the original session’s tempo and marker information. | | Render (Export) | You’re ready to lock the vocal and treat it like any other audio file. | Writes the edited material to a new audio file on disk, then automatically creates a new track in the DAW. | Both routes preserve the lossless quality of the original recording, but the Render option gives you a clean, independent file that can be archived or sent to collaborators. 1.2. Render Settings for a True‑Lossless Bounce 1. File Format – Choose WAV or AIFF (both are PCM, uncompressed). 2. Bit Depth – Match the project’s depth (24‑bit is a safe default; 32‑bit float is ideal if you plan further processing). 3. Sample Rate – Keep the session’s rate (44.1 kHz, 48 kHz, 96 kHz, etc.). Changing it here adds a conversion step that can introduce subtle artifacts. 4. Dithering – Turn it off when you’re staying in the same bit depth; enable it only if you’re reducing depth (e.g., 24‑bit → 16‑bit for CD delivery). Tip: In the “Export” dialog, enable “Include tempo and marker information” if you want the bounced file to retain the session’s musical grid. This makes later re‑import seamless. 1.3. Keeping the Timeline Intact If you used Transfer to DAW, the audio appears on a new track exactly where the original clip started, preserving all automation lanes and clip markers. When you Render, Melodyne automatically creates a new track and places the file at the same start point, but double‑check: - Zoom in to the first few milliseconds – the waveform should line up with the original transients. - Play the session while the original (un‑edited) vocal is muted; any drift indicates a sample‑rate mismatch that can be fixed by re‑rendering with the correct settings. --- 2.
